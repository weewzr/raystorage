Will small modular reactors be too costly (per kWh) to be used? Q&A

There are only a couple of real FOAK small modular reactors and no NOAK small modular reactors at this time to give a good real world
comparison of the cost (say LCOE) of small modular reactors vs large conventional reactors. Following are points for consideration:

 All first-of-a-kind (FOAK) reactors are very costly due to the development cost, new supply chain, regulatory cost, etc. Cost is
    expected to fall significantly for the Nth-of-a-kind (NOAK) reactor as seen in various past examples in most countries.

 Further cost savings could come from manufacturing (in a factory) vs construction (at the site) due to the modular nature of small
    modular reactors.

 Additional cost to use conventional large reactors, e.g., grid improvement, will have to be taken into account in comparison, or for
    isolated communities that are not connected to grid.

 Small modular reactors built for special purpose, e.g., high temperature heat for petrochemical industry or thermochemical
    hydrogen production, may also be more cost effective.

 Cost of the nuclear island is usually a small part (typically < 20% in US) of the total cost of a nuclear power construction which
    includes the balance of plant (turbine, electrical yards, EPC costs, etc.).

 With expected shorter construction time and flexibility in deployment for small modular reactors, financing could be significantly
    easier with lower cost.

What are some key takeaways?  Q&A

 Gen III / III+ Reactors based on large pressurised water reactors (PWR) will continue to be the main reactors to
   be connected to grid in the next decade that will help in reducing CO2.

 FOAK Small Modular Reactors using PWR technologies with passive safety features are starting to be deployed
   and will very likely be commercialised in early 2030.

 High Temperature Gas-cooled Reactor (HTGR) technologies has been deployed and their inherently safety has
   been demonstrated (to a certain extent). They have great potential for supplying quality high temperature heat
   to relevant industries and a new plant is currently under construction in China and one planned in US for this
   purpose.

 Hybrid systems combining nuclear and renewables are considered by many to be most likely to achieve net
   zero emission by 2050.
