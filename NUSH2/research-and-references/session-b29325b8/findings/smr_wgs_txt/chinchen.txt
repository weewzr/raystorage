ABSTRACT 
Investigation 
of the water-gas shift reaction in a microreactor 
and in a semi 
technical plant at pressures up to 30 bar over a commercial 
iron oxide/chromia 
catalyst has allowed the following conclusions 
to be drawn. 
The true activation energy for the reaction is about 121 kJ mol -1 
The reaction becomes significantly 
pore diffusion limited over 5.4 mm pellets 
at temperatures 
above 370°C and at temperatures 
above 350°C over 8.5 mm pellets 
at 30 bar operating pressure. 
A detailed assessment of heat and mass transfer effects in the microreactor 
is given. 
INTRODUCTION 
The water-gas shift reaction, the reaction of steam with carbon monoxide to 
produce carbon dioxide and hydrogen, is a reversible exothermic 
reaction of 
particular 
interestdueto 
its industrial importance. It is a step in many industrial 
processes, oarticularly 
ammonia and hydrogen production. 
Two main classes of catalysts are used industrially 
as shift catalysts; iron- 
based catalysts and copper-based 
catalysts. The iron-based catalysts are some of 
the earliest heterogeneous 
catalysts used industrially 
and are commonly called 
high temperature 
shift catalysts operating 
in the temperature 
range of about 
320-450°C. Most industrial high temperature 
shift catalysts contain chromium oxide 
as well as iron oxide and it is generally believed that Cr203 acts as a stabiliser, 
retarding sintering and loss of surface area of the iron oxide. The optimum 
beneficial Cr203 content appears to be about 14 wt% Cr203 [l], but typically 
commercial 
high temperature 
shift catalysts contain 8-10% Cr203. Before use the 
catalyst, which is essentially 
Fe203 as manufactured, 
has to be reduced to Fe304 
which is thought to be the active phase. 
Numerous studies of the kinetics and catalysts of the water-gas 
shift reaction 
over iron oxide/chromium 
oxide high temperature 
shift catalysts have been reported 
in the past 20 years, but there is little or no agreement as to either the precise 
form of the rate equation or the values of the rate constants, or surprisingly 
the 
activation energy. Some workers have proposed first order kinetics [2,31, others

Podolski and Kim [13] studied the kinetics at atmospheric 
pressure in a recycle 
reactor and attempted to discriminate 
statistically 
between most of the previously 
proposed kinetic expressions. 
They found that only the Langmuir-Hinshelwood 
and 
the power-law models could adequately describe the reaction behaviour. 
Fott et al. 
[I43 noted that 20 different kinetic equations had been proposed in the last 40 
years, differences 
among authors being especially 
in their opinions of the reaction 
mechanism and the effect of pressure on the reaction rate. They suggest that the 
reasons for this are that most kinetic measurements 
were made at atmospheric 
pressure only and using integral rather than differential 
reactors. Data from their 
differential 
reactor at pressures up to IO bar also favour a Langmuir-Hinshelwood 
model with the surface reaction between adsorbed CO and H20 the limiting step. 
Measured activation energies range from 40.6 kJ mol-' [4] through 100 kJ mol-' 
[7,133, 115 kJ mol-' [15], 134 kJ mol-' [3], 142 kJ mol-' [I71 to 150 kJ mol-' 
[18]. As Ruthven [11] points out, the low values are probably affected by diffusion 
limitations 
and the values obtained by workers using small catalyst particles are 
generally in the range 100-113 kJ mol-'. 
All workers [2,4,5,14,19] 
have reported that the rate of reaction increases 
with increase in pressure. Fott 1141 asserts that the effect of pressure is the 
main criterion which enables the preliminary 
suitability 
of a given kinetic equat- 
ion to be evaluated. His experimental 
data show an increase in rate with pressure 
up to a pressure of about 5 bar; further increase in pressure had little effect 
on the rate. Chandra [19] also reports that the rate doubles as the pressure 
increases from 5 to 15 bar, but then increases only slightly as the pressure was 
further increased. Padovani and Lotteri [20] found that the rate increased to a 
maximum at IO-20 bar and decreased with further increases in pressure. 
The fall of catalyst activity with time has been studied by numerous workers. 
Mars [3] found that the catalyst loses activity due to sintering, the degree of 
which is a function of temperature 
and the age of the catalyst. For catalyst which 
had been used at temperatures 
of 510-55O"C, the loss in activity agreed well with 
the findings of Hoogschagen 
and Zwietering 
[21]. They tried to establish a 
quantitative 
relationship 
between the decrease in surface area of a catalyst and 
its age at different operating temperatures

In summary, the best evidence seems to suggest that the reaction over commercial 
sized catalyst suffers from diffusional 
limitations, 
that the kinetics are best 
explained by a Langmuir-Hinshelwood 
model, with the surface reaction rate between 
adsorbed CO and H20 being limiting, and that the catalyst ages due to sintering 
initially at a very fast rate but the activity becomes almost constant after a 
few months. 
In this paper, the importance of mass transfer effects in the reaction over a 
commercial catalyst is assessed and other aspects of the reaction kinetics are 
described in the succeeding papers. Two types of integral reactor were used. A 
pilot plant with a large tubular reactor containing two sizes of standard catalyst 
pellets simulated the conditions 
in full-scale reactors. A pressure micro-reactor 
unit allowed the use of smaller sizes of catalyst particles and shorter reaction 
times, which were needed to study some features of catalyst performance, 
e.g. 
intrinsic activity and initial decay of activity.

unit 
In the microreactor

cylindrical

wise blocked the capillary tube and it also gave a much smoother water flow, thus 
reducing any tendency for the steam supply to be irregular. 
Gases were analysed by on-line gas chromatography, 
the feed gas directly and 
the exit gas after passage through an ice-cooled trap and a U-tube containing

60°C. The CO and H2 were separated on the molecular sieve column, whereas the CO2 
was passed down the Porapak 'S' column. Residual water vapour was backflushed 
from the primary column. A Taylor Servomex microkatharometer 
was used as detector.

Film Diffusion. 
It is necessary to consider first the possibility 
of a film 
diffusion limitation 
in the semi technical plant. A common method of checking for 
this is to keep the space velocity constant and vary the flowrate noting whether 
the conversion 
remains constant. Constant conversion 
under these conditions 
is 
taken to indicate the absence of external mass transfer influence. This test has 
been criticised by Chambers and Boudart [28] since at the low particle Reynolds 
numbers encountered 
in test units, heat and mass transfer coefficients 
are relative- 
ly insensitive to changes in flow rates.

magnitude of the concentration 
gradient of the reactants between the bulk fluid 
and the surface of the catalyst particle. The most simplified example of this 
procedure is due to Wheeler [29] who has calculated that for a completely diffusion
