A B S T R A C T
Post-combustion carbon capture is a well-established technology to limit CO2 from industrial emissions.
However, challenges such as high energy requirements for solvent regeneration persist. Research is still being
conducted to improve the energy efficiency of the process. This study aims to present the results of process
intensification in a pilot-scale CO2 capture plant. The pilot-scale investigations were carried out at Amager
Bakke, a Waste-to-Energy facility in Copenhagen, Denmark. The tests were conducted by implementing the
split flow configuration as a method of process intensification. The CO2 rich stream was split before flowing
through the rich/lean heat exchanger. One of the split streams was fed to the stripper top, and the other
was sent through the heat exchanger and then fed to the stripper. The cold split stream at the stripper top is
heated by the overhead vapours which would otherwise escape the stripper. The current work discusses the
split flow configuration results of the pilot plant by employing 30 wt% MEA as the solvent and compares it to
the base-case performance of the pilot. Experiments were conducted by splitting 22% of the rich solvent and
feeding it to the stripper top. The performance of the split flow configuration was analysed at different reboiler
duties. A minimum specific reboiler duty of 3.45 GJ/tonne CO2 is obtained for the split flow configuration,
which is less than the base case by 7.8%. Additionally, a reduction in the condenser duty by at least 68% was
achieved by the implementation of the split flow configuration.
1. Introduction
Post-combustion capture (PCC) is a critical technology to reduce the
CO2 emissions into the atmosphere from industrial operations. Amine-
based CO2 capture is a well-established and mature technology. It can
be retrofitted to the existing power plants [1,2]. Implementing carbon
capture and storage for Waste-to-Energy (WtE) plants can make waste
an attractive source of zero or negative emissions energy, depending
on the fraction of biogenic waste [3]. There are three incineration
facilities integrated with CO2 capture currently. These are located in
the Netherlands (AVR and Twence plants) and Japan (Saga City) [3–
5]. In Saga City, Japan, 10 tonnes of CO2 is captured per day utilising
aqueous amine solvent [6,7]. The captured CO2 is utilised for algae
and crop cultivation [6]. At AVR’s WtE, 60,000 tonnes of captured
CO2 per year is supplied to the horticulture sector in the region [8].
830,000 tonnes of waste is processed at the Twence WtE facility and

power production in a coal-fired power plant as parasitic load [17].
Innovative process configurations can be implemented to reduce energy
consumption for solvent regeneration [18]. Split flow configuration is
a stipulated process intensification in which a fraction of the CO2 rich
solvent stream exiting the absorber bottom bypasses the main heat
exchanger (main HE) and enters the stripper top. In the main HE,
the cold rich solvent is heated by the hot lean solvent, whereas the
split flow fed to the stripper top is heated internally by the overhead
saturated steam, condensing the steam, which would otherwise be lost
from the system. The utilisation of the overhead steam to heat a portion
of the cold rich solvent reduces the reboiler duty [18–32].
Additionally, the cooling duty of the overhead condenser is also
reduced due to the lower mass flow of steam leaving the stripper. A
slightly different version of the usage of the overhead vapours has also
been patented [33]. A few modelling studies of the split flow configura-
tion have been conducted to analyse the energy reductions obtained for
solvent regeneration [34–41]. Furthermore, a few different variations
of the split flow configuration have been simulated [13,18,41–47].
However, minimal pilot data is available on implementing a split
flow configuration. The split flow configuration was tested at the
Tarong pilot plant using MEA as the solvent. A 7% reduction in the
regeneration energy was observed, along with a 60% reduction in
the cooling water requirement in the condenser. The influence of the
split flow configuration was stated to be strongly dependent on the
performance of the main HE [48]. Another pilot-scale testing of the
split flow configuration was conducted at the Jaworzno II Power plant,
which showed a reduction in the reboiler duty by 5% [49]. It is crucial
to conduct pilot-scale testing of such process intensification methods to
understand the performance of a CO2 capture plant and to validate and
develop better models.

2. Description
DTU, in partnership with Pentair Union Engineering, has developed
and built a Mobile Test Unit (MTU) for conducting pilot-scale CO2
capture. The pilot can process 150 Nm3/h of flue gas. A 3D illustration
of the pilot and a picture of the pilot at the WtE facility are presented
in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), respectively.
Fig. 1(a) exhibits that the pilot has four columns: absorber, desorber,
wash column, and an additional column for process intensification. The
taller columns at the back are used as absorber and desorber, whereas
one of the shorter ones in the front is used as the wash column. Process
equipment, such as filters, pumps, heat exchangers, and flow meters,
are in the two skids below the columns. A detailed description of the
pilot and the process has been given in a previous work [50]. The
results of the split flow configuration are presented and discussed in
the current work. The Process Flow Diagram (PFD) of the split flow
configuration is presented in Fig. 2.
Fig. 2 represents the PFD of the pilot for the split flow configuration.
The PFD of the process for the base case configuration and the control
structure have been described in detail in the previous work [50].

In the base case configuration, the flue gas is contacted with 30 wt%
MEA in the absorber. During the start-up of the pilot plant, the liquid
circulation through the absorber and the stripper is first established.
This is done either manually or in auto-mode. A control structure was
designed and implemented in the Programmable Logic Controller (PLC)
for the pilot plant. When the operator activates the auto-mode, the
rich pump starts, followed by the lean pump with a few minutes of
delay. The flows were expected to stabilise in approximately 10 min.

After a delay of 10 min, the reboiler was automatically switched on
at the duty given as the set point. Once the reboiler switches on, the
solvent circulating in the stripper begins heating. The control structure
is designed so that the pilot can accept flue gas once the stripper
column reaches a temperature close to 100 ◦C. It takes approximately
an hour for the stripper to reach this temperature. Following this, the
system sends a request to the WtE facility for flue gas, which triggers
the automatic gas inlet and outlet valves to open. Once the flue gas
is available to the pilot, capture and desorption of CO2 starts. The
start-up procedure takes approximately 1 to 2 hours, depending on the
functioning of the pumps, controllers, availability of flue gas, and other
factors. The control structure was designed to run the pilot 24 hours a
day with safety trips in place to ensure that the pilot runs safely when
operators are not on site and to avoid long start-ups every day.
The flue gas flow rate (FT1 in Fig. 2) is controlled by a Proportional
Integral (PI) controller. CO2 from the flue gas is captured by the
solvent, and the flue gas depleted of CO2 goes through the wash tower
for removing amine slip and maintaining water balance. The level in
the absorber sump (LT1 in Fig. 2) is maintained by a PI controller which
manipulates the cooling water flow rate to the water wash section in
the wash tower. The water wash level (LT3 in Fig. 2) is maintained by
a PI controller which manipulates the water make-up flow rate to the
absorber top.
The CO2-rich solvent (rich solvent) exits the absorber bottom and
is pumped through the main heat exchanger (main HE) before being
fed to the stripper at a packing height of 10.4 m (bed E). The rich
solvent flow rate (FT2 in Fig. 2) is controlled by a PI controller. The
stripper is operated at a pressure of 0.85 to 0.89 barg and the reboiler
temperature is maintained around 117 ◦C to 120 ◦C. The reboiler
temperature depends on the stripper pressure. The stripper top pressure
(PT1 in Fig. 2) is maintained by a PI controller. CO2 from the rich
solvent is stripped, and the CO2-lean solvent (lean solvent) exiting the
stripper is pumped through a flash vessel and the main HE for energy
recovery. The level in the flash vessel (LT2 in Fig. 2) is maintained
by a PI controller which manipulates the lean solvent flow rate to the
absorber.
The reboiler at the stripper bottom is integrated with a steam
generator. The steam generator consists of an electric heating ele-
ment submerged in demineralised water. When the steam generator
is switched on and duty is provided, the water is heated, and steam
is generated as the saturation temperature is reached. As steam is
generated, the pressure in the steam generator increases. The reboiler
is a plate heat exchanger. The steam from the steam generator is the
hot fluid, and the lean solvent flowing down the stripper is the cold
fluid. Due to the heat exchange, the steam is cooled and then condensed
as it reaches the dew point at the pressure maintained in the steam
generator. Condensation provides further heating to the lean solvent.
The lean solvent is heated until it reaches the saturation temperature
at the pressure maintained in the stripper. Once the saturation tem-
perature is reached, steam is generated from the lean solvent. This
steam produced from the lean solvent acts as the stripping agent in the
desorber. The steam and the condensate flow from and to the steam
generator by the thermosyphon concept. The steam flow rate produced
from the lean solvent corresponds to the electrical duty given as an
input to the heating element. The unutilised steam leaves the stripper
top along with the stripped CO2. The steam-CO2 mixture is cooled
in the overhead condenser. The steam is condensed, and the water is
added to the absorber sump, whereas the CO2 exits the pilot plant and
joins the WtE facility’s (Amager Bakke) exhaust line. Process conditions
such as flow rates, temperatures, pressures, densities, sump levels, and
concentrations are measured at several locations in the pilot plant. The
process conditions and the locations at which they are measured can
be found in Appendix B.
In the split flow configuration, the gas flow is the same as in the base
case configuration. A difference in solvent circulation is prominent. As
it can be seen from Fig. 2, a fraction of the rich solvent that exits the
absorber bottom at around 40 ◦C bypasses the main HE and directly
enters the stripper above packed bed F, which is at an accumulated
packing height of 13.9 m. This is called the ‘‘split flow’’. The split
flow’s flow rate is set by manipulating a manual valve. The remaining
rich solvent goes through the main HE, which is heated to around
100 ◦C by the hot lean solvent from the flash tank. The heated rich
solvent, ‘‘hot rich solvent’’, enters the stripper above packed bed E at an
accumulated packing height of 10.4 m. The split flow joins the hot rich
solvent in the liquid distributor above packed bed E. In the base case
configuration, all the solvent exiting the bottom of the absorber passes
through the main HE and enters the stripper at the liquid distributor
above packed bed E. Therefore, in the base case configuration, packed
bed F is unused. The details about the equipment are provided in
Appendix A. However, in the split flow configuration, the CO2-steam
mixture exiting packed bed E and moving through packed bed F is
expected to heat the cold split flow.
Components such as hoses, heat exchangers, liquid sumps and ves-
sels, and the reboiler are insulated with Armaflex. The four columns are
insulated with mineral wool, and the flue gas components are insulated
with glass wool.
3. Methodology
The pilot plant operation has been described in detail in the previ-
ous work [50]. The operation of the pilot, for the most part, was the
same for the split flow configuration, except for bypassing a fraction
of the rich solvent from the main HE. The split flow was diverted
from the main HE by a ball valve. Manipulating the valve helped
in adjusting the split solvent’s flow rate. Approximately 22% of the
rich solvent was sent as a split flow to the stripper. The experiments
were initially designed for 15% and 20% split flows as they showed
the most promising results in simulations conducted by the project
partner Pentair Union Engineering. As shown in Fig. 2, a PI controller
maintains the rich solvent flow rate, while a manual hand valve is
adjusted to maintain the split flow rate. However, due to improper
PI controller tuning and sensitivities of the hand valve, the split flow
could only be maintained at 22%. Nevertheless, the results obtained
at a 22% split flow have provided significant insights (as discussed
in Section 4) into the pilot’s performance. At this split flow of 22%,
the reboiler duty was varied to obtain various capture efficiencies and
analyse the pilot’s behaviour. The manipulation of the reboiler duty
was automated, which aided in running the pilot continuously while
maintaining the other operating parameters constant. Fig. 3 depicts an
example of this operation.
From Fig. 3, it can be observed that the total rich solvent flow
rate and the split solvent flow rate were maintained at a constant
magnitude, whereas the heating element duty was increased in steps
of 1 kW every 2.5 h. As mentioned in Section 2, the heating element
duty is equivalent to the steam flow rate generated in the reboiler
and is therefore considered the reboiler duty. In this example, the
rich solvent flow rate was maintained at 258 ± 6 kg/h, and the split
solvent’s flow rate was maintained at 41 ± 1 kg/h, and the reboiler
duty was stepped from 15.3 kW to 19.3 kW. The stripper pressure was
maintained for the experiments within a range of 0.87 to 0.89 barg.
As the solvent flow rate and stripper pressure were maintained as
constants, the changing reboiler duty influenced the lean loading,
rich loading, and capture efficiency. Solvent samples along the length
of the absorber were collected for the steady states obtained during
a daytime operation and analysed for MEA concentration and CO2
loading. For the steady states obtained during the night-time operation,
the measured densities of the rich and lean solvents were utilised to
determine the corresponding loadings. The methodology for loading
determination was described in the previous publication [50]. The most
important operating parameters of the pilot results discussed in this
work are presented in Table 1.

4.3. Influence of the split flow on the main heat exchanger

bypasses the main HE, whereas the lean solvent flow rate is similar

2. Desorption of CO2 by provided energy equivalent to the heat of

4.5. Influence of split flow on total cooling duty
In the split flow configuration, as a fraction of the rich solvent
bypasses the main HE and contacts the overhead steam in the stripper
top, the cooling duties of the heat exchangers are impacted. The PFD
presented in Fig. 2 depicts two locations where cooling is required:
1. The lean solvent exiting the main HE is cooled in the lean cooler
before entering the absorber.
2. The saturated CO2 stream exiting the stripper top is cooled in
the condenser before it joins the exhaust line.
The energy recovery is reduced due to bypassing a fraction of
the cold rich stream from the main HE. A reduction in the energy
recovery leads to an increase in the cooling duty of the lean cooler.
Simultaneously, using the overhead steam to heat the split flow in the
stripper reduces the amount of steam exiting the stripper. This reduces
the condenser duty, as seen in Fig. 19. Therefore, there is a trade-off of
these two cooling duties, as shown in Fig. 21.
Fig. 21 shows the cooling duties required in the lean cooler and the
condenser for the base case and split flow configurations at varying
stripping intensities. It can be observed that the cooling duty of the lean
cooler is higher for the split flow configuration than the base case. As
previously mentioned, lower energy recovery in the main HE causes the

4.6. Influence of lean temperature on absorber profile
The temperature of the inlet lean solvent influences the performance
of the absorber. High temperatures in the absorber enable the kinetics
of CO2 absorption, whereas low temperatures are thermodynamically
favourable [29]. The temperature profile in the absorber depends on
the liquid and gas flow rates, the lean solvent inlet temperature, and
the heat of absorption. As discussed in Section 4.5, with respect to the
total cooling duty, split flow configuration is advantageous only if the
lean cooler is operated at higher temperatures. Otherwise, the lowered
condenser duty is offset by the increased lean cooler duty.
The total cooling duty can be lowered provided higher lean temper-
atures do not worsen the absorber performance and ensure the desired
capture efficiency (𝜖). The absorber temperature profiles for the base
case and split flow configuration are presented in Fig. 23. The operating
and performance parameters for these data points are in Table 3. These
data points have similar lean loading, gas and liquid flow rates, inlet
gas temperature, and packing heights. Lean temperature is the only
variable parameter (39 ◦C for the base case and 49 ◦C for the split
flow).
Fig. 23 displays the temperature profile developed in the absorber
for the two configurations in which the lean temperatures differ. It can
be observed that the behaviour exhibited is similar in both cases. How-
ever, for the split flow configuration, for which the lean temperature

was 49 ◦C, the temperatures in the absorber are higher than in the
base case. A consequence of a high lean temperature is better kinetics
of absorption of CO2 into the solvent. Despite the temperatures being
higher at the absorber top and middle sections for the absorber for
the split flow case, it can be observed that the temperatures at the
bottom for the base case and split flow configurations. Therefore, the
kinetics are improved at the absorber top and middle due to higher
temperatures without negatively impacting the VLE at the bottom.
Consequently, the rich solvent loading for the split flow config-

maintain the lean temperature of the solvent higher than the rec-
ommended 40 ◦C as long as the temperature at the bottom is not
significantly increased. The patent by Arronwilas and Veawab [56],
has also shown that partitioning the absorber into high and low-
temperature regions in the upper and lower sections, respectively, is
beneficial. The authors predicted rich loadings as high as 0.58 mol

into kinetic- and thermodynamic-driven absorption sections. Therefore,
operating the lean cooler at higher temperatures is advantageous for
the upper half of the absorber. The temperature in the lower half can
be further lowered by using an intercooler or the rich solvent exiting
the absorber bottom as suggested in [56]. However, a higher lean
temperature could negatively influence the amine slip into the flue
gas [57], which will not be a part of the discussion of the present work.
4.7. Limits of sensible heat gained
The split flow utilises the latent heat from the overhead steam to
gain sensible heat. This occurs as the split flow and the steam pass
through packed bed F. Therefore, the temperature of the heated split
flow above packed bed E helps determine the magnitude of sensible
heat gained by the split flow (Fig. 2). However, due to process con-
ditions in the stripper, there is a limit on the amount of sensible heat
that can be extracted from the overhead steam. The amount of sensible
heat gained by the split flow and the temperature of the split flow above
packed bed E are presented in Fig. 24.

5. Conclusion
This current work analyses the performance of a pilot-scale CO2
capture plant by implementing the rich split flow configuration as a
method of process intensification. The tests were performed by utilisa-
tion of 30 wt% MEA.
The tests were conducted by splitting 22% of the cold rich solvent
before the main HE and feeding it to the stripper top. The reboiler
duty was varied to obtain different capture efficiencies. 𝜖of 90% was
obtained at a stripping intensity of approximately 200 kJ/kgtotal for
both the base case and split flow configurations. The SRD of the system
was 3.49 GJ/tonne CO2, which was 5.4% less than the base case
configuration, which was 3.69 GJ/tonne CO2 to achieve an 𝜖of 90%.
At any desired 𝜖, the split flow configuration was observed to result
in a lower SRD when compared to the base case. The minimum SRD
obtained for the split flow configuration was 3.45 GJ/tonne CO2, less
than the base case minimum by 7.8%, for an 𝜖of 83%.
The split flow configuration was observed to result in higher tem-
peratures in the stripper as compared to the base case. The split flow
entering the stripper top was heated by the overhead vapours exiting
the stripper. Depending on the stripping intensity maintained, a duty of
2.5 kW (20% of RD excluding heat loss) to 2.8 kW (21% of RD excluding
heat loss) could be gained from the overhead steam by the split flow.
Consequently, the saturated steam exiting the stripper top was observed
to reduce from 3 kg/h to 7 kg/h, requiring a cooling duty of 3 kW (25%
of RD excluding heat loss) to 7 kW (37% of RD excluding heat loss) in
the base case to 0.2 kg/h to 1.7 kg/h requiring a cooling duty of 0.2 kW
(2% of RD excluding heat loss) to 1.8 kW (11% of RD excluding heat
loss). A split flow of 22% could therefore reduce the cooling duty by at
least 68% from the base case. The split flow configuration also results
in an increase in the lean cooler duty due to lowered energy recovery in
the main HE. As a consequence of a higher lean cooler duty, the total
cooling duty of the system is similar for the base case and split flow
configurations.
A maximum limit of 2.8 kW was observed in terms of energy gained
due to the flashing of the solvent in the stripper. A deeper analysis
of the influence of different split flow values needs to be conducted.
Nevertheless, implementing split flow aids in energy gain from the
overhead vapours and reduction of the cooling duty requirements with
minimal process modifications. The SRD of the system can be reduced
by 5.4% by a split flow of 22%.
The results presented in the current work can be used for scaling
up of CO2 capture plants. The solvent flows can be determined based
on simple mass balances, and gas flows to be treated. The stripping
intensity and the SRD values would remain unchanged for a desired
capture efficiency at full scale. Utilities such as steam and cooling water
can also be scaled linearly. Apart from utilities, the auxiliary operating
costs include the power required for running the pumps, blower, the air
cooler, the PLC, electricity consumed by the operators, maintenance
work, make-up solvent, and safety sensors. The energy consumption
for these auxiliary units is well understood and is not discussed in this
work. This work does not also cover analysis related to solvent make-up
and service of components, which can be considered a separate study.
Scaling up of the equipment can be done in different ways. For the
columns, if the scale-up is done based on a constant residence time
and velocity of the flue gas in the absorber, the area of the column
would scale linearly with the gas flow to be treated, and the diameter
of the column would scale linearly on a logarithmic scale. The mass
transfer observed in the absorber and the stripper can be expected to be
equivalent to a full-scale plant, as the total surface area of the packing
material can be scaled based on the gas flows to be treated. The height
of the column would remain unchanged. The surface area of the plate
heat exchangers (main HE, lean cooler, condenser, and reboiler) used
at several places in the process scale linearly with the fluid flow rates,
assuming the heat transfer coefficient and the design LMTD is the same
as those used in the pilot plant. Pumps for solvent circulation can be
scaled based on the system curves. While discussion of other fixed costs
such as land, material, construction is vital, it is out of scope of the
current work.
