A B S T R A C T
Two HTGR-integrated hydrogen-production pathways based on steam methane reforming—helium-heated SMR 
(h-SMR) and electrified SMR (e-SMR)—are proposed. The former directly supplies HTGR heat to the reformer 
through helium, whereas the latter converts HTGR heat into electricity. Aspen Plus evaluates these systems 
against conventional and electrolysis-based benchmarks. Elimination of the fired furnace reduces natural gas 
consumption by 18.8% for h-SMR and 23.3% for e-SMR relative to gray hydrogen. Levelized cost of hydrogen is 
estimated at $2.37/kg for h-SMR and $2.99/kg for e-SMR, indicating cost competitiveness relative to the 
benchmarked pink and green hydrogen pathways under the assumed conditions. Furthermore, life-cycle as­
sessments reveal net global warming potentials of 3.5 and 3.18 kgCO2/kgH2 for h-SMR and e-SMR, a reduction of 
>70% relative to gray hydrogen. Sensitivity analysis confirms the economic robustness of the h-SMR pathway 
across market scenarios. HTGR-integrated SMR processes offer a practical and cost-effective transition pathway 
for large-scale hydrogen production.

1. Introduction
Achieving net-zero pathways commonly requires decarbonization 
strategies that combine various renewable electricity sources, such as 
wind and solar energy, with dispatchable low-carbon options, including 
nuclear generation and fossil-based systems equipped with carbon 
capture and storage (CCS) [1]. In this context, nuclear energy is an 
attractive low-carbon option because it can supply electricity and 
high-grade heat continuously and stably on a large scale, enabling 
centralized substitution for fossil-based energy inputs [2]. Many fossil 
fuel-based hydrogen production routes, such as reforming and gasifi­
cation, are strongly endothermic, and a significant fraction of plant-wide 
CO2 emissions originate from the combustion of additional fuels 
required to supply process heat. Therefore, replacing this heat input 
with non-carbon energy sources presents a direct opportunity to reduce 
CO2 emissions while conserving fossil fuels [3].
In this context, small modular reactors are of particular interest 
because their modular deployment and enhanced safety characteristics 
can enable more flexible operation than conventional large reactors, 
including the ability to vary thermal and electrical outputs through

power ramping and cogeneration [2]. Among small modular reactor 
designs, high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) concepts are 
frequently highlighted due to their inherent safety features and high 
outlet temperatures reaching ~950 ◦C [4], thereby supporting efficient 
electricity generation and the delivery of high-grade process heat to 
chemical and petroleum applications [5]. Although alternative 
nuclear-driven hydrogen pathways—including thermolysis, thermo­
chemical cycles, radiolysis, and photolysis—have been proposed, tech­
nological and economic limitations have hindered their near-term, 
large-scale deployment [6]. Consequently, HTGR-integrated steam 
methane reforming (SMR) is widely considered a promising near-term 
scalable route for low-carbon hydrogen production [7].
Helium-heated steam methane reforming (h-SMR) has been pro­
posed as a nuclear-assisted route for reducing the carbon intensity of 
conventional hydrogen production by directly supplying high- 
temperature heat to the reformer [8]. In Japan, the technical feasi­
bility of HTGR–SMR integration has been experimentally examined 
using a large-scale mockup facility [9]. Additionally, the Japan Atomic 
Energy Agency (JAEA) is conducting a project to integrate an SMR with 
the 30 MWt High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR), which 
supplies process heat at temperatures ranging from 750 to 800 ◦C. This 
system is expected to reduce methane consumption by 35% compared 
with the conventional route [10], with a demonstrated hydrogen pro­
duction capacity exceeding 800 Nm3/h [11]. Furthermore, the HTTR 
has achieved a reactor-outlet coolant temperature of 950 ◦C, enabling 
HTGR usage beyond electricity generation by providing high-grade heat 
suitable for advanced power cycles and hydrogen production [12]. In 
the United States, techno-economic evaluations performed by the Idaho 
National Laboratory have similarly suggested that HTGR-integrated 
SMR can reduce natural gas consumption by 11.6% and CO2 emissions 
by up to 38.6% when CCS is implemented [13]. Beyond such 
system-level assessments, reactor-scale studies have advanced the 
design of helium-heated reformers. For instance, Wu et al. developed a 
one-dimensional reaction–flow model for an HTGR-heated reformer 
tube and validated it against experimental results [14]. Later, they 
further extended this framework to a membrane reformer to quantify the 
effect of selective H2 removal on methane conversion and hydrogen 
yield [15]. Additionally, Mozdzierz et al. employed numerical analysis 
to examine the operating configuration of a helium-heated reformer and 
discuss a parallel-flow scheme for alleviating excessive temperature 
gradients and the associated thermal stress risks. Helium heating has 
also been explored for other endothermic catalytic processes [16]. For 
example, Zhang et al. performed a non-dominated sorting genetic 
algorithm-based multi-objective optimization of an industrial-scale 
helium-heated reverse water-gas shift reactor using a two-dimensional 
pseudo-homogeneous model [17].
Electrified steam methane reforming (e-SMR) is driven by the limi­
tations of conventional fired reforming in which heat must be supplied 
externally, resulting in inefficient reactor heat transfer and substantial 
methane fuel consumption [18]. Resistive heating, also known as Ohmic 
or Joule heating, is a specific implementation of electrification that in­
volves passing an electrical current through a conductive reactor to 
directly generate high-temperature heat [19]. Wismann et al. demon­
strated this concept using Ni-based catalyst-coated FeCrAl alloy tubes 
with Cu sockets at the tube ends to deliver electrical power for Ohmic 
heating [20]. This direct resistive heating scheme is associated with 
highly uniform heat delivery near the catalyst layer. Specifically, the 
predicted temperature difference across the catalytic coating is report­
edly <2 ◦C, supporting stable operation under strongly endothermic 
conditions [21]. Such integrated electrified SMR concepts enable 
compact reactor configurations and rapid start-up responses, as heat is 
supplied directly by electricity rather than by an externally fired furnace 
[22]. Significantly, scaling analysis indicated that a conventional 
1100 m3 side-fired reformer producing 2230 kmol H2/h could be 
replaced by a 5 m3 resistance-heated reformer, demonstrating the 
effectiveness of this technology for modular scale applications [23].
Despite these advances, a comprehensive plant-wide analysis is 
essential, as reactor and heat-supply configurations significantly influ­
ence the utility, heat recovery, and optimal energy layout of hydrogen 
plants [24]. Accordingly, this study proposes HTGR-integrated nuclear 
heat integration (h-SMR) and nuclear electricity integration (e-SMR) 
and comparatively evaluates their techno-economic and environmental 
performances. Specifically, the system boundary explicitly incorporates 
the HTGR-based supply of both heat and electricity, rather than treating 
nuclear energy as an external utility. To ensure comparability, all 
pathways are evaluated based on consistent functional units and system 
boundaries. To further evaluate the competitiveness of the proposed 
HTGR-integrated configurations, pink and green hydrogen production 
pathways are established as reference benchmarks. These pathways 
represent the most prominent low-carbon alternatives currently being 
pursued and provide broader context for comparative assessment. In 
Fig. 1, gray and blue hydrogen correspond to SMR without and with 
CCS, respectively, while pink and green hydrogen refer to hydrogen 
produced via nuclear- and renewable-powered electrolysis [26].
2. Process description and modeling
2.1. Technological overview
Both h-SMR and e-SMR are hydrogen production processes based on 
SMR, which is the primary industrial process employed for hydrogen 
production [27]. SMR involves the reaction of natural gas with 
high-temperature steam; however, since natural gas typically contains 
sulfur and heavier hydrocarbons—in addition to its primary component, 
methane—a pre-reformer is employed to prevent coke formation in the 
main reformer [28]. During pre-reforming, the conversion of represen­
tative species, such as ethane and propane, follows the reaction stoi­
chiometry outlined in Eqs. (1) and (2): 
C2H6 + 2H2O →2CO + 5H2, ΔH◦= 347 kJ / mol,
(1) 
C3H8 + 3H2O →3CO + 7H2, ΔH◦= 499 kJ / mol.
(2) 
Subsequently, SMR converts natural gas into hydrogen according to 
the reforming reaction outlined in Eq. (3). Since this reaction is highly 
endothermic, a continuous external heat supply is required. In conven­
tional SMR processes, a fired furnace is employed in which radiant heat 
is transferred to the process gas flowing through catalyst-filled tubes. 
CH4 + H2O ↔CO + 3H2, ΔHR = 206 kJ/mol
(3) 
To maximize the hydrogen yield, the reformer effluent was directed 
into a water-gas shift (WGS) reactor. Since the WGS reaction (outlined in 
Eq. (4)) is exothermic, the conversion in the high-temperature shift 
reactor is thermodynamically limited by equilibrium at elevated 
temperatures. 
CO + H2O ↔CO2 + H2, ΔHR = −41 kJ/mol
(4) 
Subsequent hydrogen purification is achieved using pressure-swing 
adsorption (PSA), which exploits the principle that impurities are 
selectively adsorbed onto solid adsorbents at elevated pressures as 
hydrogen passes through. The PSA process typically consists of five 
primary steps: adsorption, regeneration, pressure equalization, depres­
surization, and pressurization. These steps proceed sequentially in a 
cyclic manner across multiple adsorbent beds. In conventional pro­
cesses, high-purity hydrogen is recovered for storage, whereas the 
remaining gas mixture, referred to as PSA tail gas, is utilized as fuel for 
the SMR furnace.
The transition from gray to blue hydrogen was achieved by inte­
grating a carbon capture unit (CCU) into a conventional SMR process. In 
this study, the CO2 capture unit was placed downstream of the WGS 
section and upstream of the PSA unit to capture CO2 from the shifted 
syngas stream. Although various carbon capture technologies have been 
developed, including adsorption, absorption, and membrane separation,

In the h-SMR process, the conventional gas-fired furnace is replaced

HTGR flows through the shell side, transferring thermal energy to the 
catalyst-filled tubes where the reforming reaction occurs [30]. By 
eliminating the requirement for natural gas combustion as a heat source, 
this configuration significantly reduces direct CO2 emissions from the 
furnace stack. In the e-SMR process, a conventional gas-fired furnace is 
replaced by an electric heating mechanism based on Ohmic heating 
principles [23]. Unlike traditional SMR in which heat is supplied via 
combustion, e-SMR uses electrical energy to directly drive the endo­
thermic reforming reaction. In the process simulation, the required 
thermal duty is supplied by electricity generated from the Brayton cycle 
of the HTGR [31]. Electrification necessitates a significant modification 
of the process configuration in which the combustion section is elimi­
nated. Furthermore, the PSA tail gas, which contains unreacted methane

2.2. Process modeling
All processes were simulated using Aspen Plus V14 [32]. The Pen­
g–Robinson equation of state was used as the thermodynamic model for 
the hydrogen production processes, while the ENRTL-RK model was 
applied to the carbon capture processes. Figs. 2 and 3 illustrate the 
simplified process flow diagrams for the proposed systems.
For the purpose of this study, it was assumed that the natural gas 
feedstock was desulfurized [33]. Within the pre-reformer operating at 
500 ◦C, almost all heavier hydrocarbons were converted into carbon 
oxides, methane, and hydrogen. The steam-to-carbon (S/C) ratio was set 
to 2.7 to optimize reaction efficiency while mitigating carbon deposi­
tion. The other key assumptions employed for process modeling are 
summarized in Table 1. Unlike conventional SMR, which employs the 
PSA tail gas as fuel for the furnace, the h-SMR and e-SMR configurations 
recycle this stream back into the process, since these systems operate 
without a fired furnace. Consequently, it was necessary to install an 
additional compressor downstream of the PSA.
The HTGR served as the primary thermal energy source in this study. 
Using helium as the coolant, thermal energy was transferred from the 
primary loop to the secondary loop via an intermediate heat exchanger, 
assuming a temperature difference of 50 ◦C. The key design parameters 
of the HTGR are summarized in Table 2. In the process simulation, the 
HTGR core was modeled using a heater block to simulate steady heat 
generation. Specifically, secondary helium heated to ~900 ◦C was 
supplied to the reformer to maintain the required reaction temperature. 
From a safety perspective, the operating pressure of the process gas in­
side the reformer tubes was maintained at lower than that of the sec­
ondary helium coolant. This pressure differential ensures that in the 
event of tube rupture, leakage occurs from the helium side to the process 
side, thereby preventing ingress of flammable gases into the nuclear 
system [10].
Electrolysis-based hydrogen production routes, including pink and 
green hydrogen, are included as benchmarks because they represent 
widely discussed low-carbon alternatives in market and policy contexts 
[35,36]. These benchmarks were evaluated using literature-based, 
parameterized techno-economic, and environmental assessment calcu­
lations rather than detailed process simulations, consistent with the 
scope of this work, which focuses on HTGR-integrated reforming and 
plant-wide integration. All pathways were compared using consistent 
functional units, financial assumptions, and system boundaries.

3. Evaluation methodology
3.1. Techno-economic analysis (TEA)
The capital expenditure (CAPEX) and operating expenditure (OPEX) 
were estimated based primarily on the methodology and cost correla­
tions provided by Turton et al. [37]. The key economic assumptions used 
in the techno-economic analysis (TEA) are summarized in Table 3. 
However, costs associated with nuclear waste disposal were not 
included in the cost estimation as a separate item. This exclusion is, 
therefore, treated as a limitation of the present economic assessment.
The base cost of each piece of equipment was calculated using the 
key capacity attributes derived directly from the process simulation 
results, as described by the following scaling law (Eq (5)):

Subsequently, the cost of the bare module was determined by applying 
scaling and pressure factors to account for the operating conditions. 
Detailed values are presented in Table S2 and S3. All costs were adjusted 
to the 2024 base year using the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index 
(CEPCI) to account for inflation.
3.2. Levelized cost of hydrogen
The Levelized Cost of Hydrogen (LCOH) was adopted as the primary 
economic indicator to ensure consistent comparison and evaluate the 
feasibility of various production pathways. Sensitivity analysis was 
conducted to account for the technological maturity and inherent un­
certainty of the input parameters. The key variables selected for this 
analysis included the price of natural gas, HTGR cost of capital, interest 
rate, and carbon tax. Through this analysis, the key cost drivers exerting 
the most significant influence on the LCOH were identified. These eco­
nomic and sensitivity results provide critical insights for determining the 
cost competitiveness of current technologies and identifying the optimal 
conditions for the transition toward hydrogen production with reduced 
emissions.
3.3. Life cycle assessment
Life cycle assessment (LCA) is widely recognized as a fundamental 
tool for quantifying the environmental impacts of hydrogen production 
technologies [38]. In this study, LCA was conducted in strict accordance 
with the methodological framework outlined in the ISO 14040 and ISO 
14044 standards [39,40], which serve as global benchmarks for envi­
ronmental auditing in the energy sector. Global warming potential 
(GWP) was selected as an impact factor. The system boundary was 
defined as “cradle-to-gate,” covering direct process emissions as well as 
indirect emissions associated with the upstream production of feed­
stocks and utilities. However, the environmental impacts of nuclear 
waste management were not separately quantified. Therefore, the LCA 
results for HTGR-integrated pathways may vary depending on 
waste-management practices. All assessment data were sourced from the 
Ecoinvent Ecoquery database, as detailed in Table 4 [41].
4. Results and discussion
4.1. Technical results
Initially, the technical performance of the proposed hydrogen- 
production scenario was evaluated. Specifically, the impact of inte­
grating HTGR nuclear heat on reducing natural gas consumption and 
shifting the distribution of energy resources is discussed in detail.
4.1.1. Model validation
To ensure the reliability of the process simulation model, the gray 
hydrogen process, which served as the base case, was validated against 
benchmark data from the IEAGHG technical report [34]. Table 5 pre­
sents a comparison between the simulation results and the reference 
data. The key performance indicators, including specific CO2 emissions, 
showed strong correlation with the reference values, with relative errors 
under 1%, confirming that the developed model accurately captures the 
characteristics of commercial SMR processes.
4.1.2. Process performance
The hydrogen production pathways were modeled based on the 
validated gray hydrogen case. To ensure a consistent comparative 
framework, all pathways were constrained to a production capacity of 
9 t/h and a fixed product purity of 99.99%. This approach isolates the 
effects of process configuration on feedstock consumption, utility re­
quirements, and environmental impacts; the results are summarized in 
Table 6. Both h-SMR and e-SMR processes avoid the use of natural gas as 
a fuel by substituting the heat required for reforming with high- 
temperature helium from the HTGR and from nuclear-generated elec­
tricity, respectively.

4.2. Economic results
Fig. 4 illustrates the LCOH breakdown for natural-gas-based 
hydrogen production methods. In conventional gray and blue SMR, 
LCOH is predominantly driven by feedstock costs, which account for 
55−57 % of the total unit cost. In the HTGR-integrated pathways, the 
cost structure shifts as HTGR-related costs become an additional major 
contributor. For h-SMR, feedstock cost remains the largest component, 
while HTGR-related costs account for 27% of the LCOH. In contrast, e- 
SMR is more strongly affected by HTGR-related expenses, which account 
for 53% of the LCOH, mainly due to the additional nuclear heat-to- 
electricity conversion required for electric reformer heating. Further­
more, h-SMR had a lower unit cost than e-SMR, which can be attributed 
to the superior exergetic efficiency of direct thermal integration, which 
eliminates the energy losses associated with converting nuclear heat into 
electricity for reformer heating.
Fig. 5 presents the LCOHs for gray, blue, h-SMR, and e-SMR 
hydrogen production, which were derived from the current process 
simulation. For benchmarking purposes, the corresponding values are 
also shown for pink and green hydrogen, calculated based on literature 
data [42–44]. The HTGR-integrated reforming pathways (h-SMR and 
e-SMR) demonstrated superior economic performance over electrolysis. 
While the gray and blue processes maintained the lowest LCOHs 
($1.78–1.93/kg), the proposed h-SMR ($2.37/kg) and e-SMR 
($2.99/kg) systems bridged the gap between fossil-based and 
renewable-based hydrogen. Compared to the benchmarked pink and 
green pathways, nuclear-integrated SMR routes offered a significantly 
more cost-effective decarbonization strategy.
4.3. Environmental considerations
Fig. 6 provides a breakdown of the net GWPs for the various 
hydrogen production pathways, which are categorized into direct CO2 
emissions, natural gas, electricity, and water. Gray hydrogen exhibited 
the highest net GWP at 12.91 kg CO2/kgH2, where direct CO2 emissions 
constituted the dominant share at 9.10 (70%), followed by a natural gas 
contribution of 3.80 (29%). Although the total GWP of blue hydrogen 
decreased to 8.23, direct CO2 emissions (51%) remained the primary 
contributor, as CO2 capture was applied only to the shifted syngas 
stream, whereas the CO2 in the fired reformer flue gas was not captured. 
By contrast, the HTGR-integrated h-SMR and e-SMR processes proposed 
in this study demonstrated significant reductions, yielding GWP values 
of 3.5 and 3.18, respectively, representing a decrease of >70% 
compared with gray hydrogen. In particular, the contribution of direct 
CO2 emissions dropped sharply to 4% in both processes. This result in­
dicates that the primary driver of net GWP reduction is the structural 
elimination of direct emissions. Unlike conventional SMR, which gen­
erates vast quantities of CO2 through tail gas combustion within a 
furnace, the proposed pathways suppress these emissions by recycling 
tail gas as a process feedstock. Consequently, the dominant GWP 
contributor shifts from direct CO2 emissions to emissions from natural 
gas.
Fig. 7 compares the net GWPs of electrolysis-based pathways with 
those of the proposed methods. The pink hydrogen pathway, which uses 
HTGR-derived power, achieved the lowest GWP (0.26–0.38) in this 
comparison, while the environmental performance of green hydrogen 
varied significantly depending on the renewable energy source. Specif­
ically, wind-based green hydrogen (0.66–0.97) exhibited a low GWP, 
whereas solar-based (photovoltaic) green hydrogen (3.31–4.95) gave a 
relatively high GWP, primarily due to greater emissions from photo­
voltaic power systems compared to wind. Furthermore, considering the 
same power source, the solid oxide electrolyzer cell (SOEC) exhibited a 
lower GWP than the alkaline electrolyzer (ALK) and the proton ex­

4.4. Sensitivity analysis
Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the effects of market 
uncertainties (e.g., feedstock prices and carbon taxes) on the economic 
competitiveness of the proposed pathways. Fig. 8 presents the results of 
the sensitivity analysis, highlighting the impact of fluctuations in indi­
vidual variables on the LCOH, while maintaining all other parameters 
constant. Since the baseline cost estimation assumes an environment 
without carbon tax, the range of the carbon tax includes only the base 
and maximum values. The obtained results indicate that the h-SMR and 
e-SMR processes are the most sensitive to changes in natural gas prices, 
as they still rely on natural gas as the primary feedstock.

5. Conclusion
This paper proposed heat-integrated h-SMR and electricity- 
integrated e-SMR as HTGR-integrated hydrogen production pathways 
and evaluated their technical, economic, and environmental perfor­
mances through process simulations. From a technical perspective, the 
proposed processes maximized resource efficiency by eliminating the 
conventional fired furnace through HTGR integration and recycling the 
PSA tail gas as a process feed. Consequently, the HTGR-integrated pro­
cesses reduced natural gas consumption by 18.8% for h-SMR and 23.3% 
for e-SMR compared with conventional gray hydrogen. Furthermore, 
direct carbon emissions were suppressed by >98% via structural 
changes in the heating mechanism, which fundamentally altered the 
emission profile of the SMR process. Additionally, techno-economic 
analysis demonstrated that HTGR-integrated reforming offers a signifi­
cantly more cost-effective decarbonization strategy than electrolysis- 
based routes. The LCOH was estimated to be $2.37/kg for h-SMR and 
$2.99/kg for e-SMR, revealing high cost-competitiveness against 
benchmarked pink hydrogen ($5.10–6.98/kg) and green hydrogen 
($5.35–9.86/kg). Sensitivity analysis further confirmed the economic 
robustness of the h-SMR pathway, which maintained a broad dominant 
region over blue hydrogen across various natural gas prices and carbon 
tax scenarios. Furthermore, life-cycle assessments revealed that the 
proposed processes substantially reduced climate impact, with net GWPs 
of 3.5 kgCO2/kgH2 for h-SMR and 3.18 kgCO2/kgH2 for e-SMR. These 
values represent significant reductions of >70% compared to gray 
hydrogen (12.91 kgCO2/kgH2). Notably, the contribution of direct CO2 
emissions dropped sharply from 70% in the conventional process to 4% 
in the proposed systems, confirming that the benefits of HTGR- 
integrated pathways arise from structural decarbonization rather than 
simple end-of-pipe capture. Overall, the study findings demonstrate that 
HTGR-integrated SMR processes exhibit potential as a bridging tech­
nology for reducing emissions from fossil fuel-based hydrogen produc­
tion. Notably, integration with HTGR can achieve energy independence 
during hydrogen production, eliminate the requirement for PSA tail gas 
combustion, and exploit existing SMR infrastructure, thereby structur­
ally reducing direct CO2 emissions. This approach could, therefore, 
represent a practical, scalable, and short-term solution for large-scale 
hydrogen supply with reduced emissions. Building on the steady-state 
assessment and environmental framework developed in this study, 
future research should focus on developing a dynamic simulation model 
to evaluate the operational flexibility of the system under HTGR load- 
following scenarios.
