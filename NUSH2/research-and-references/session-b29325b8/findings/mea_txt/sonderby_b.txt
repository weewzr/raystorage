1. Introduction
Carbon dioxide is emitted from large sources of fossil fuel com- bustion, particularly power plants. It is one of the key man-made greenhouse gases that must be minimized in order to avoid cata- strophic climate change. The capturing of trillions tons of CO2 is a daunting task, but is a challenge that has to be overcome success- fully if disaster is to be averted (Schrag, 2007).
The process for post combustion capture consists of four stages as sketched in Fig. 1. First, CO2 is removed from the ﬂue gas by absorption in a packed absorber column. Then ‘rich’ solvent con- taining CO2 is led to a desorber column and heated in a reboiler to release the CO2. Next, CO2 is compressed and transported to a geo- logical storage site or injected into an oil and gas reservoir. Finally the regenerated ‘lean’ solvent is recycled to the absorber.

A pilot absorber column for CO2 recovery from ﬂue gases was constructed and tested with aqueous 30 wt% monoethanolamine (MEA), a primary amine, as capture solvent. The pilot plant data were compared with a mathematical rate based packed-column model. The simulation results compared well with the pilot plant data.
The packed height of the column can be varied from 1.6 to 8.2 m by means of ﬁve different liquid inlets. The column has an inner diameter of 100 mm and is packed with structured Mellapak 250Y packing. Counter-current ﬂow is used.
The pilot plant performance was investigated by changing three parameters: the absorption height, liquid ﬂow rate, and the loading of lean MEA. This was done using a synthetic ﬂue gas consisting of 10% CO2 with a ﬂow rate of approximately 33 m3/h at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure. 23 runs were performed.
It was observed that while CO2 recovery increases with an increase in ﬂow rate of absorbent and absorption height, it decreases as the lean CO2-loading of the absorbent increases. In addition it has been possible to obtain temperature bulges in the bottom part of the absorber by the applied operation conditions. Bulges are observed at liquid ﬂows around 4.2 L/min and below. The results showed that is was possible to achieve 80% recovery with 3.3 m absorption height and a liquid ﬂow of 2.1 L/min. The simulations show good agreement with the experimental values, although slight deviations arise as the CO2-loading increases and the temperature bulge becomes more distinct.

A number of studies have been carried out in order to inves- tigate CO2 capture in pilot plants using MEA. Most of these have appeared since 2006, with the exception of a few earlier studies. The older work conducted at University of Regina in Canada is described by Tontiwachwuthikul et al. (1992), Aroonwilas et al. (1999), and Aboundheir (2002) and more recently by Idem et al. (2006, 2009). There is a growing activity on post combustion capture in Norway evidence by the MEA related studies of Tobiesen et al. (2007) and Hessen (2010). Work has been performed at University of Kaiser- slautern (Notz et al., 2007) as well as several studies at University of Texas, Austin (Dugas, 2006; Kvamsdal and Rochelle, 2008). A number of larger industrial funded consortium projects have also appeared (Hessen, 2010; Dugas et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2008).
While some studies have been performed concerning the mech- anisms of CO2 capture from ﬂue gases using solvents, there is limited pilot plant data for such systems, where enough informa- tion has been reported to enable model development. A vast number of both structured and random (dumped) packing types are available from commercial suppliers. Different packing types have been used in the above-mentioned works. Structured packing was considered in Hessen (2010), including 250Y Mellapak, whereas dumped packing was used by Lee et al. (2008), Dugas et al. (2009), Kvamsdal and Rochelle (2008), and Tontiwachwuthikul et al. (1992). Here the Koch-Glitsch IMTP 25, 40, 50 and ceramic Berl saddles, 12.7 mm, were used. Some studies included the same pilot facility but with different packing material. Aboundheir (2002) tested the A4 structured packing and the two dumped packings, IMTP 15 and Pall Ring-16mm. Dugas (2006) tested the structured Flexipac 1Y and IMTP 40 dumped packing. Finally Aroonwilas et al. (1999) tested the Gempak 4A structured and IMTP dumped packings. Both packings were tested in three different sizes. They found that the structured packings performed better than dumped

While packing suppliers should have a comprehensive under- standing of the performance of their packing, far from all packings have received a thorough and independent comparison in the open literature. An interesting study could be performed across research laboratories where different pilot plants independently compared the same packings. On the other hand, having validated our pilot plant here for a given packing and a “standard” solvent (MEA) we have conﬁdence that the same plant could be used to evaluate both different packings and different capture solvents.
Previous pilot scale studies (Tontiwachwuthikul et al., 1992; Aroonwilas et al., 1999; Aboundheir, 2002; Idem et al., 2006, 2009; Tobiesen et al., 2007; Hessen, 2010; Notz et al., 2007; Dugas, 2006; Kvamsdal and Rochelle, 2008; Dugas et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2008) cover a range of operation conditions. Three input vari- ables are interesting from a process point of view. These are the solvent CO2 loading, solvent concentrations, and ﬂue gas com- position. The lean loading covered is 0–0.35 and rich loading is 0.33–0.49 molCO2/molMEA. The concentration of MEA in the MEA- H2O solvent varies from 18 to 54 wt%. Most of the experiments are carried out using 30 wt% MEA concentration. The CO2 concentra- tion in the ﬂue gas coming into the bottom of the absorber is in the range of 4–20 vol%.
Several analytical methods have been used in the determination of liquid CO2 content for loading evaluation. Of the studies above, CO2 content was mainly found by a barium chloride method very similar to the one outlined in Appendix A of this work. It is cheap but time consuming. Other methods are the Total Organic Carbon (TOC) analysis, used by Dugas (2006) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) used by Idem et al. (2009). The beneﬁt of this last method is that it is on-line and unmanned measurements can be performed. This method was not pursued because of the high cost involved. A titration method described by Horwitz (2006) may also be used. We chose to proceed with the Barium Chloride method since we had prior experience with it.
The absorber temperature proﬁle (temperature along the length of the column) can be measured by means of appropriately placed temperature probes. The reaction between CO2 and the solvent is exothermic, resulting in a steep temperature increase at cer- tain conditions. An observations by Kvamsdal and Rochelle (2008) was that the location of the temperature bulge can move from the bottom to the top of the column. The temperature bulge is quan- tiﬁed by its magnitude and location in the column and is seen as a convex curve on the temperature proﬁle. At a critical liquid to gas (L/G)-ratio the temperature bulge will be in the middle of the

The aim of this work is to report results for a new pilot-scale (10 m high, 10 cm internal diameter) absorber. Results are reported for Mellapak 250Y structured packing and MEA solution as cap- ture solvent. In addition to reporting this new data, which can be used for model development and testing (here it is tested against an existing published model (Gabrielsen et al., 2006; Faramarzi et al., 2009)), a secondary aim is to validate the plant for a given packing and solvent in order to proceed to other packings and sol- vents with greater conﬁdence. The following operation conditions were covered: A lean loading of 0.11–0.30 and a rich loading of 0.15–0.39 molCO2/molMEA. A 30 wt% aqueous MEA solution and a synthetic ﬂue gas composition of 9.2–10.9 vol% CO2. A modiﬁed and optimized barium chloride method was developed for the analysis of the liquid phase for the determination of the total dissolved CO2 (see Appendix A). The plant was ﬁtted with two storage tanks with a volume of 1 m3 each. Therefore it was possible to run a series of experiments with the same lean loading and MEA concentration. The basis of the different operation conditions are consequently very similar and therefore comparable. The results of the various experimental runs contain detailed information about the loading and temperature proﬁles throughout the column. Additionally the CO2 concentration in the gas phase was measured at both the gas inlet and outlet of the column.
The data were also modelled using a previously developed rate-based model (Gabrielsen et al., 2006; Faramarzi, 2010). While further development of the model was not undertaken as part of this work, the production of new pilot-plant data did provide fur- ther validation of the model, as usable pilot plant data are not plentiful.
2. Modelling CO2 absorption
The experimental pilot runs performed in this work were mod- elled using a rate based column model originally developed by Gabrielsen et al. (2006, 2007) and Gabrielsen (2009) and fur- ther modiﬁed by Faramarzi et al. (2009, 2010) and Faramarzi (2010). The main purpose of this work was the experimental validation of the pilot scale absorber. More detail on the model can be found in previous work (Gabrielsen et al., 2006, 2007; Gabrielsen, 2009; Faramarzi et al., 2009, 2010; Faramarzi, 2010) and only the basic elements are presented here. Previous works have presented models for mass transfer coefﬁcient, mass trans- fer area, hydraulics and physical properties. A comprehensive comparison of the various components that make up a com- plete rate-based model is considered beyond the scope of this work. The model is mass transfer based and accounts for trans- port properties, thermodynamics, phase equilibrium and reaction kinetics. The thermodynamic model used is extended UNIQUAC (Faramarzi, 2010). Extended UNIQUAC is used here to model the thermodynamic properties required in the full rate-based model, because it is the model where we have most experience (Gabrielsen et al., 2006, 2007; Gabrielsen, 2009; Faramarzi et al., 2009; Faramarzi, 2010). Any thermodynamic model for electrolytes which is at a reasonable level of development (for example e- NRTL) could have been used, although it was not the purpose of this work to develop and present comparisons of thermodynamic models.

The thermodynamic component of the model is used in the calculation of gas and liquid properties in order to estimate heat of CO2 absorption, heat capacities, Henry’s constants, and partial pressures (which are required to provide an estimate of various driving forces). Billet and Schultes (1999) and Rocha et al. (1993, 1996) have provided equations for mass transfer coefﬁcients. In this study the correlation by Rocha et al. was used, since it gave better agreement between the modelling and experiments per- formed in this work. Correlation parameters were obtained from the original work of Rocha et al. The surface enhancement factor (FSE) was set equal to FSE = 0.25 (in Rocha et al., 1996, it is set to 0.35). The reason for the deviation from the Rocha et al. correlation is that the pilot plant used in this work does not have as high a surface efﬁciency of an industrial sized column which has a much greater diameter.
The kinetic component of the model is used in the calculation of the enhancement factor, E, in Eq. (10), where it describes the excess driving force obtained due to reaction between the solvent and CO2 compared to the case without reaction. An investigation of the enhancement factor theory was made by Faramarzi et al. (2010) where a discussion is given of the more advanced implicit and sim- ple explicit enhancement factor equations. It was concluded that all enhancement factors gave good results for the absorption part. In this work, the more advanced implicit equation derived by Kreve- len and Hoftijzer was used. It is a more general equation which can also be used in the stripper calculation. In the enhancement factor equation derived by Astarita et al. (1983) (see Table 1) an explicit expression has been derived. It involves a variable n which is a con- stant describing the smoothness of the transition of CO2 from gas phase to the liquid phase. A higher value of n gives a smoother transition. The square root of M is the Hatta number and E∞is the enhancement factor for instantaneous reaction. For the condi- tions of the experiments performed here, all enhancement factors gave reasonable agreement with experiments, although it may be expected that at other conditions (for example in the stripper) the pseudo ﬁrst order approximation may no longer be valid.
The rate-based model is solved as a boundary value prob- lem (BVP) involving Eqs. (1)–(8). The boundary conditions are the deﬁned inlet ﬂows of ﬂue gas, liquid composition, pressure, and temperature. The main results are the properties of the cleaned gas and rich liquid. The model is thus capable of predicting more than just the temperature and the CO2-loading proﬁles: Compositions, ﬂows, transport properties, thermodynamic properties, and kinetic properties throughout the length of the column are also calculated.
3. Experimental setup

The packing height for each section is 0.82 m and thus a maximum effective absorp- tion height of 8.2 m can be obtained. The packing diameter is 0.084 m. Liquid samples can be taken from every section using custom- made sampling valves, V1–V10 in Fig. 2. These consist of special liquid collection rings which are machined into the section con- nectors. Samples can also be taken from the storage tanks T1 and T2.

The gas phase composition is measured at the inlet and outlet of the column, Ci1 and Ci2 in Fig. 2. The carbon dioxide transmitters used are Vaisala CARBOCAP® series GMT221. These cover a range of 0–20% CO2, with a ±2% expected accuracy of the full scale reading. Flow is measured manually by four calibrated rotameters FI1–FI4.
In the top of the absorber unit there is a demister with a sub- sequent steam trap to ensure that no solvent is recycled with the gas stream. It is possible to cool down the recycle gas with heat exchanger HE1, although this was not used for these experiments.
Two storage tanks each with a volume of 1000 L are located next to the column. Before a run commences stock solution of the desired composition is prepared and stored in one of the tanks. The stor- age tanks are large enough to collect liquid from several runs. This gives the possibility to perform similar experiments with the same concentration of MEA and lean loading of the solution. One stor- age tank holds the spent rich solvent while the other contains fresh solvent with a known loading.
3.1. Chemicals used
85 wt% (99.8% purity) MEA was obtained from BRENNTAG Nordic, Denmark. The solvent was diluted using demineralized water to the desired concentration of 30 wt% MEA. A synthetic ﬂue gas was created by nitrogen and carbon dioxide from gas cylinders from AGA Denmark, with purities of 99.95% and 99.8% respectively. For the liquid analysis of CO2 using the barium chloride method, 0.1 M NaOH and 0.5 M HCl solution were obtained from VWR. The concentrations are known with an accuracy of 0.1%. The 0.5 M Bar- ium Chloride solution was prepared from BaCl2 · 2H2O(s) from VWR dissolved in water.
4. Experimental procedure
Before a run begins, pure nitrogen is continuously recycled in the column in order to minimize degradation of the solvent from air oxidation. The lean liquid ﬂow valve is then opened and CO2 ﬂow adjusted in order to reach a deﬁned inlet gas concentration. The recycle gas ﬂow is kept constant in all experiments in the rage 470–540 L/min. The CO2 concentration was maintained at 10%. The CO2 ﬂow varied from 107 to 113 g/min, depending on the over- all ﬂow of gas. The concentration of MEA was 30 wt% based on the initial batch mixed in the storage tank. The lean MEA was at room temperature varying between 19.5 and 23 ◦C. 4.1. Obtaining steady state
Steady state was usually obtained within 5–25 min, depending on the operation condition and previous set point. Steady state was assumed to have been attained when the internal column temper- atures and the inlet and outlet CO2 concentrations deviated from their ﬁnal values by less than 4% (see Fig. 3). A process variabil- ity limit within 4% was felt to be an adequate representation of steady-state.

4.3. Analysis of samples
The CO2 content in the liquid sample was determined by the so-called the barium chloride method (Sun et al., 2005). The CO2 is precipitated as BaCO3, which is dissolved by adding a known amount of 0.5 M HCl and back titrated using 0.1 M NaOH. The amount of CO2 can then be determined as the difference of excess HCl compared to NaOH. Every sample was analysed twice and the experimental error was estimated to be less than ±4%. To reach this low deviation a great deal of optimization had to be done with respect to the existing procedure. The optimized analysis procedure can be found in Appendix A.

It should be noted that a lean loading greater than zero will generally result in greater errors, because of the errors inherent in the measurement of CO2 concentration.

The column pressure drop measured in this work is shown in Table 5. A number of factors contribute to it. Consequently the solvent cannot absorb as much CO2 and the driving force is diminished. A decrease in recovery with higher loading is therefore expected.

5.9. Sources of error during modelling
To minimize errors all instruments were calibrated prior to each experimental campaign. It is believed that the manual ﬂow meters are the sources of most deviation. Accuracy in ﬂow measurement could be improved by replacing the manual instruments with dig- ital readouts which are also logged directly to the computer.

6. Conclusion
Experimental data (CO2 recovery, temperature proﬁles, pres- sure proﬁles, CO2 loadings and CO2 gas phase concentrations) for a pilot plant absorber using structured packing (Mellapak 250Y) for capturing CO2 with 30 wt% MEA are presented and validated against an in-house absorber model.
There is good agreement between the experimental values and model predictions with respect to temperature, CO2-loading, and CO2 gas phase concentration. Some deviations are observed where the model predicts the onset of a so-called “ignited” state. An ignited state was not observed experimentally.
A temperature bulge was observed which moved upwards and changed size as various L/G-ratios were applied. A bulge was never observed in the top part of the absorber.”
