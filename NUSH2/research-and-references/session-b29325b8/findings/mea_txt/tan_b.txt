1. Introduction
CO2 removal is an essential process in natural gas sweet- ening. Raw natural gas typically contains a large quantity of methane along with heavier hydrocarbons such as ethane, propane, isobutene, n-butane and signiﬁcant amount of CO2 (Faiz and Al- Marzouqi, 2011). Prior to sale or usage, CO2 must be removed to an acceptable level, as CO2 is highly corrosive in the presence of mois- ture thus rapidly destroys pipelines and equipment; it also reduces the heating value of a natural gas stream and wastes pipeline capac- ity (Safari et al., 2009). Natural gas in commercial operations constitute varying amounts of CO2 ranging from CO2–free natural gas in Siberia to CO2 content of up to 90% in the Platong and Erawan ﬁelds in Thailand. The Natuna ﬁeld in the Greater Sarawak Basin in Indonesia is the largest gas ﬁeld in Southeast Asia, with an estimated 46 trillion cubic feet of recoverable reserves. Unfortunately, it remains unex- plored primarily due to high CO2 content at 71% (IEA, 2008a). Meanwhile, natural gas reserves with low CO2 content are rapidly depleting with the continuous extraction of the fossil fuel over the years. Hence, there is an urgent need to explore the high CO2

Meanwhile, in steam reforming of natural gas, it is also important to separate CO2 from H2 in the pre-combustion capture process before H2 could be fed into combustion turbine (Olajire, 2010). In Malaysia, CO2 content in natural gas ﬁelds varies from 28 to 87%. Over 13 trillion cubic feet of natural gas reserves are unde- veloped due to high concentration of CO2 (Darman and Harun, 2006). These high CO2 content gas ﬁelds in Malaysia represent sig- niﬁcant CO2 capture and storage (CCS) opportunity. Besides that, pre-combustion CO2 capture i.e. gasiﬁcation plants with high pres- sure and high CO2 concentration synthesis gas is also another area of opportunity for CCS (Figueroa et al., 2008). Globally, as of year 2011, there were 62 active or planned commercial scale integrated CCS projects, comprising capture, transport and sequestration elements, for sequestering of CO2. However, only seven projects were in the operational stage while the rest were in the evaluation, deﬁnition, or execution stages (Hardisty et al., 2011). As such, valuable insight generated by continuous and intense research on CCS is required in order to accelerate the transition from evaluation to operational stage. The success of this commercial demonstra- tion would enable the exploration of high CO2 content natural gas reserves around the world (Michael et al., 2011). Meanwhile, amine systems have been widely used for many years to remove entrained CO2 in raw natural gas (Abu-Zahra et al., 2007; Hardisty et al., 2011). One of the advantages of amine sys- tems is low hydrocarbon loss, besides being able to meet the low CO2 speciﬁcation required in processed natural gas (Sridhar et al., 2007). In general, most of the existing CO2 capture systems are based on chemical absorption combined with heat induced CO2 recovery (Desideri, 2010; IEA, 2008a). For elevated pressure absorption system, water was formerly used for CO2 absorption but due to its low CO2 removal efﬁciency and extremely high pumping load, it was gradually replaced with more efﬁcient system using chemical or organic physical solvents (Kohl and Nielsen, 1997). However, the chemical solvents are usu- ally diluted in water before usage under continuous absorption process in order to reduce the chemical’s corrosiveness. Among the solvents available, the most widely used solvent in indus- trial process for capturing CO2 is monoethanolamine (MEA), which is predominantly diluted to 10–30 wt% in water (Aroonwilas and Tontiwachwuthikul, 1997; Dave et al., 2009; Sønderby et al., 2013). Absorption in a physical solvent relies on the solubility of CO2 in the solvent rather than the chemical reaction with the solvent. It is usually more suitable for high pressure bulk removal applica- tion (Chakma, 1999). The solvent could be regenerated by varying the pressure or temperature. However, since the CO2 concentra- tion in ﬂue gas from coal ﬁred power plant is typically up to 15% (Dave et al., 2009; IEA, 2008a; Thomas and Kerr, 2005; Yan et al., 2008) at low pressure condition, chemical absorption is gener- ally preferred for CO2 capture in ﬂue gas because of their higher absorption capacity at low CO2 partial pressure (Aroonwilas and Tontiwachwuthikul, 1997; Schwendig, 2010). Hybrid solvent, which consists of both physical and chemical solvents, has the potential to minimize the weakness of each sol- vent whilst simultaneously offer the opportunity to formulate the solvent according to the high pressure offshore ﬁeld or onshore gas received
condition. As indicated in Fig. 1, hybrid solvent is expected to provide ﬁne cleanup at high CO2 partial pressure.
To date, information on hybrid solvent is very limited in open literature. In recent years, there are growing research on hybrid solvents using a combination of methanol with diethanolamine (DEA) or MEA (Archane et al., 2008; Usubharatana and Tontiwachwuthikul, 2009) and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) with diethylene glycol (DEG) and MEA (Vaidya and Mahajani, 2005). However, thus far, these studies were conducted under batch or semi-batch condition. von Harbou et al. (2014) assessed the potential of using ethanol as co-solvent for aqueous MEA based on simulations with an equi- librium stage model for post-combustion CO2 capture process and found that the absorption rate of CO2 was enhanced by addition of ethanol. However, its volatility caused very high ethanol loss through the top of the absorber. This led to increased complexity of the capture plant due to incorporation of an additional ethanol recovery unit. As such, it was concluded that other solvents bear- ing similar properties yet have lower vapor pressure should be used instead.
Usubharatana and Tontiwachwuthikul (2009) who studied on the enhancement factor and kinetics of CO2 capture using MEA–methanol hybrid solvent under batch condition found that when
methanol was mixed into the MEA solution, the rate of MEA deprotonation decreased. However, the CO2 absorption rate increased with the presence of methanol in the MEA hybrid solvent as methanol enhanced the diffusivity and solubility characteristics of the solvent. This showed the complex behavior of hybrid solvent; hence, more studies need to be conducted for each combination of hybrid solvent in order to understand its unique behavior. Due to the increasing need to explore the high pressure and high CO2 content natural gas ﬁelds in order to fulﬁll the rising energy demand worldwide (IEA, 2008b), more efﬁcient solvents should be developed to purify natural gas with high CO2 content. Mix- ing a speciﬁc organic solvent into chemical solvent is expected to provide ﬁne cleanup for natural gas processing due to improved afﬁnity to absorb CO2 compared to water. However, a comparison study on the absorption performance of physical solvent, aqueous chemical solvent and hybrid solvent under continuous ﬂow process at high CO2 concentration and high pressure condition needs to be conducted to establish better understanding of the hybrid solvents unique characteristics. Hence, the main objective of this work is to study the absorption performance of high CO2 concentration nat- ural gas at various pressure conditions in a countercurrent packed column using a solvent which consists of MEA as well as water and/or NMP. A study on the individual physical solvents’ perfor- mance was also conducted in order to gain insight on its ability to absorb CO2 on its own. NMP was selected as the organic physical solvent in this study due to the following reasons:
(a) Low viscosity and is completely miscible with water. This would facilitate pumping and reduce pumping power needed for the process (Shannon et al., 2011). (b) Able to tolerate and remove H2S. It could be used for treating high pressure gases with high sour gas concentrations at ambi- ent temperature (Hochgesand, 1970; Shannon et al., 2011). This means that it could be applied for offshore application which deals with signiﬁcant amount of H2S. (c) Its maximum operating temperature is high (200 ◦C). This would enable operation at a wide temperature range (Shannon et al., 2011).
NMP is the physical solvent used in the Purisol process due to its attractive properties of low vapour pressure, low viscosity and high CO2 absorption capacity (Tian et al., 2012). Being a very polar organic solvent, NMP was found to have superior CO2 solubility compared
to dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) at elevated pressure up to 9 MPa (Rajasingam et al., 2004).

2. Materials and
2.1. Materials
Monoethanolamine (MEA) (99%) and N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) (99%) used in this study were purchased from Acros Organ- ics. Distilled water was used to prepare the aqueous and hybrid solvent for this work. CO2 (99.9%) used in this experiment was pur- chased from Air Product Malaysia while natural gas with 97% of CH4 and 2% of CO2 was supplied by PETRONAS Dagangan Bhd.
2.2. Experimental set up and procedure
A schematic diagram of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. The absorption column has an inner diameter (ID) of 0.145 m and packing height of 1.0 m. The packing used in this study was structured packing FLEXIPAC 1Y by Koch-Glitsch. There was a dis- tance of 0.5 m between the packing and liquid distributor. The absorption experiment was initiated by introducing a gas mixture of CO2 and natural gas to the bottom of the absorption col- umn. The concentration of CO2 in the CO2/NG feed gas mixture was adjusted using the mass ﬂow controller. A back pressure regulator was installed to maintain the pressure of the absorption column at the desired level. Liquid feed was then pumped to the top of the column at a ﬂow rate of 1 l/min from a 200-l feed tank to cre- ate countercurrent contact between gas and liquid. The absorption process was operated until steady state condition was achieved. The CO2 concentration in the feed gas and treated gas was mea- sured from the start of the experiment until it reached steady state, using CO2 infrared (IR) gas analyzer model ZRJF4Y25 (Fuji Electric Instruments).

3. Results and
discussion
3.1. Effect of
pressure on physical solvents
Fig. 3 shows the CO2 removal performance along the column at steady
state condition using water as the physical solvent. T “zero” column height represents the location of CO2 inlet while the high- est point was the location of liquid distributor where fresh solvent and gas ﬁrst came into contact. Water was included as a physical solvent in this work as the reference point. Since it would also be present in MEA solution, it was necessary to understand the contri- bution of water in removal of CO2 absorption physically. From the graph, it was observed that the CO2 removal efﬁciency improved with increasing pressure. Less than 1% of CO2 was removed by water at 0.1 MPa. At 1 MPa, slight improvement was observed as approximately 4% of CO2 was removed at the gas outlet. CO2 removal efﬁciency further improved when the column pressure was increased to 5 MPa. The results obtained were consistent with previous report that CO2 was relatively insoluble in water due to liquid ﬁlm resistance (Kohl and Nielsen, 1997). Kohl and Nielsen (1997) reported that water had low CO2 removal efﬁciency even at elevated pressure. Fig. 4 depicts the CO2 removal performance along the column at steady state condition using NMP as the organic physical solvent. The results obtained showed 100% CO2 removal at gas outlet at all three pressure condition tested i.e. 0.1 MPa, 1 MPa and 5 MPa. This showed that NMP was an efﬁcient physical absorbent since CO2 was completely removed even at a very short column distance; 0.39 m at both 1 MPa and 5 MPa as well as 0.83 m at 0.1 MPa.

3.2. Effect of pressure on MEA solution
Fig. 5 shows the CO2 removal performance along the column at steady state condition for 20 wt% MEA aqueous solution. Approxi- mately 76% of CO2 removal was achieved at the gas outlet when the pressure was low (0.1 MPa). The removal efﬁciency was observed to be improved to 95% when the column pressure was 1 MPa. This trend was consistent with ﬁndings by Godini and Mowla (2008) who also observed increased CO2 removal efﬁciency when CO2 absorption test was performed in a pilot plant using MEA aqueous solution at pressure condition between 0.1 and 1 MPa. Zhang et al. (2003) who conducted a CO2 absorption study in packed column using MDEA and piperazine (PZ) up to 0.625 MPa also indicated that the absorption rate of CO2 at elevated pressure was larger than that of atmospheric pressure because of the Marangoni effect. The Marangoni effect could occur because at high CO2 partial pres- sure, there was an increase of interfacial tension resulting from the concentration increase in the interface. This condition would increase the instability of the gas/liquid-phase interface and lead to interfacial disturbance (Tan et al., 2012). Based on Fig. 5, CO2 was completely removed at an elevated pressure of 5 MPa at the gas outlet. In fact, the CO2 absorption performance was observed to be signiﬁcantly improved at high pressure as compared to CO2 absorption at 1 MPa because 100% CO2 removal was detected from a column distance of 0.83 m onwards. Despite the earlier observation from Fig. 3 in Section 3.1 that the increase
of pressure from 1 MPa to 5 MPa only had minimal inﬂu- ence to the CO2 removal performance in the middle of the column

when water was used as the solvent, results shown in Fig. 5 indi- cated that at high pressure of 5 MPa, the CO2 intake capacity of the MEA aqueous solution could be substantially enhanced. This observation was in line with ﬁnding by Ghulam et al. (2013) that the CO2 solubility of amine solution was signiﬁcantly improved at high pressure of up to 6 MPa. From Fig. 5, it was also observed that at 0.1 MPa and 1 MPa, sub- stantial difference of CO2 removal performance was detected from the point of column height of 1.77 m to 1.05 m. This was because at 1.77 m, fresh solvent ﬁrst came into contact with the gas stream in the column. At this point, the active absorbent in the fresh sol- vent has the highest absorption capacity; hence, the efﬁciency of CO2 removal was highest. At 1.05 m column height, the solvent had reacted with CO2 to a certain extent. Hence, the CO2 loading in the solvent which increased as the solvent travelled downwards from height of 1.77 m to 1.05 m in this countercurrent absorption process reduced the efﬁciency of CO2 removal as observed in Fig. 5. Increase of CO2 loading in the solvent reﬂected lower available active absorbent concentration in the solution and this condition could signiﬁcantly reduce the mass transfer coefﬁcient (Buzek et al., 1997). Arashi et al. (1997) observed that when CO2 loading in the lean solution was high, the driving force of mass transfer from gas to solution became small and the decrease in CO2 removal efﬁciency could not be compensated even with the increase of solution ﬂow rate. Similar trend was found by Dinca and Badea (2013) whereby the CO2 removal capacity was signiﬁcantly reduced in rich CO2 loading MEA solvent. The strong inﬂuence of CO2 loading to the mass transfer coefﬁcient was also discovered by Setameteekul et al. (2008). Among the process parameters studied (amine concentra- tion, CO2 partial pressure in feed gas, temperature, CO2 loading of solvent and liquid circulating rate), the authors observed that CO2 loading of the solution was the most inﬂuential process parameter affecting the overall mass-transfer coefﬁcient. Fig. 6 depicts the CO2 removal performance along the column at steady state condition for hybrid solution consisting of 20 wt% MEA solution with 40 wt% NMP + 40 wt% water. A similar trend was also observed for this hybrid solution with substantial improvement of CO2 removal at 5 MPa.

The results seemed to be in agreement with observation by Rivas and Prausnitz (1979) who also found that NMP was the best solvent for absorb- ing CO2 as the Henry’s constant for NMP was the lowest among the ﬁve solvents tested (i.e. monoethanolamine, diglycolamine, sul- folane, propylene carbonate and NMP) at pressure below 1 atm. The seemingly superior performance of NMP, even at low pressure, was surprising since the typical equilibrium lines for chemical and physical absorption indicated that physical absorption is favored at high CO2 partial pressure while chemical absorption is favored at low CO2 partial pressure (Hochgesand, 1970). However, Vaidya and Mahajani (2005), who studied the reaction kinetics of CO2 with MEA + NMP + DEG (diethylene glycol) using stirred cell reactor operated batch-wise, investigated the role of NMP in the hybrid solvent by conducting two experiments in the absence of MEA at low CO2 partial pressure up to 5 kPa. They observed that the rate of CO2 absorption was higher than that expected. They deduced that in addition to physical absorption, chemical reaction between CO2 and NMP could possibly take place.

Leites (1998) compared the CO2 solubility in aqueous MEA and MEA–organic solvents mixtures at CO2 partial pressure of up to 0.103 MPa and found that CO2 solubility in MEA aqueous solution was higher compared to solubility in MEA–NMP solution. However, the difference between them was not signiﬁ- cant. Therefore, the CO2 removal performance trend observed for MEA aqueous and hybrid solution at lower pressure seemed to be in agreement with the observation on CO2 solubility by Leites (1998). On the other hand, Fig. 9 shows that all three solvents achieved 100% CO2 removal at the gas outlet for at 5 MPa. Substantial increase of CO2 absorption along the column suggested increased CO2 intake capacity at higher pressure. This higher intake is expected as carbamate hydrolysis can occur with amine, particularly at high pressures. This hydrolysis would generate free amine which can react with additional CO2, hence increasing the loading of the amine solution (Sartori and Savage, 1983). Based on the solubility study for partial pressure of CO2 up to 0.005 MPa, Vaidya and Mahajani (2005) observed that NMP enhanced the solubility of CO2 in hybrid solution. It was also deduced that the addition of NMP did not interfere with the reaction between MEA and water (Vaidya and Mahajani, 2005). However, based on Figs. 7–9, when a portion of the water was replaced with NMP, the higher CO2 intake capacity trend was only observed for MEA hybrid solution at 5 MPa. At 0.1 MPa and 1 MPa, it was observed that MEA aqueous solution seemed to have better CO2 removal performance compared to MEA hybrid solution. This sug- gested that different absorption mechanism might be taking place for the hybrid solution when it was at high pressure condition. The results from Figs. 8 and 9 were consistent with ﬁndings by Rivas and Prausnitz (1979) who conducted preliminary design calculations for sweetening of sour natural gas based on gas solu- bility data. They found that at 1.5 MPa, the ﬂow rate for MEA–NMP solvent had to be 50% more than the ﬂow rate for MEA aque- ous solution in order to achieve similar CO2 removal performance. However, when the pressure was increased to 7 MPa, MEA–NMP solvent could achieve similar CO2 removal performance with aqueous

4. Conclusions
From this study, it was conﬁrmed that water had minimal phys- ical
absorption for CO2. On the contrary, NMP was observed to be an efﬁcient physical absorbent for CO2. Increasing pressure was found to have positive impact on CO2 removal performance for water, NMP, MEA aqueous solution and MEA hybrid solution. This trend was especially signiﬁcant when the pressure was increased to 5 MPa. The CO2 intake capacity for MEA solutions were notice- ably increased at high pressure condition. The results from this study suggested that high pressure could accelerate the reaction between MEA aqueous solution and MEA hybrid solution with CO2. The MEA–NMP hybrid solution showed better CO2 removal perfor- mance compared to the aqueous solution only at 5 MPa. At 0.1 MPa and
1 MPa, it
was observed that MEA aqueous solution had better CO2 removal performance compared to MEA hybrid solution.
