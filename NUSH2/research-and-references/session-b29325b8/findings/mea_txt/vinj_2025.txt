A B S T R A C T
Post-combustion CO2 capture is a widely recognised technology for controlling industrial CO2 emissions.
However, issues like the substantial energy demands for solvent regeneration still need to be solved. The
current work aims to discuss the outcomes of process optimisation within a pilot-scale CO2 capture facility
by implementing interheating in the stripper to reduce energy consumption. The pilot-scale experiments
were executed at Amager Bakke, a Waste-to-Energy plant in Copenhagen, Denmark. These tests used 30 wt%
Monoethanolamine as the solvent. A semi-lean solvent from the middle of the stripper is withdrawn for external
heating by an interheater and fed to the stripper at the same height. A steam generator with adjustable
electrical duty is used as the heat source for the interheater. The novelty of this work lies in the pilot scale
demonstration of stripper interheating at an industrial facility, which has seldom been done. The objective is to
experimentally demonstrate the performance of the Interheating configuration at the pilot scale and analyse
the potential improvements in the energy consumption of the process. The interheating experiments were
conducted by varying the reboiler duty in the range of 12 to 20 kW and the interheater duty in the range of 2
to 5 kW while maintaining a solvent flow of 267 kg/h and a flue gas flow of 99 kg/h. The results provide several
possible scenarios, are discussed in detail, and are compared to previously obtained base case results. While
maintaining a capture efficiency of around 83%, stripper interheating has demonstrated a specific reboiler duty
of 2.71 GJ/tonne CO2 compared to 3.71 GJ/tonne CO2 achieved by the base case operation. A 3% reduction in
the specific reboiler duty was achieved by interheating at a capture efficiency slightly over 90%. A preliminary
model based on the experimental results has shown a further reduction of SRD to 2.25 GJ/tonne CO2 at high
capture efficiencies.
1. Introduction
Post-combustion capture (PCC) is a critical technology essential to
eliminate industrial CO2 emissions into the atmosphere. PCC is closely
linked to achieving SDG goals such as Climate Action and Afford-
able and Clean Energy. Amine-based CO2 capture is a well-established
technology which can effectively capture 90 to 100% of the CO2
emissions from flue gases released by fossil-based power plants, Waste-
to-Energy (WtE) facilities processing Municipal Solid Waste (MSW)
and biogenic waste, and biogas plants. Monoethanolamine (MEA) is
one of the most known and commonly used amine solvents. MEA
has several advantages, such as fast reaction kinetics, high solvent

high solvent regeneration energy requirements, solvent degradation,

Additionally, compressing the recovered CO2 for transportation further

advanced process configurations that optimise the stripper operation
can help overcome the disadvantages. MEA is a well-documented and
tested solvent in operation, solvent degradation, emissions, and process
simulation. Therefore, it is a good candidate for testing advanced
process configurations. There are several documented process config-
urations in the literature which report an improvement in the energy
efficiency of CO2 capture, such as intercooled absorber, interheated
stripper, split flow, lean vapour compression, among several others [9].
In our previous work, we have discussed in detail the demonstration
of split flow configuration [10]. The current work aims to discuss the
implementation of stripper interheating.
Interheating is done by withdrawing a stream of semi-lean solvent
from the middle of the stripper and injecting it back into the strip-
per [9,11–15]. This is a simplified version of heat integrated stripper
and is easier to implement on existing structures [9]. Interheating in
the stripper optimises the temperature profile in the stripper’s top and
middle. An optimal temperature profile can be achieved by heating
the semi-lean solvent, bringing the stripper temperature in the middle
closer to the reboiler temperature [9]. The interheater modification was
predicted to reduce the stripper parasitic loss by 4.6 to 6.1% [13,15,
16]. Several other works can be found in the literature which conducted
simulations of interheating of the stripper [17–26].
The semi-lean solvent could be interheated by various sources [9]:
• the hot flue gas before it is cooled and sent to the absorber [27]
• the reboiler condensate [11,16]
• the hot lean solvent exiting the stripper bottom [11,16,28]
• a combination [11]
• high-temperature heat pumps integrated with waste heat recov-
ery.
Simulations were conducted to model waste heat recovery from
different parts of a post-combustion power plant as a heat source for
interheating the semi-lean solvent in the stripper [12]. The patent
says that waste heat recovery for interheating in the stripper causes
a reduction in the reboiler duty by 6.77% and an increase in the
condenser duty by 1% [18].
Muhammad et al. [29] has simulated the use of reboiler conden-
sate for interheating the stripper. Simulations were done to optimise
the withdrawal and feed stages in the stripper. An optimal SRD was
observed when a CO2-rich solvent was withdrawn from stage 2 (from
the top) and fed to the stripper at stage 15 (from the top). When the
solvent was interheated, it caused further steam and CO2 production.
As the interheated solvent was fed to stage 15, the vapour stream had
a longer contact time with the solvent in the stripper, thus stripping

De Miguel Mercader et al. [15] provides an overall perspective of
the implementation of several configurations with no and low level of
heat integration and their impact on the net efficiency of a 1070 MWe
coal-fired power plant connected to a CO2 capture plant (250 MWe
equivalent). It was reported that implementing an interheated stripper
with low-level heat integration results in the least penalty on the net
efficiency of the power plant. The interheated case with low-level heat
integration gave the best results compared to several configurations,
such as base case, Lean Vapour compression (LVC), split flow, and
LVC + split flow. However, an interheated stripper without any heat
integration was shown to have a higher penalty on efficiency when
compared to LVC + split flow with no integration. Karimi et al. [30]
has detailed the positive and negative effects of interheating through a
simulation study. Interheating was shown to negatively impact the SRD
of the process without heat integration in the system. However, when
done in conjugation with heat integration, interheating was beneficial
by bringing the temperature profile in the stripper closer to the optimal.
An optimal stripper temperature profile would be linear, eliminating
pinch regions and efficiently using the packing. Both the reboiler and
the condenser duty were shown to be reduced by 5.7% and 13.3% for
one interheater and 9.9% and 22.7%, respectively, for two interheater
cases. The optimal location for the placement of the interheater was
also simulated and said to be 1/3rd from the top of the column.
Van Wagener [26] has shown that interheating has the most sig-
nificant impact when done in the middle of the stripper. When the
interheater was placed at the bottom, directly above the reboiler, the
extracted solvent from the stripper was much hotter than the middle
of the stripper. Therefore, less heat could be exchanged. Additionally,
the driving force at the reboiler was higher than the middle or the
stripper top. Implementing interheating just above the reboiler would
cause desorption of CO2 due to a high driving force rather than heating
the solvent. The interheater at the stripper middle would operate
closer to the mass transfer pinch point, thus effectively heating the
solvent. The equivalent work was shown to be reduced by 1.5 kJ/mol
CO2 by implementing interheating. It was presented that the stripper’s
performance was improved by interheating due to higher selectivity of
CO2 compared to water at high temperatures. Hot lean solvent exiting
the stripper was used to interheat the semi-lean solvent.
Frailie et al. [17] conducted a simulation study of interheating in
stripper for 8 m PZ, 7 m MDEA/2 m PZ, and 5 m PZ solvents. The hot
fluid for interheating was the hot lean solvent exiting the stripper.
As the duty was displaced from the main HE to the interheater, the
temperature of the hot rich solvent entering the stripper top was lower.
A decrease in the hot rich solvent temperature resulted in an increased
value of PCO2∕PH2O and consequently reduced the SRD of the system. Li
et al. [19] also showed that the use of hot lean solvent for interheating
the stripper reduces the energy requirement for the production of
steam and sensible heating of the solvent, thus reducing the SRD by
6.1% from 3.6 GJ/tonne CO2 to 3.38 GJ/tonne CO2. Zhao et al. [20]
has shown that the SRD could be reduced to 2.56 GJ/tonne CO2 by
implementing interheating by hot lean solvent for MDEA/PZ based
solvent. Additionally, the net efficiency penalty was reported to reduce
from 8.49% to 8.25%.
Li et al. [25] conducted a Techno-economic assessment on several
advanced process configurations such as split flow, intercooling, and
interheating. Interheating the stripper was reported to reduce the net
costs by US$2.9/tonne CO2 despite the increased capital costs due to
extra heat exchanger requirements.
Stoffregen et al. [31] reported a pilot-scale demonstration of strip-
per interheating at a coal-fired power plant. However, no informa-
tion is available regarding the configuration’s performance. Stripper
interheating by use of the hot lean solvent and steam condensate
from the reboiler has been commercialised by Mitsubishi Heavy In-
dustries at a natural gas-fired power plant in Abu Dhabi [32]. The
commercial plant utilised KS-1 solvent, and an energy consumption of
2.9 GJ/tonne CO2 was obtained using stripper interheating. However,
there is no documentation of the operation or detailed analysis of the
configuration.

Pilot-scale studies have been reported for heat-integrated stripper
configuration [8,33,34]. The pilot plant had internal heat exchangers
built in the stripper [35,36]. Due to the location of the heat exchangers
internal to the stripper, ambient losses were reduced, column internal
temperatures and solvent regeneration rates were improved, and SRD
values were reduced [18,28,37]. The reboiler duties were reported to
reduce by 9 to 11% using a combination of split flow configuration and
interheated stripper through internal heat exchangers [33]. However,
no difficulties or challenges were reported regarding servicing such
internal heat exchangers in the stripper.
It can, therefore, be concluded that interheating in stripper can
reduce the penalty on the net efficiency of a power plant, reduce the
SRD of the capture plant, and reduce the capital costs of captured
CO2 [15,19,20,25]. Though there are abundant simulation studies of
the stripper interheating configuration, there is a lack of experimen-
tal implementation and detailed analysis of this configuration, which
leaves a considerable knowledge gap in its practical implementation
and operation at a pilot-scale. Implementing such advanced configu-
rations at a pilot scale is critical to understanding the performance of
the capture plant and operational difficulties, validating the existing
models, developing better ones, and conducting FEED studies.
The current work aims to bridge this considerable knowledge gap
by presenting the results of a pilot scale demonstration of the strip-
per interheating configuration. The goal of the stripper interheating
configuration is to optimise the energy consumed in the stripper by
improving the temperature profile while maintaining a high capture
efficiency compared to the base case configuration.
Implementing advanced configurations such as interheating at a pi-
lot scale is crucial before scaling up to full-scale plants. Uncertainties in
the operating and ambient conditions can be challenging and influence
the performance of capture plants, as detailed in Vinjarapu et al. [5].
Therefore, this study is valuable in understanding the improvements in
the performance of the process in an industrial setting as opposed to
controlled environments maintained in a laboratory.
The tests were conducted at Amager Bakke, operated by Amager
Resource Center (ARC), in Copenhagen, Denmark. Amager Bakke is a
WtE facility that annually emits 600,000 tonnes of CO2. It incinerates
non-recyclable MSW collected from the neighbouring communities and
industries. The waste comprises biogenic (66%) and non-biogenic waste
(34%) and is processed for co-generation of district heating and elec-
tricity. The experiments were conducted on the flue gas emitted by
the WtE facility using 30 wt% MEA as the solvent. The results of the
interheater configuration will be compared to the base case results. The
base case results have been presented in detail in a previous work [38].
2. Description
DTU, in association with Pentair Union Engineering A/S, designed
and constructed a Mobile Test Unit (MTU) for pilot-scale CO2 capture.
The pilot plant can be transported to multiple industrial facilities for
conducting tests. The plant can process 150 Nm3/h of flue gas and has
a reboiler capacity of 40 kW. A 3D illustration of the pilot plant and a
picture of the pilot plant at the WtE facility are presented in Figs. 1(a)
and 1(b), respectively.
Fig. 1(a) shows that the pilot plant has four columns and two
skids. The absorber and desorber are located on the skid to the right.
The wash column and an additional column for advanced process
configurations are on the skid to the left. Process equipment, such
as pumps, heat exchangers, flash vessel, filters, knockout drums, and
electrical cabinets, are in the bottom of the two skids. A detailed
description of the pilot plant and the equipment is presented in an
earlier publication [38]. The pilot plant can be configured in several
ways to optimise its performance. The process flow diagram (PFD) for
the interheater configuration is presented in Fig. 2.
Fig. 2 exhibits the PFD of the pilot plant for the interheater con-
figuration. The PFD of the base case configuration is detailed in a
previous work [38]. In the base case, the flue gas is scrubbed with
the solvent (30 wt% MEA) in the absorber. The solvent captures CO2,
resulting in a CO2-depleted flue gas that passes through a wash tower
to eliminate any residual amine and maintain a water balance. The
CO2-rich solution, known as the ‘‘rich solvent’’, is discharged from the
bottom of the absorber and is then pumped through the main heat
exchanger (main HE) before entering the stripper at a height of 10.4 m
(referred to as bed E in Fig. 2). The stripper operates at a pressure
range of 0.85 to 0.89 barg, with the reboiler maintaining a temperature
between 117 oC and 120 oC. Within the stripper, the rich solvent is
subjected to a stripping process, separating the CO2 from the solvent.
The resulting CO2-lean solvent, known as the ‘‘lean solvent’’, exits the
stripper and undergoes further energy recovery by passing through a
flash vessel and the main HE.
At the bottom of the stripper is a reboiler, which functions in
conjunction with a steam generator with a capacity of 40 kW. The

2.1. Interheater design and operation
A 3D illustration highlighting the interheater section is presented
in Fig. 3(a). As shown in Fig. 3(a), the interheater is in the first skid,
external to the stripper column. Withdrawal and feed pipes from the
stripper on the second skid are connected to the interheater section in
the first skid. The original 3D illustration has been edited to point to
the interheater section.
The PFD of the interheater section is presented in Fig. 3(b). As
shown in Fig. 3(b), the stream withdrawn from the stripper is pumped
through a plate-heat exchanger known as the interheater and fed to
the stripper. On the hot side, the interheater is connected to a steam
generator. The steam generator consists of an electrical heating element
submerged in demineralised water. Electrical duty is provided as an
input to the steam generator. The heating element produces steam
from the water, which rises into the interheater. The steam acts as
the hot fluid to heat the withdrawn stream. The condensed steam
flows back into the steam generator. This is the functioning of the
interheater section. The interheater and its steam generator are sized
similarly to the reboiler and its steam generator. Therefore, the capacity
of the interheater’s steam generator is 40 kW and the electrical input
given to the steam generator is considered the interheater duty in the
subsequent sections.
2.1.1. Operation of the interheater
During the start-up of the interheater section, a solvent flow is
first established by switching on the interheater pump. The flow rate
through the interheater is maintained by a pressure or flow PI controller
(PC or FC, respectively). The pressure PC manipulates the flow rate
to maintain a desired suction pressure (PT1). The FC maintains a
desired flow rate (FT1), which results in the manipulation of suction
and discharge pressures (PT1 and PT2, respectively). The PI controller
giving the lowest opening signal to the motorised valve is automatically
selected during operation. The pressure PI controller ensures that the
suction pressure does not get too low, which would empty the suction
pipe and damage the pump (as they should not run without liquids
in the pipe). The flow PI controller ensures that the flow rate through
the interheater is not disturbed. However, using a flow controller could
risk emptying the suction pipe. This is why a pressure controller is also
provided. Recycling around the interheater is also provided to ensure
the pump does not run empty. The recycle flow is controlled by a needle
valve (V1). During the start-up, the flow rate is established by manip-
ulating the controllers and by manually changing the position of the
needle valve V1. A steady flow from the stripper to the interheater was
first established before electrical duty is provided to the interheater.
In the interheater configuration, while the gas flow operation is
the same as in the base case, a difference in the stripper operation
is prominent as an additional heat exchanger provides energy to the
middle of the stripper at a height of 6.9 m. The interheater is a plate
heat exchanger located external to the stripper. A solvent stream is
withdrawn from the liquid collector above packed bed D in the stripper.
This stream is pumped through the interheater and fed to the liquid
distributor above packed bed D in the stripper. The liquid distributor
is located below the liquid collector above every packed bed in the
stripper.
A steam generator provides steam to the interheater on the hot
side. The steam generator’s duty is provided as an input. The steam
generator integrated with the interheater has the same design as that
used for the reboiler. The design of the reboiler’s steam generator was
duplicated to avoid issues with permits. Both the steam generators have
a capacity of 40 kW. The input to the reboiler and the interheater were
provided as electrical duties. The hot rich solvent exiting the main
heat exchanger (main HE) is fed to the stripper above packed bed E
at an accumulated packing height of 10.4 m. Three packing stages (E,
D, and C) are available for stripping. Packing bed C is right above
the reboiler section. The temperature and the driving forces are the
highest at the stripper bottom. The temperature usually decreases from
the bottom to the top of the column, and a mass transfer pinch is
observed at the top [28]. Therefore, it is ideal for the stripper to have
high temperatures in the middle and bottom sections of the column to
maintain a consistent driving force. At the top, it is better to have low
temperatures to reduce the condenser duty. Low temperatures at the
top help condense the steam and reduce latent heat loss through steam
escape. Therefore, it is ideal to provide interheating at the middle of the
column to maintain temperatures close to the reboiler and compensate
for heat loss. In the current setup, the interheated stream has a packing
of 6.9 m below it and 7 m above it. However, the top 3.5 m of the
packing bed (F) is unused for stripping to maintain consistency with
the base case. The details about the relevant equipment are provided
in the Supplementary information.
Ideally, the semi-lean solvent could be interheated by the hot flue
gas before it is cooled and sent to the absorber [27], the reboiler con-
densate [11,16], the hot lean solvent exiting the stripper bottom [11,
16,28], or a combination [9,11]. As shown in Fig. 2, a steam generator
provides the hot fluid to the interheater in the current setup. The influ-
ence of using various heat sources mentioned above can be simulated
by varying the reboiler and interheater duties.
3. Methodology
The pilot plant operation in base case configuration has been dis-
cussed in detail previously [38]. For the most part, the interheater
configuration is similar to the base case. The additional component
is the operation of the interheating section of the pilot plant. The
interheater is located external to the stripper. Therefore, a stream of
the solvent was withdrawn from the liquid collector above the packed
bed D, pumped through the interheater and fed to the liquid distributor
above the packed bed D in the stripper. Base case testing of the pilot
has shown the presence of a pinch region in packed beds D and
C [38]. As bed B is not used for stripping, packed bed C would be the
topmost section where the steam and the solvent are in contact with
each other before the steam-CO2 mixture exits the stripper and enters
the condenser. Therefore, implementing interheating at bed C would
cause additional steam losses. Based on this rationale, interheating was
implemented at bed D.
The flow through the interheater is controlled by a Proportional–
Integral controller (PI controller). The duty provided to the interheater
steam generator was varied from 0 to 5 kW. This range was selected
to avoid operational difficulties in the interheater, which were more
prominent at higher duties.The reboiler duty was altered from 12 to
22 kW. The experiments were conducted by fixing the interheater duty
and varying the reboiler duty in steps, as presented in Fig. 4(a).
Fig. 4(a) shows that multiple steady states could be obtained by
changing the reboiler duty in steps while keeping the other operating
parameters at reasonably constant values. The rich solvent flow rate
is maintained around 268 ± 8 kg/h. The reboiler duty was stepped
from 15 kW to 20 kW in steps of 1 kW. The heat loss in the stripper
and the reboiler was between 2 to 3 kW. As previously mentioned, the
interheater was located outside the stripper. Therefore, around 0.9 kW
of heat loss to the environment was incurred as the interheated solvent
exited the interheater and entered the stripper.

3.1. Steady state determination
As previously mentioned, the solvent flow rate maintained was
around 268 kg/h. During the start-up of the pilot plant, approximately
300 kg of 30 wt% MEA was added to the pilot, implying that it would
take almost an hour for the solvent to circulate through the pilot once.
The solvent was allowed to circulate two to three times in a stable
condition for obtaining a steady state. Therefore, each step change
was maintained for 2.5 to 3 h and the plant reached a steady state
within 2 h. Every steady state was held for 30 min to 1 hour. The steady-
state operation of the pilot was mainly determined by the inlet and
outlet CO2 gas concentrations. The flue gas flow rate was maintained
throughout the operation with no disturbances. Inlet flue gas CO2
concentration is one of the major sources of disturbance in the pilot,
and the outlet CO2 concentration is one of the main consequences of
the pilot’s operation. Therefore, these two parameters were observed
when determining a steady state of pilot operation.
Fig. 4(b) shows the inlet and outlet CO2 concentrations. The red
region is the period where the reboiler duty was stepped up. While the
inlet CO2 concentration is stable, the outlet concentration takes about
an hour to reach a steady value with a fluctuation within ±10%. The
operation was maintained in this steady state (green region) for about
45 min before changing the reboiler duty. Extensive details regarding
the calibration of equipment such as gas flow meters and CO2 probes
can be found in a previous publication [38].
3.2. Experimental campaigns
The most important operating parameters of the pilot plant for all
the experiments (19 steady states in total) are presented in Table 1.
The base case experiments were initially performed in the experi-
mental campaigns. Several operating parameters, such as the solvent
flow rate, gas flow rate, stripper pressure, and reboiler duties, were
tested. The results for these campaigns have been discussed in detail
in a previous publication [38]. Based on the operational experience
gained with the base case configuration, the operating parameters
for the interheating configuration have been chosen. The base case
operation was stable for a solvent flow range of 250 to 300 kg/h, with a
gas flow of 100 kg/h and stripper pressure of 0.8 to 0.9 barg. The lean
temperature of 40 oC is a standard in CO2 capture plants. Therefore,
these conditions were used as a basis for developing the experimental
campaigns for the interheater configuration. The range of the inter-
heater duty was maintained between 2 and 5 kW to avoid operational
difficulties. Furthermore, the heat loss in the pilot is approximately
2.91 kW (Section 3.3.1). One of the purposes of the interheater is to
minimise the heat loss in the pilot. Therefore, the range selected is
appropriate for the estimated heat loss. Nevertheless, minor changes in
the design of the interheating section will enable the plant to explore
a wider range of interheater duties. However, these changes could not
be implemented in the current work due to limited time availability.
Table 1 depicts some of the most critical operating parameters of
the pilot for the base case and interheater configurations. It can be
observed that most of the parameters, such as solvent and gas flow
rates, solvent and gas inlet temperatures, and stripper bottom pressure,
were maintained consistently for both cases. This was deliberately done
to ensure that the changes observed in the interheating configuration
are not caused by variations in the main operating parameters. The
reboiler and interheater duties were adjusted to test the pilot’s perfor-
mance. Due to operational difficulties (expanded in the Supplementary
information), the interheating experiments were conducted as night
runs with the remote pilot operation. Consequently, liquid samples
could not be collected for these experiments to determine the rich
and lean loadings. Therefore, the loadings of the configuration are not
discussed in the interpretation of the experimental results.
The objectives of the study are to understand the influence of
stripper interheating on the performance of the pilot. In the range of
operations chosen for the campaigns, it would be clear whether inter-
heating improves the temperature profile in the stripper by reducing
heat loss. An improvement in the temperature profile is expected to
reduce the energy consumption. Furthermore, the impact of interheat-
ing on the capture efficiency of the process will also be determined.
A reduction in energy consumption and an improvement in capture
efficiency would be desirable outcomes. Parameters such as gas flow
rates and concentrations, temperatures along the length of the stripper,
reboiler and interheater duties, CO2 capture flow rates are measured
for analysing and interpreting the results. The performance of the
interheating configuration compared to the base case will be presented
in detail in the subsequent sections.

4. Results and discussion
In the current work, experiments were conducted to understand the
influence of interheating the solvent in the stripper. A steam generator
provides the hot fluid to the interheater (Fig. 2). The steam produced by
the generator was used as the heat source to heat the semi-lean solvent
extracted from the middle of the stripper. The duty was provided to the
steam generator by the operator. The interheater was located outside of
the stripper. The steam (from the steam generator) is a simulated case
for heat from hot flue gas, high-temperature heat pump installation,
and other heat integration methods.
Generally, experimental results of CO2 capture are analysed at a
capture efficiency of 90%, especially when comparing the performance
of an advanced configuration against the base case. However, it is not
necessary that the capture efficiency is 90% to be able to compare the
performances. Two or more configurations can be compared against
each other by considering different criteria. In the current work, strip-
per interheating and base case configurations are compared based on
the following criteria:
• At a constant energy provided to the reboiler
• At a constant total energy provided to the stripper
When constant energy is provided to the reboiler for both config-
urations (base case and stripper interheating), interheating is done in
addition to reboiling. On the other hand, when constant total energy
is provided to the stripper for both configurations, interheating is done
as a replacement to reboiling. This distinction is presented in Fig. 5.
Fig. 5a shows that both the base case and stripper interheating
configurations have the same reboiler duty, and the energy provided to
the interheater is an addition. Fig. 5b shows that both configurations
have the same total duty; therefore, interheating replaces reboiling.
The subsequent sections discuss the results considering the scenarios
mentioned above.

4.1.2. Characteristic stripper temperature profiles
Before discussing the influence of interheating on the stripper tem-
perature profiles, the characteristic curves for low, intermediate, and
high stripping intensities are presented in Fig. 9. The characteristic
curves do not have any values on the X and Y axes, as they are meant
to qualitatively show the temperature profiles developed in a stripper
column independent of the scale and operating pressure.
Fig. 9 shows a clear distinction between the stripper temperature
profiles at low, intermediate, and high Strintreb values. At low Strintreb,
a significant temperature difference is usually observed at the strip-
per bottom, indicating condensation of steam and desorption of CO2.
However, the middle and top sections of the stripper have similar
temperatures, indicating a pinch region. A pinch region indicates the
absence of steam condensation. Condensing steam provides the energy
required for desorption of CO2 and sensible heating of the solvent. In
the pinch region, there is no condensation of steam and, therefore,
no desorption of CO2. Additionally, the driving force for mass transfer
from the liquid phase to the gas phase is minimised in the pinch region.
Consequently, the packing in the stripper is not efficiently utilised.
A linear temperature profile is expected in the intermediate Strintreb.
This can also be referred to as the optimal temperature profile. The
temperature along the stripper is constantly changing. Therefore, a
consistent driving force is available throughout the stripper, leading to
better efficiency of the packing and higher desorption of CO2. At high
Strintreb, the temperature profile is a reversed case of the low Strintreb.
At high stripping intensities, the driving force is lower at the bottom
and middle sections of the stripper. However, a large 𝛥T at the stripper
top exits. Therefore, most steam condensation occurs at the top, which
provides energy for CO2 desorption, while the bottom two-thirds are
not utilised efficiently.
To optimise the stripper operation, it is desirable to operate it in
the intermediate Strintreb range or modify the configuration to ensure
a linear temperature profile in the stripper so that pinch regions are
eliminated. Furthermore, it is desirable to have low temperatures at the
stripper top to minimise the overhead steam loss and condenser duty.

4.2.4. Energy lost in condenser vs gained by IH
The reboiler present at the bottom of the stripper boils the solvent
to form steam, which moves up the column. The energy available in
the steam is used for sensible heating of the solvent and desorption
Fig. 22. Influence of interheating on the condenser duty at various Strinttotal and SIreb
values.
of CO2. The unused steam exits the stripper top with the stripped
CO2 and passes through the condenser. The steam is condensed in the
condenser and is added to the solvent to maintain the water balance.
The temperature of the steam-CO2 mixture exiting the stripper top is
dictated by the hot rich inlet temperature. The condenser duty (Fig. 22)
indicates a parasitic energy loss in the stripper.
From Fig. 22, it can be seen that the condenser duty increases with
Strinttotal. As Strinttotal increases, the extent of reboiling also increases,
leading to higher steam production and, therefore, a higher condenser
duty. However, at first glance, the impact of SIreb on the condenser duty
is unclear. Upon closer inspection, it can be observed that the condenser
duty slightly decreases as the extent of interheating increases. At an
Strinttotal in the range of 180 to 190 kJ/kgtotal, the condenser duty
reduces from 3.9 kW to 3.3 kW as the SIreb decreases from 100%
to 70%. Similarly at Strinttotal of 210 kJ/kgtotal, the condenser duty
reduces from 5.1 kW to 4.7 kW as the SIreb changes from 100% to 90%.
The reduced condenser duty is a consequence of the lowered reboiling
of the solvent. It also indicates that interheating the solvent does not
contribute to additional parasitic steam loss from the stripper.
The energy lost as condenser duty could be used as a source of heat
integration in the capture plant. As detailed in a previous work [10],
bypassing a fraction of the cold rich solvent exiting the absorber
bottom from the main HE and feeding it to the stripper top helps in
reducing the saturation temperature of the exiting steam-CO2 mixture.
Therefore, the condenser duty can be reduced, and the temperature
profile in the stripper can be improved. However, it was also noted that
the lean cooling duty increases by implementing the split flow config-
uration [10]. Another way of reducing the condenser duty could be
through a heat pump installation where the energy from the condenser
is displaced to the middle of the stripper through an interheater.
Alternatively, split flow configuration and interheating could be
implemented in conjugation. The heat source for interheating could
then be obtained from alternative sources such as the hot flue gas or
the reboiler condensate. Implementing a split flow configuration will
aid in reducing the parasitic steam loss from the stripper top, and
interheating the stripper middle will help compensate for the heat loss
from the stripper and improve the temperature profile. A combination
of these two configurations could further reduce the SRD of the system
compared to what was experimentally demonstrated for the two cases
individually. Liu and Chen [43] simulated this combination of split flow
configuration with an interheated stripper. A split fraction of 11% of
the cold rich solvent was bypassed through the main HE and fed to
the stripper top to recover heat from the overhead steam. A stream

5. Conclusion
The current work discusses the optimisation of a CO2 capture pilot
plant by implementing interheating in the stripper. The experiments
were conducted at a WtE facility, utilising 30 wt% MEA as the solvent.
This work aims at bridging the knowledge gap existing due to the lack
of experimental studies on pilot scale demonstration of stripper inter-
heating, which has been shown as a promising advanced configuration
by several simulation studies.
The experiments were done by withdrawing a stream of semi-lean
solvent from the stripper middle, heating it, and feeding it to the
stripper at the same height. The energy to the interheater was provided
by a steam generator with an adjustable duty, which the operator gave
as input. The reboiler and interheater duties were varied to analyse
the pilot’s performance regarding SRD, capture efficiency, stripper
temperature profile, and condenser duty. The results were analysed as
simulations of different scenarios where interheating was considered
an addition or replacement to reboiling at the stripper bottom. Even
though an additional duty was spent on interheating in the current
work, it has been considered a simulation of energy obtained through
heat integration with the WtE facility, heat pumps, or hot lean solvent.
Adding interheating to reboiling improved the pilot’s performance
in terms of SRD and 𝜖. The SRD was reduced in the range of 2% to
11% by adding interheating to the base cases. Additionally, the capture
efficiency was improved, possibly due to better cyclic capacity of the
solvent. The CO2 stripped was also enhanced by adding interheating.
The condenser duty is impacted by the addition of interheating depend-
ing on the value of Strintreb. At Strintreb < 210 kJ/kgtotal, the condenser
duty is increased by at least 2% when interheating is added to the base
case. At Strintreb in the range of 210 to 240 kJ/kgtotal, the condenser
duty was similar for both configurations.
Replacing reboiling with interheating was also depicted to optimise
the pilot’s performance, depending on the operation parameters. Re-
placing 10% of reboiling with interheating reduced the SRD by at least
7%. Additionally, the capture efficiency is not significantly impacted.
Further replacement of 28% reboiling with interheating can reduce
SRD to as low as 2.73 GJ/tonne CO2 with a capture efficiency of 80%.
Operating with the right reboiling and interheating could potentially
result in a capture efficiency of 90% and SRD of 2.25 GJ/tonne CO2.
Replacing reboiling with interheating was also shown to improve the
stripper temperature profile at low and high Strinttotal values. The
condenser duty can be reduced by replacing reboiling at high Strinttotal
(∼240 kJ/kgtotal).
Stripper interheating in this work has thus been shown to reduce
the SRD while maintaining desirable capture efficiencies. Integration
within the pilot could be even more advantageous. Using a hot lean
solvent as the heat source for interheating displaces a fraction of
the energy recovery from the main HE and moves it to the strip-
per middle. Consequently, the hot rich solvent entering the stripper
would be at temperatures lower than 100oC depending on the extent
of energy recovery displacement. Reducing the hot rich temperature
will consequently lower the condenser duty while interheating the
stripper middle will ensure high temperatures in the lower two-thirds
of the stripper. Furthermore, a combination of split flow configuration
with interheating using hot lean solvent could optimise the pilot’s
performance in terms of SRD, capture efficiency, and total cooling duty.
Application of stripper interheating requires an additional heat
exchanger, contributing to the CAPEX costs. Therefore, the data from
the pilot could be utilised to conduct a cost–benefit analysis before
implementing the configuration at a full-scale plant. Furthermore, the
PID design of the interheater section could be improved to obtain
a steady state operation easily without significant disruptions to the
process. Additionally, the influence of stripper interheating on solvent
degradation needs to be evaluated.
There is a considerable knowledge gap in the experimental demon-
stration of advanced configurations, such as stripper interheating,
which have been shown to reduce energy consumption in simulation
studies. These simulation studies, however, lack experimental data for
the configuration to validate their results. Therefore, this work seeks to
close the knowledge gap and provide an in-depth experimental under-
standing of the configuration. Based on this pilot scale work, simulation
models developed for 30 wt% MEA can be validated, especially for
the interheating configuration. These experiments are also valuable in
developing new models, FEED studies and Techno-economic analysis.
With the growing implementation of full-scale CO2 capture in several
industries worldwide, pilot-scale studies are extremely valuable sources
of theoretical and operational knowledge.
