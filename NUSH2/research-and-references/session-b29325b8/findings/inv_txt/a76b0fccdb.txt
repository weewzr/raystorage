A B S T R A C T
Post-combustion carbon capture is a well-established technology to limit CO2 from industrial emissions.
However, challenges such as high energy requirements for solvent regeneration persist. Research is still being
conducted to improve the energy efficiency of the process. This study aims to present the results of process
intensification in a pilot-scale CO2 capture plant. The pilot-scale investigations were carried out at Amager
Bakke, a Waste-to-Energy facility in Copenhagen, Denmark. The tests were conducted by implementing the
split flow configuration as a method of process intensification. The CO2 rich stream was split before flowing
through the rich/lean heat exchanger. One of the split streams was fed to the stripper top, and the other
was sent through the heat exchanger and then fed to the stripper. The cold split stream at the stripper top is
heated by the overhead vapours which would otherwise escape the stripper. The current work discusses the
split flow configuration results of the pilot plant by employing 30 wt% MEA as the solvent and compares it to
the base-case performance of the pilot. Experiments were conducted by splitting 22% of the rich solvent and
feeding it to the stripper top. The performance of the split flow configuration was analysed at different reboiler
duties. A minimum specific reboiler duty of 3.45 GJ/tonne CO2 is obtained for the split flow configuration,
which is less than the base case by 7.8%. Additionally, a reduction in the condenser duty by at least 68% was
achieved by the implementation of the split flow configuration.
1. Introduction
Post-combustion capture (PCC) is a critical technology to reduce the
CO2 emissions into the atmosphere from industrial operations. Amine-
based CO2 capture is a well-established and mature technology. It can
be retrofitted to the existing power plants [1,2]. Implementing carbon
capture and storage for Waste-to-Energy (WtE) plants can make waste
an attractive source of zero or negative emissions energy, depending
on the fraction of biogenic waste [3]. There are three incineration
facilities integrated with CO2 capture currently. These are located in
the Netherlands (AVR and Twence plants) and Japan (Saga City) [3–
5]. In Saga City, Japan, 10 tonnes of CO2 is captured per day utilising
aqueous amine solvent [6,7]. The captured CO2 is utilised for algae
and crop cultivation [6]. At AVR’s WtE, 60,000 tonnes of captured
CO2 per year is supplied to the horticulture sector in the region [8].

power production in a coal-fired power plant as parasitic load [17].
Innovative process configurations can be implemented to reduce energy
consumption for solvent regeneration [18]. Split flow configuration is
a stipulated process intensification in which a fraction of the CO2 rich
solvent stream exiting the absorber bottom bypasses the main heat
exchanger (main HE) and enters the stripper top. In the main HE,
the cold rich solvent is heated by the hot lean solvent, whereas the
split flow fed to the stripper top is heated internally by the overhead
saturated steam, condensing the steam, which would otherwise be lost
from the system. The utilisation of the overhead steam to heat a portion
of the cold rich solvent reduces the reboiler duty [18–32].
Additionally, the cooling duty of the overhead condenser is also
reduced due to the lower mass flow of steam leaving the stripper. A
slightly different version of the usage of the overhead vapours has also
been patented [33]. A few modelling studies of the split flow configura-
tion have been conducted to analyse the energy reductions obtained for
solvent regeneration [34–41]. Furthermore, a few different variations
of the split flow configuration have been simulated [13,18,41–47].
However, minimal pilot data is available on implementing a split
flow configuration. The split flow configuration was tested at the
Tarong pilot plant using MEA as the solvent. A 7% reduction in the
regeneration energy was observed, along with a 60% reduction in
the cooling water requirement in the condenser. The influence of the
split flow configuration was stated to be strongly dependent on the
performance of the main HE [48]. Another pilot-scale testing of the
split flow configuration was conducted at the Jaworzno II Power plant,
which showed a reduction in the reboiler duty by 5% [49]. It is crucial
to conduct pilot-scale testing of such process intensification methods to
understand the performance of a CO2 capture plant and to validate and
develop better models.

2. Description
DTU, in partnership with Pentair Union Engineering, has developed
and built a Mobile Test Unit (MTU) for conducting pilot-scale CO2
capture. The pilot can process 150 Nm3/h of flue gas. A 3D illustration
of the pilot and a picture of the pilot at the WtE facility are presented
in Figs. 1(a) and 1(b), respectively.
Fig. 1(a) exhibits that the pilot has four columns: absorber, desorber,
wash column, and an additional column for process intensification. The
taller columns at the back are used as absorber and desorber, whereas
one of the shorter ones in the front is used as the wash column. Process
equipment, such as filters, pumps, heat exchangers, and flow meters,
are in the two skids below the columns. A detailed description of the
pilot and the process has been given in a previous work [50]. The
results of the split flow configuration are presented and discussed in
the current work. The Process Flow Diagram (PFD) of the split flow
configuration is presented in Fig. 2.
Fig. 2 represents the PFD of the pilot for the split flow configuration.
The PFD of the process for the base case configuration and the control
structure have been described in detail in the previous work [50].

In the base case configuration, the flue gas is contacted with 30 wt%
MEA in the absorber. During the start-up of the pilot plant, the liquid
circulation through the absorber and the stripper is first established.
This is done either manually or in auto-mode. A control structure was
designed and implemented in the Programmable Logic Controller (PLC)
for the pilot plant. When the operator activates the auto-mode, the
rich pump starts, followed by the lean pump with a few minutes of
delay. The flows were expected to stabilise in approximately 10 min.
