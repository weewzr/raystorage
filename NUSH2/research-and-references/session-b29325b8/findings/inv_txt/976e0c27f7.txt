conducted at Amager Bakke, a Waste-to-Energy facility in Copenhagen, Denmark. The pilot operation is fully
automated, and the process control structure helps achieve multiple steady states at regular intervals. In
addition, the pilot is capable of several configurations which can assist in optimising the energy required for

30 wt% MEA as the solvent. Experiments were conducted to analyse the influence of reboiler duty, solvent

in industries that are difficult to decarbonise, such as cement and
steel production [4]. Post-combustion capture (PCC) is a promising
technology for reducing greenhouse gas emissions from large station-
ary industrial processes, such as heat and power generation [5]. PCC
involves the treatment of industrial gases consisting of low CO2 par-
tial pressures in large streams of non-condensable gases. The process
technology for acid gas removal by chemical absorption from natural
gas was developed in the 1920s [6]. Amine-based PCC offers various
advantages: it has high capture efficiency and selectivity, the tech-
nology is commercially available, and it can be retrofitted to existing
power plants [7–9]. Nevertheless, the technology has some limitations,
such as the high energy consumption required for the regeneration
process, the corrosive nature of the amine solvents, and the potential
for solvent degradation due to contaminants in the flue gas [8,10].
30 wt% Monoethanolamine (MEA), which is the most widely used
solvent, requires regeneration energy of 3.6–3.8 GJ/tonne CO2 [10,11]
which corresponds to an 11%–15% drop in the thermal efficiency of
the power plant including CO2 compression [12,13]. Regeneration of
MEA contributes to 70%–80% of the operating cost [14]. The energy
efficiency of the process could be improved by configuring the pilot for
optimisation [15], heat integration with the power plant, development
of new solvents [11,13,16–18], or a combination.
Pilot-scale testing of CO2 capture is essential for developing reliable
and cost-effective technologies. Pilot testing helps identify potential
problems and evaluate the performance of the capture process. Several
pilot-scale tests have been conducted at coal-fired power plants [10,19–
22] and natural gas-fired power plants [13,23–26] employing MEA as
the solvent. All of these studies were conducted without employing any
optimisation of a general absorption–desorption process for CO2 cap-
ture, which is essential for improving energy efficiency and reducing
the cost of the capture process. Literature on pilot-scale testing of CO2
capture employing various flue gas sources is presented in Table 1.

In addition to fossil fuel-based power plants, Waste-to-Energy (WtE)
facilities also produce CO2 emissions. These facilities use thermal treat-
ment of waste to produce energy. They are becoming increasingly
popular as an alternative to landfilling due to their environmental ben-
efits and use of renewable energy sources [41–44]. Shell’s CANSOLV
capture technology and solvent DC-103 pilot tests were conducted for
2000 operational hours at the Forum Oslo Varme WtE plant in Oslo,
Norway. However, limited information is available about the energy
consumed for solvent regeneration [45]. The Saga waste incineration
plant in Saga City, Japan has a CO2 capture plant that can capture
10 tonnes of CO2/day and use it for algae cultivation [46]. However,
information about the capture technology’s operation and performance
is scarce. The AVR Duiven WtE facility has implemented a commercial-
scale CO2 capture system with the ability to capture 100,000 tonnes
of CO2/yr [47]. A commercial-scale CO2 capture system is expected
to be operational at the Twence WtE facility in the Netherlands by
the end of 2023 [48]. These are the current full-scale and pilot-scale
activities related to CO2 capture in WtE facilities. WtE plants inte-
grated with CO2 capture are part of BECCS and contribute to negative
emissions [49]. Therefore, it is critical to conduct more pilot-scale
tests to develop reliable and cost-effective CO2 capture technologies for
full-scale implementation.
The current work aims to present baseline results from a pilot-scale
CO2 capture plant tested at Amager Bakke, Copenhagen, Denmark,
which is a WtE facility. The baseline experiments employed a 30 wt%
MEA solution, which will be the basis for comparing the performance
of advanced process configurations and new solvents. The Amager
Bakke WtE facility, operated by Amager Resource Center (ARC), is
expected to emit 600,000 tonnes of CO2 annually. Therefore, CCUS in-
tegration is essential to achieve net-zero emissions. To gain knowledge
and experience before constructing a full-scale facility, the Technical
University of Denmark (DTU) conducted pilot-scale tests on-site from
September to December 2021. In addition to the base case experiments,
the tests included several process configurations such as rich recycle,
split feed, inter-cooled absorber, interheated stripper, Lean Vapour
Recompression (LVC), and flexible operation of the pilot with 30 wt%
MEA. Several new solvent formulations have also been tested with
the above-mentioned process configurations. New solvent formulations
and advanced process configurations have the potential to reduce the
specific reboiler duty (SRD) considerably. To determine the extent of
optimisation obtained, it is essential to establish the operation of the
pilot in a base case mode which serves as the standard for comparison.
The current article details the performance of the pilot plant using
30 wt% MEA operated in a base case configuration. In addition, a
parametric analysis was conducted to better understand the perfor-
mance of the pilot at varying operating conditions. Literature available
on CO2 capture typically provides a very superficial analysis of the
experimental data, often limiting the discussion around the SRD. It is
crucial to obtain a thorough understanding of the several aspects of
the pilot functionality for efficient full-scale implementation of such a
capture plant. This work gives an in-depth insight into various aspects
of the pilot plant which are most often overlooked.
2. Description of the Mobile Test Unit
DTU, in collaboration with Pentair, has designed and constructed
a Mobile Test Unit (MTU) for pilot-scale CO2 capture. The pilot is de-
signed to test using aqueous MEA or similar solvents to clean 150 Nm3/h
flue gas. A 3D drawing of the pilot plant is presented in Fig. 1(a). In
addition, a picture of the pilot is presented in Fig. 1(b).
Fig. 1(a) shows that the pilot consists of four columns: absorber,
desorber, wash tower, and an additional column for advanced con-
figurations. It has two skids within which several process equipment,
such as filters, heat exchangers, pumps, flow meters, etc., are placed.
In addition to flexibility in terms of solvent use, the MTU allows the
testing of several advanced configurations which enhance absorption,
allow for heat integration, and optimise energy consumption for solvent
regeneration. A simplified Process Flow Diagram (PFD) of the pilot is
provided in Fig. 2. However, the pilot plant can have several process
configurations not presented in the PFD.
The gas and solvent flow through the system are configured for
a base case operation. The processes involved are briefed in the sub-
sequent sections. A detailed description of the pilot is available in
Appendix A.

through a knock-out drum. The knock-out drum acts as a gas–water
