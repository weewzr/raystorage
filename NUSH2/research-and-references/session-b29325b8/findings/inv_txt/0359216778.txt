A pilot absorber column for CO2 recovery from ﬂue gases was constructed and tested with aqueous 30 wt%
monoethanolamine (MEA), a primary amine, as capture solvent. The pilot plant data were compared with
a mathematical rate based packed-column model. The simulation results compared well with the pilot
plant data.
The packed height of the column can be varied from 1.6 to 8.2 m by means of ﬁve different liquid inlets.
The column has an inner diameter of 100 mm and is packed with structured Mellapak 250Y packing.
Counter-current ﬂow is used.
The pilot plant performance was investigated by changing three parameters: the absorption height,
liquid ﬂow rate, and the loading of lean MEA. This was done using a synthetic ﬂue gas consisting of 10%
CO2 with a ﬂow rate of approximately 33 m3/h at ambient temperature and atmospheric pressure. 23
runs were performed.
It was observed that while CO2 recovery increases with an increase in ﬂow rate of absorbent and
absorption height, it decreases as the lean CO2-loading of the absorbent increases. In addition it has
been possible to obtain temperature bulges in the bottom part of the absorber by the applied operation
conditions. Bulges are observed at liquid ﬂows around 4.2 L/min and below. The results showed that is
was possible to achieve 80% recovery with 3.3 m absorption height and a liquid ﬂow of 2.1 L/min. The
simulations show good agreement with the experimental values, although slight deviations arise as the
CO2-loading increases and the temperature bulge becomes more distinct.

1. Introduction
Carbon dioxide is emitted from large sources of fossil fuel com-
bustion, particularly power plants. It is one of the key man-made
greenhouse gases that must be minimized in order to avoid cata-
strophic climate change. The capturing of trillions tons of CO2 is a
daunting task, but is a challenge that has to be overcome success-
fully if disaster is to be averted (Schrag, 2007).
The process for post combustion capture consists of four stages
as sketched in Fig. 1. First, CO2 is removed from the ﬂue gas by
absorption in a packed absorber column. Then ‘rich’ solvent con-
taining CO2 is led to a desorber column and heated in a reboiler to
release the CO2. Next, CO2 is compressed and transported to a geo-
logical storage site or injected into an oil and gas reservoir. Finally
the regenerated ‘lean’ solvent is recycled to the absorber.
The supply of heat for solvent regeneration is by far the most
expensive aspect of the post combustion capture process. The cost

A number of studies have been carried out in order to inves-
tigate CO2 capture in pilot plants using MEA. Most of these have
appeared since 2006, with the exception of a few earlier studies. The
older work conducted at University of Regina in Canada is described
by Tontiwachwuthikul et al. (1992), Aroonwilas et al. (1999), and
Aboundheir (2002) and more recently by Idem et al. (2006, 2009).
There is a growing activity on post combustion capture in Norway
evidence by the MEA related studies of Tobiesen et al. (2007) and
Hessen (2010). Work has been performed at University of Kaiser-
slautern (Notz et al., 2007) as well as several studies at University
of Texas, Austin (Dugas, 2006; Kvamsdal and Rochelle, 2008). A
number of larger industrial funded consortium projects have also
appeared (Hessen, 2010; Dugas et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2008).
While some studies have been performed concerning the mech-
anisms of CO2 capture from ﬂue gases using solvents, there is
limited pilot plant data for such systems, where enough informa-
tion has been reported to enable model development. Here we
report the construction and validation of a new pilot-scale facility,
which we hope will contribute to research in CO2 capture, as this

A vast number of both structured and random (dumped) packing
types are available from commercial suppliers. Different packing
types have been used in the above-mentioned works. Structured
packing was considered in Hessen (2010), including 250Y Mellapak,
whereas dumped packing was used by Lee et al. (2008), Dugas et al.
(2009), Kvamsdal and Rochelle (2008), and Tontiwachwuthikul
et al. (1992). Here the Koch-Glitsch IMTP 25, 40, 50 and ceramic
Berl saddles, 12.7 mm, were used. Some studies included the same
pilot facility but with different packing material. Aboundheir (2002)
tested the A4 structured packing and the two dumped packings,
IMTP 15 and Pall Ring-16mm. Dugas (2006) tested the structured
Flexipac 1Y and IMTP 40 dumped packing. Finally Aroonwilas
et al. (1999) tested the Gempak 4A structured and IMTP dumped
packings. Both packings were tested in three different sizes. They
found that the structured packings performed better than dumped

packings, because of the greater surface area per unit volume.
However there are some disadvantages, as the structured pack-
ings require excellent initial distribution to obtain the full beneﬁt.
Finally the cost of structured packings is also higher than dumped
packings (Wilson, 2004).
While packing suppliers should have a comprehensive under-
standing of the performance of their packing, far from all packings
have received a thorough and independent comparison in the open
literature. An interesting study could be performed across research
laboratories where different pilot plants independently compared
the same packings. On the other hand, having validated our pilot
plant here for a given packing and a “standard” solvent (MEA) we
have conﬁdence that the same plant could be used to evaluate both
different packings and different capture solvents.
Previous pilot scale studies (Tontiwachwuthikul et al., 1992;
Aroonwilas et al., 1999; Aboundheir, 2002; Idem et al., 2006, 2009;
Tobiesen et al., 2007; Hessen, 2010; Notz et al., 2007; Dugas,
2006; Kvamsdal and Rochelle, 2008; Dugas et al., 2009; Lee et al.,
2008) cover a range of operation conditions. Three input vari-
ables are interesting from a process point of view. These are the
solvent CO2 loading, solvent concentrations, and ﬂue gas com-
position. The lean loading covered is 0–0.35 and rich loading is
0.33–0.49 molCO2/molMEA. The concentration of MEA in the MEA-
H2O solvent varies from 18 to 54 wt%. Most of the experiments are
carried out using 30 wt% MEA concentration. The CO2 concentra-
tion in the ﬂue gas coming into the bottom of the absorber is in the
range of 4–20 vol%.
Several analytical methods have been used in the determination
of liquid CO2 content for loading evaluation. Of the studies above,
CO2 content was mainly found by a barium chloride method very
similar to the one outlined in Appendix A of this work. It is cheap but
time consuming. Other methods are the Total Organic Carbon (TOC)
analysis, used by Dugas (2006) and gas chromatography–mass
spectrometry (GC–MS) used by Idem et al. (2009). The beneﬁt of
this last method is that it is on-line and unmanned measurements
can be performed. This method was not pursued because of the high
cost involved. A titration method described by Horwitz (2006) may
also be used. We chose to proceed with the Barium Chloride method
since we had prior experience with it.
The absorber temperature proﬁle (temperature along the length
of the column) can be measured by means of appropriately placed
temperature probes. The reaction between CO2 and the solvent
is exothermic, resulting in a steep temperature increase at cer-
tain conditions. An observations by Kvamsdal and Rochelle (2008)
was that the location of the temperature bulge can move from the
bottom to the top of the column. The temperature bulge is quan-
tiﬁed by its magnitude and location in the column and is seen as
a convex curve on the temperature proﬁle. At a critical liquid to
gas (L/G)-ratio the temperature bulge will be in the middle of the

The aim of this work is to report results for a new pilot-scale
(10 m high, 10 cm internal diameter) absorber. Results are reported
for Mellapak 250Y structured packing and MEA solution as cap-
ture solvent. In addition to reporting this new data, which can be
used for model development and testing (here it is tested against
an existing published model (Gabrielsen et al., 2006; Faramarzi
et al., 2009)), a secondary aim is to validate the plant for a given
packing and solvent in order to proceed to other packings and sol-
vents with greater conﬁdence. The following operation conditions
were covered: A lean loading of 0.11–0.30 and a rich loading of
0.15–0.39 molCO2/molMEA. A 30 wt% aqueous MEA solution and a
synthetic ﬂue gas composition of 9.2–10.9 vol% CO2. A modiﬁed and
optimized barium chloride method was developed for the analysis
of the liquid phase for the determination of the total dissolved CO2
(see Appendix A). The plant was ﬁtted with two storage tanks with
a volume of 1 m3 each. Therefore it was possible to run a series of
experiments with the same lean loading and MEA concentration.
The basis of the different operation conditions are consequently
very similar and therefore comparable. The results of the various
experimental runs contain detailed information about the loading
and temperature proﬁles throughout the column. Additionally the
CO2 concentration in the gas phase was measured at both the gas
inlet and outlet of the column.
The data were also modelled using a previously developed
rate-based model (Gabrielsen et al., 2006; Faramarzi, 2010). While
further development of the model was not undertaken as part of
this work, the production of new pilot-plant data did provide fur-
ther validation of the model, as usable pilot plant data are not
plentiful.
2. Modelling CO2 absorption
The experimental pilot runs performed in this work were mod-
elled using a rate based column model originally developed by
Gabrielsen et al. (2006, 2007) and Gabrielsen (2009) and fur-
ther modiﬁed by Faramarzi et al. (2009, 2010) and Faramarzi
(2010). The main purpose of this work was the experimental
validation of the pilot scale absorber. More detail on the model
can be found in previous work (Gabrielsen et al., 2006, 2007;
Gabrielsen, 2009; Faramarzi et al., 2009, 2010; Faramarzi, 2010)
and only the basic elements are presented here. Previous works
have presented models for mass transfer coefﬁcient, mass trans-
fer area, hydraulics and physical properties. A comprehensive
comparison of the various components that make up a com-
plete rate-based model is considered beyond the scope of this
work. The model is mass transfer based and accounts for trans-
port properties, thermodynamics, phase equilibrium and reaction
kinetics. The thermodynamic model used is extended UNIQUAC
(Faramarzi, 2010). Extended UNIQUAC is used here to model
the thermodynamic properties required in the full rate-based
model, because it is the model where we have most experience
(Gabrielsen et al., 2006, 2007; Gabrielsen, 2009; Faramarzi et al.,
2009; Faramarzi, 2010). Any thermodynamic model for electrolytes
which is at a reasonable level of development (for example e-
NRTL) could have been used, although it was not the purpose of
this work to develop and present comparisons of thermodynamic
models.
The model is built up around eight differential equations
describing the mass and energy balances of the gas and liquid, to
which a number of equations are related in order to account for the
dependency of different parameters on temperature, concentra-
tion, pressure, etc. In the development of the model some general
assumptions were made in order to simplify the system sufﬁciently
for modelling:
1 The reaction rate is fast enough so that all the reaction will occur
in the liquid ﬁlm while the CO2 concentration in the bulk phase
is assumed to be in equilibrium.
2 Liquid side heat transfer resistance is small compared to the gas
phase, thus the interface temperature is the same as the bulk
temperature.
3 The liquid side mass-transfer resistance for the volatile solvent
is negligible.
4 The interfacial surface area is the same for heat and mass transfer.
5 The system is adiabatic.
