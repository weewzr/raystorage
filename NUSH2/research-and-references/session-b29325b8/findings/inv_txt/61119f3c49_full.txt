A B S T R A C T
The continuous exploration of new gas reserves along with the increase in the global power demand necessitates
the development of less demanding technologies, especially in terms of energy minimization. However, the
development of CO2-rich type of gas resources poses signiﬁcant challenges in the sour gas treatment industry due
to bulk CO2 contaminants and high overﬂowing pressure at the well- head. For the treatment of CO2 rich natural
gas, reliable data at high CO2 partial pressure condition is very crucial and the insight of the transient conditions
must be thoroughly studied to design a controlled process that can handle any disturbances that might arise in
the future. This paper reports the dynamic modelling and basic regulatory control studies of a high-pressure
absorption pilot plant, which is located at Universiti Teknologi PETRONAS (UTP), for CO2 removal using MEA
solvent. Steady state simulation has been demonstrated using Aspen Plus utilizing both equilibrium and rate
based approaches. Dynamic and control analysis is then carried out in Aspen Dynamic using a modiﬁed equi-
librium stage model.
1. Introduction
Natural gas (NG) is formed in the underground and can usually be
found in areas where both coal and oil are situated. Natural gas exists in
onshore and oﬀshore reservoirs either as produced oil-associated gas or
oil-free gas. In 2014, the estimated gas reserves are at 6972 million
cubic feet, against an annual consumption of 78.4 trillion cubic feet
(Alcheikhhamdon and Hoorfar, 2016). Noting that the crude gas cannot
be utilized as produced, the gas must ﬁrst undergo a treatment process
to reduce the concentration of the contaminants to meet pipeline spe-
ciﬁcations.
The process for removing H2S and CO2 from a natural gas stream is
referred to as gas sweetening (Abkhiz et al., 2014). There are diﬀerent
methods for gas sweetening and the commonly used techniques are
adsorption and absorption (Tan et al., 2015). With the increasingly
strict environmental regulations on emissions from natural gas treat-
ment plants and the market demand for high-quality natural gas, the
gas sweetening process has become mandatory (Rezakazemi et al.,
2011).
The continuous exploration of new gas reserves along with the in-
crease in the global power demand necessitates the development of less
demanding technologies, so that the treatment process can be accom-
plished at a lowest cost (Alcheikhhamdon and Hoorfar, 2016). Natural
gas industry relies heavily on theoretical approaches to design, improve
and optimize production and processing units. Testing at large scale is
so expensive and it is common to use process simulation to evaluate
such processes. The computer aided process simulation tools are also
helpful

In the open literature, various steady state and dynamics analysis
have been reported for amine-based CO2 absorption (Benadda et al.,
2000; Maalej et al., 2003; Mores et al., 2012). However, they are mostly
focusing on post-combustion processes (Manaf et al., 2016). CO2 ab-
sorption can be represented either via equilibrium-based stage eﬃ-
ciency model or non-equilibrium rate-based model. The superiority of
rate based approach to the traditional equilibrium stage model has been
highlighted in many studies (Borhani et al., 2016; Isa et al., 2016), but
this is achieved at the expense of higher complexity. In addition, most
of the simulation analysis is often based on low-pressure experimental
data. Despite the importance of dynamic model, it is acknowledged that
the complexity of mathematical formulation for dynamic modelling
especially using rate-based approach becomes a hindrance and thus
limits the simulation analysis. Therefore, an alternative technique
needs to be exploited to increase robustness and reduce computational
load (Trapp et al., 2015) especially for high-pressure system.

The aim of this research is to contribute towards the understanding
of the complex transient behaviour of natural gas process treated at the
elevated pressure that is concurrent with the industrial requirement for
controllability and ﬂexible operations to meet the high-quality gas
standard. The simulation model of the absorption system studied in this
paper is developed and validated using the experimental set-up and
data acquired from the high-pressure pilot plant located at Universiti
Teknologi PETRONAS. In the present study, the equilibrium stage
model has been modiﬁed to replicate the result of the actual plant and
the rate based approach. The dynamic analysis and control studies are
then carried out using Aspen Dynamic platform utilizing the modiﬁed
equilibrium model.
2. Model development
2.1. Process description
A simpliﬁed process ﬂow diagram for CO2 pilot plant in Universiti
Teknologi PETRONAS is shown in Fig. 1. Two types of packing are
available in the pilot plant which are structured and random column. In
this study, structured packing is selected in both the experimental and
simulation runs as it has advantages in providing a lower pressure drop
for every single theoretical stage and improved capacity compared to
random packing type. The packing material of the pilot plant is of metal
type FLEXIPAC IY.
Initially, CO2 and CH4 are heated in the heat exchanger to avoid
liquidation in the tubing. Then, the gases blend in the static mixer
before they are compressed and sent to the bottom of the column. For
adjustment of CO2 concentrations, a mass ﬂow controller is used to
control the respective gas in the sour gas mixture. The desired level of
pressure is maintained by regulating the back-pressure regulator. The
sour gas in this study contains only CO2 and CH4 and monitored at a
ratio of 1:1. In practical applications, liquid hydrocarbons and dissolved
gases are typically eliminated prior to the acid gas treatment by ﬂashing
the stream in a ﬂash separator maintained at an intermediate pressure
(Mitra, 2015). The heavy hydrocarbons condense in the ﬂash drum
from the gas stream, and the gas then proceeds to the gas exit of the
separator to the acid gas removal unit.
CO2 is removed from the sour gas by means of chemical absorption
utilizing the 20 wt% aqueous monoethanolamine solution (MEA). The
lean solvent enters the column at 25–30 °C and pressure of approxi-
mately 10–11 bar. The lean solvent ﬂowrate is transported using high
pressure pump at 1 l/min to the top of the column. These conditions are
similar to the experimental set-up of the pilot plant. Counter current
mechanism takes place in the absorber column as two phases contacts
are occurring within the liquid bulk, vapour bulk, ﬁlms and an inter-
face.
Due to the size of the pilot plant, each run requires a signiﬁcant
amount of solvent and feed gas to be fed into the system. Since the work
reported in this paper is part of a larger project scope, experimental
runs were conducted only twice per pressure value due to cost issue. In
addition, the liquid level in the column is maintained by regulating the
drainage pipe, and this may cause temporary pressure ﬂuctuations in
the column.
2.2. Modelling approach
Aspen Plus V8.0 is utilized in this paper for both the steady-state
analysis and validation. After convergence is achieved, the model is
converted into Aspen Dynamic V8.0 for transient analysis and control
studies. For steady-state simulation, two approaches are available
within Aspen: equilibrium and rate-based approaches.
Both techniques will be employed to observe the performance of
CO2 removal at high pressure to ensure the result agrees with the actual
pilot plant. Even though the superiority of rate-based model is widely
acknowledged but the simplicity of traditional equilibrium stage model
is still elegant from the mathematical point of view and the basis for so
many commercial column simulation programs (Taylor et al., 2003). In
addition, the Aspen Dynamic is unable to support rate-based approach.
To compensate for the deviations of these two modelling approaches,

2.2.1. Murphree eﬃciency
The Murphree tray eﬃciency has been used for many years to relate
the theoretical number of stages with the actual trays in an absorber.
This eﬃciency can be based upon the liquid or the vapour composition
on a particular stage at certain conditions of temperature and pressure
(Queiroz et al., 2012). In the equilibrium separation model, two distinct
phases usually operated in counter current conﬁguration and contacted
thoroughly at each stage and leaves at equilibrium. This type of se-
paration process is treated as an equilibrium system and this assump-
tion depends on the ability of each stage to achieve equal pressure,
temperature and phase equilibria. This kind of assumption is not
practical and therefore, tray eﬃciencies are used to compensate the
deviation from equilibrium.
The equilibrium stage model typically assumes that gas and liquid
temperatures are equal at each stage. Using Murphree eﬃciencies gives
a more realistic description of the concentration and temperature pro-
ﬁles as a function of column height rather than using ideal equilibrium
stages (ErikØi, 2012). The deﬁnition of Murphree eﬃciency based on
gas phase mole fraction is illustrated in Fig. 2.

2.2.2. Rate -based approach
Even though the equilibrium stage model has been successfully
applied in many separation processes, the rate-based or non-equili-
brium approach has taken over the standard approach in gas absorber
design (Hoﬀ, 2003) in recent years. This model accounts explicitly the
ﬁnite rates of mass transfer, heat transfer and the chemical reaction
(Borhani et al., 2016). This has led to the speciation of the liquid phase
including molecular and ionic species and not just the total con-
centration of CO2 at a given partial pressure (Hoﬀ, 2003). The rate-
based model avoids the approximation of eﬃciency, by analysing the
mass and heat transfer phenomena that occur on a real tray or actual
packing height. Fig. 3 shows the representation of mass transfer model
at two ﬁlm liquid interface for rate-based approach.

excess molar Gibbs energy. It has the capability to describe multi-
component electrolyte systems over a wide range of state conditions
(Kwaterski and Herri, 2011). In Aspen Plus V8.0, eNRTL model pro-
vides a comprehensive electrolyte thermodynamic framework to model
thermophysical properties of all kinds of electrolyte systems. Two op-

2.5. Steady state absorber model
For steady state modelling, both equilibrium and rate-based ap-
proaches have been applied and one of the best features in Aspen si-
mulator is the RADFRAC model to represent the absorber column. In
order to simulate the steady state conditions, the present model follows
the following assumptions (Gáspár and Cormoş, 2011);
• Counter current with well-mixed between vapour and liquid.
• The vapourization of solvent is negligible.
• The heat released to surrounding is very small.
• The heat and mass transfer between phases follow the two-ﬁlm
theory.
For equilibrium approach, the default setting has been applied by
using vapourization eﬃciency. On the other hand, the rate-based ap-
proach requires more speciﬁcations such as mass transfer coeﬃcient,
heat transfer correlation, eﬀective interfacial area and ﬂow model. In
this study, rate based correlation of Bravo (Rocha et al., 1996) has been
selected due to the suitability of the correlation with structured packed
column. The default value for heat transfer correlation is used while the
ﬂow model is counter current. Film non-ideality correction for liquid
has been selected and eﬀective interfacial area factor has been main-
tained at 1. In the convergence section, the strongly non-ideal liquid has
been chosen due to the usage of MEA and high operating pressure. The
data for the feed streams and the absorption column are essential for
simulation of steady-state conditions and hence, the data has been
collected from UTP pilot plant and tabulated in Table 1 and the sche-
matic diagram of the steady model is as presented in Fig. 4.
2.6. Entering dynamic simulation
To establish the dynamic simulation, several additional inputs need
to be added before it can be converted to Aspen Dynamic. These include
equipment sizing and the hydraulics information (Taqvi et al., 2017).
However, Aspen Plus is not able to support the rate-based approach in
Aspen Dynamic, thus, equilibrium approach has been applied. To
compensate for the deviation between these two approaches, the steady
state model has been adjusted by changing operating conditions and
design speciﬁcations (He and Ricardez-Sandoval, 2016; Trapp et al.,
2015). The present study adopted a similar approach as in (He and
Ricardez-Sandoval, 2016), where the number of stages and Murphree
eﬃciency have been adjusted to meet similar result as in the rate-based
approach. The mode of simulation needs to be setup, i.e. either pressure
driven or ﬂow driven. In this model, ﬂow driven has been selected. The
pressure driven mode is preferred when the pressure of the system is
signiﬁcant as in the gas phase. The ﬂow-driven mode is suitable for
liquid-phase systems or systems with good pressure and ﬂow control.
Once all the necessary information has been provided and pressure-
check has been performed, the steady state simulation is carried out and
the dynamic environment is activated. Fig. 5 illustrates the simulation
environment for transient analysis in Aspen Dynamic.

2.7. Study on the control strategies
An important goal of the dynamic simulation is to develop an ef-
fective base-level regulatory control structure for the absorber. In this
study, controllers will be installed in the dynamic state simulation to
keep the controlled variables such as ﬂowrate of lean solvent, the
composition of CO2 in the sweet gas stream, liquid level and pressure in
the absorber unit at a certain value called the set point (SP). The
Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) controllers will be tuned based
on the diﬀerent gain (Kc), integral (τI) and derivative (τD) values to
observe the corrective action of the controllers. Two control strategies
will be studied, namely, ﬂow control and composition control.
2.7.1. Flow controller
The valve B1 as in Fig. 6 is to control the amount of lean solvent
entering the absorber unit based on the signal sent by the ﬂow con-
troller. The controller is reverse acting which means that when ﬂowrate
of the lean solvent loading increases, a reduced signal will be sent to the
control valve to bring the process variable (PV) to the SP. The corrective
action of the ﬂow controller with diﬀerent Kc values will be in-
vestigated to see the quickness of the corrective action based on the
disturbance introduced. The integral value (τI) will be set constant at
20 min and the derivative value (τD) is zero since the controller is а
Proportional-Integral (PI) controller. Fig. 7 shows the face plate of а
controller.
2.7.2. Composition controller
А composition controller will be installed in the dynamic simulation
to control the composition of CO2 in the sweet gas stream coming out of
the absorber unit. The valve B1 in Fig. 8 controls the opening to enable
a certain amount of lean solvent to pass through to maintain the
composition of CO2 in the sweet gas stream. Fig. 9 shows the face plate
of the tuning parameters of the composition controller. The composi-
tion controller is а PID controller. To investigate the composition con-
troller, τD is set at 1 and τI is maintained at 12. The controller is direct
acting and initialization will be done once the controller is installed.
The set point of the composition of CO2 is 0.41 kmol/kmol and the set
point will be changed to observe the performance of the composition
controller to bring the process variable to the set point based on dif-
ferent Kc values.

3. Results & discussion
3.1. Simulation of steady-state absorption
During the experimental run on the pilot plant absorption column,
the CO2 content at the top of the column is measured using an infrared
gas analyser and compared with the inlet CO2 content in the sour gas
stream. A reduction of CO2 content in the sweet gas stream is observed
indicating that chemical absorption took place successfully at elevated
pressure with the use of aqueous MEA solvent. From the experimental
data, the percentage of CO2 removal at the column pressure of 10 bar is
calculated to be approximately 85%. Both the equilibrium and rate-
based approaches are then developed in the Aspen simulator to com-
pare the CO2 removal percentage.
Table 2 shows the CO2 removal percentage between the pilot plant
and the simulation result. It can be observed that the rate-based ap-
proach produced a better estimate with minimal deviation error. On
the other hand, a signiﬁcant deviation of approximately 35% is ob-
served when the equilibrium approach is used. This is as expected
since rate-based approach is more eﬃcient in describing the separa-
tion system.
As mentioned previously, since Aspen Plus only supports the equi-
librium approach for dynamic simulation, adjustments have been in-
troduced to produce similar result as rate based approach (He and
Ricardez-Sandoval, 2016). This step is also important to achieve a
stable steady state condition for Aspen Dynamic model. In the
simulation performed in this paper, the steady-state equilibrium-based
model is adjusted by varying (1) the Murphree eﬃciency, and (2) the
number of stages. Murphree eﬃciency is varied in order to observe the
eﬀectiveness of this factor in rectifying the activities in the stages and
yields out the best process performance (He and Ricardez-Sandoval,

result suggests that a controller needs to be installed in the lean solvent
stream to accommodate any changes in the output stream. The corre-
sponding temperature proﬁles are as shown in Fig. 20a and b, respec-
tively.

where the gas enters the column to stage 1 where the lean solvent
enters the column. From Fig. 21b, the sustained decrease of −10% in

column as expected due to the less amount of gases entering the
column. It can be generally observed that the overall pressure drop

and vice versa.

3.2.3. Case 5: step change in sour gas and lean solvent input temperature
In any chemical, biological, and physical reactions, the temperature
is an important parameter. Chemical absorption is inﬂuenced by tem-
perature and diﬀerent absorbents behave diﬀerently at diﬀerent tem-
peratures. Figs. 25 and 26 demonstrate the changes in the CO2 com-
positions in the sweet gas stream (blue line) as the temperature of the
lean solvent and sour gas (black line) are subjected to +10% and
−10% changes, respectively. As the lean solvent temperature increases
in Fig. 25a, the CO2 composition in the sweet gas stream also increases
due to exothermic reaction and thus leads to low eﬃciency in the ab-
sorption process (Tan et al., 2012). This result suggests that CO2 ab-
sorption at high pressure can be optimized if being conducted at am-
bient temperature, however high-pressure pump and the exothermic
reaction may also cause the lean solvent temperature to increase. Due
to that factor, intercooler should be installed into the column to reduce
any temperature bulge as suggested by (Walters et al., 2016). As ex-
pected, the opposite trend is observed for a −10% change in the solvent
inlet temperature as shown in Fig. 25b.
An increase or decrease in the sour gas inlet temperature, on the
other hand, results in higher or lower amount of CO2 being absorbed in
the column, respectively.
3.3. Basic control strategy
3.3.1. Case 6: ﬂow controller
А ﬂow controller maintains the ﬂowrate at the desired set-point (SP)
in the presence of disturbances. In the standard Proportional-Integral-
Derivative (PID) controller, the proportional term considers how far the
process variable (PV) is from SP at any instant in time and its con-
tribution to the controller output is based on the size of the error, e(t)
only at time, t. As e(t) grows or shrinks, the inﬂuence of the propor-
tional term grows or shrinks immediately and proportionately.
The integral term adds up the error, e(t) and describes how long and
how far PV has been away from SP. Thus, even a small error, if it
persists, will have a sum that grows over time and the inﬂuence of the
integral term will similarly grow. The steepness of the curve and the
rate of change of a signal trace at a speciﬁc point in time are described
by the derivative term. Correcting the ﬂowrate without delay prevents
upset in the process operation.

the sweet gas stream, more lean solvent will be fed to the absorber
column to prevent disturbance to the process as the eﬃciency of the
process will be aﬀected. The valve on the lean solvent stream controls
the amount of lean solvent ﬂowing in based on the opening percentage.
Integral time and derivative time have been initiated for 12 min and
1 min respectively and these settings clearly show PID controller is
utilized. From the trend observed, the larger gain value, Kc is able to
reduce delay in the response. Contrary to the previous controller, de-
rivative action is implemented to further reduce transient errors as well
as basing а control’s portion in error predicting activities (Knospe,
