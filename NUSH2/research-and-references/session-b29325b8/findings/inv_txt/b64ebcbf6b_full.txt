a b s t r a c t
A pilot plant for CO2 recovery of 2 ton-CO2/day from ﬂue gas emitted from a 500 MW coal ﬁred power
plant was tested with aqueous amine absorbents. Based on a chemical absorption/regeneration process
with 30 wt% aqueous monoethanolamine (MEA) solution, we studied the CO2 recovery as a function of
ﬂow rate and input location of absorbent, pressure and temperature of stripper, and temperature of ﬂue
gas. Experimental data include the temperature proﬁle of the absorber/stripper and CO2 lean/rich
loading values, which remained stable during the operation. We evaluated the energy requirement for
regeneration of aqueous amine and the degree of CO2 removal. While regeneration energy of aqueous
amine increased with an increase in ﬂue gas temperature and stripper pressure, it decreased with an
increase in injection height of absorbent. The optimum point in ﬂow rate of absorbent was 1300 kg/h in
350 Sm3/h ﬂue gas. The regeneration energy using 30 wt% MEA solution was 3.92 GJ/ton CO2 in 90% CO2
removal, ﬂue gas temperature of 40 C, stripper pressure of 0.5 kgf/cm2 and L/G ratio of 3.7 kg/Sm3. In
addition, corrosion rate of the pilot plant were measured in six points. It is the largest as 79.11 mpy in the
bottom of the stripper tower.

1. Introduction
The capture of CO2 is receiving great attention from world
scientists around the world. There are many technology options to
separate CO2 from ﬂue gas; chemical and physical absorption,
cryogenic
separation,
gas-separation
membranes,
bio-
mineralization and vegetation [1e3]. The most common option for
separating CO2 from thermal power plants is scrubbing the gas
stream using an amine solution, which can be used to dilute CO2
streams systems such as coal combustion ﬂue gases, which contain
only 14e16% CO2 by volume [4]. An aqueous MEA solution has been
used as an industrially important absorbent because of rapid
reaction, low cost of the solvent, and thermal stability. However,
one of the disadvantages of amine-based absorption processes is
that they require large amounts of energy in the regeneration stage
of the amine absorbent. Dallos and co-workers showed that the
thermal energy required for the regeneration exceeded half of the
total capture energy [5]. Thus, for the development of a cost
effective CO2 capture process, many research groups have focused
on the reduction of regeneration energy.
Among the alkanolamines, monoethanolamine (MEA), dieth-
anolamine
(DEA),
N-methyldi-ethanolamine
(MDEA),
di-
isopropanolamine
(DIPA),
and
2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol
(AMP) have been widely used as chemical absorbents for removal
of acid gases (CO2 and H2S) [6e9]. Tertiary amines such as MDEA
and triisopropanolamine (TIPA) do not form carbamate due to the
absence of an NeH bond. They only act as bases, contributing to the
formation of bicarbonate. The advantage of tertiary amines is that
the equilibrium is more easily reversed in the stripper. The use of
blended amine solvents in acid gas treatment processes is receiving
considerable attention from researchers [10e12]. Lee and co-
workers investigated the CO2 capture capability of 30 wt%
aqueous solutions of AMP or MDEA in ﬂue gas emitted from an
LNG-based power plant [13]. Although various tests have been
performed for CO2 capture using amine absorbent, only a few
experimental data on regeneration energy of CO2 using an aqueous
MEA solution have been reported in a test bed of thermal power
plant (more than 1 ton CO2/day capacity).
We studied CO2 recovery from a newly constructed a pilot plant
of which capacity is 2 ton CO2/day. In this study, we operated the
CO2 capture process, which could treat 350 Sm3/h ﬂue gas,
continuously for 30 days in order to analyze its long-run operation
characteristics. The system used 30 wt% aqueous MEA solutions.
The temperature of each tower, changing of CO2 loadings of lean/
rich solutions, and MEA concentration were analyzed. Subse-
quently, we have also calculated the regeneration energy of an MEA
solution in the stripper using the values of degree of CO2 removal
from an on-line CO2 gas analyzer and gas ﬂow meters installed at
the absorber. We also converted the potential heat of steam to
energy in order to evaluate the energy consumption at the stripper.

2. Experiment
2.1. Materials
The MEA used in the present study was obtained from BASF
Chemical Co. with a mass purity of >99% and used without further
puriﬁcation. The aqueous solution was prepared by diluting the
concentrated MEA with distilled water. The concentration of MEA
was 30 wt%.
2.2. Experimental set up
The pilot plant for CO2 recovery is shown schematically in Fig. 1.
To minimize side effect of SOx for absorbent, ﬂue gas desulfurization
system that use magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)2) slurry was
installed in ﬁrst step of total processes. It managed SOx concentra-
tion less than 20 ppm. Processes after desulfurization process are
a typical amine-based absorption process consisting of an absorber
(diameter ¼ 0.4 m, total height ¼ 23.5 m) and a stripper (diame-
ter ¼ 0.35 mm, total height ¼ 17 m) with other auxiliary equipment
such as a gas burner, reboiler, pump, condenser and lean/rich amine
exchanger. Each tower (absorber and stripper) was packed with
ring-shaped stainless steel packing material (Intalox Metal Tower
Packing (IMTP), supplied by Norton Co. USA) to increase retention
time and surface area for effective contact between the carbon
dioxide and absorbent. The gas stream exhausted from the top of
each tower was fed to an on-line gas analyzer (model ULTRAMAT 23,
supplied by Siemens, France) for the analysis of CO2 concentration in
the gas stream by non-dispersion infrared method (NDIR).
The ﬂue gas was cooled to about 40 C in order to decrease its
moisture content and subsequently introduced to the absorber.
Then, CO2 in the ﬂue gas reacted counter-currently with a CO2-lean
MEA solution. The gases that did not react with the absorbent were
emitted from the top of the absorber. At the exit line of the
absorber, a water trap was installed to remove vaporized liquids
from the gas stream of the absorber during the operation.
The CO2-rich MEA solution from the bottom of the absorber was
transferred to the stripper via a lean/rich plate heat exchanger in
which the CO2-rich MEA solution was heated to between 90 and
95 C and the CO2-lean MEA discharged from the bottom of the
stripper was simultaneously cooled. In the stripper, CO2 and MEA
solutions were separated from the CO2-rich MEA solution by
heating between 110 C and 120 C. The mixture of steam and CO2-
rich MEA exits from the top of the stripper and is cooled in the
condenser to separate the CO2. The water vapor was sent back to
the stripper from the reﬂux drum and 99% pure CO2 was produced.
The regenerated CO2-lean amine solution was then cooled and
recycled back to the absorber for further CO2 removal from the ﬂue
gas. The CO2-lean MEA solution could be fed into the absorber at
different heights (H1: 16.8 m, H2: 12.6 m, H3: 8.4 m) depending on
the experimental conditions.

2.5. Calculation of corrosion rate
The corrosion cell was cylindrical in shape and made of carbon
steel 1018 with a chemical composition of 0.175% carbon, 0.75%
manganese, and balance iron. A prepared specimen was mounted
to the holder in the process. The corrosion cell was then connected
to a potentiometer equipped with the data acquisition software.
The polarization scan began when the potential of the specimen
reached equilibrium [15]. Three electrochemical techniques, Tafel
plot, potentiodynamic polarization, and cyclic polarization were
used for corrosion measurements. Producing a Tafel plot involves
polarizing a specimen in both anodic and cathodic directions in the
potential range of 1 mV from equilibrium corrosion potential
(Ecorr) and generating an anodic/cathodic polarization curve of the
specimen. The obtained corrosion current density (icorr) was used to
calculate the corrosion rate of the specimen using the following
equation:
CR ¼ ð0:13  icorr  E:W:Þ=ðA  DÞ
where CR is corrosion rate in mils (thousandths of an inch) per year
(mpy), icorr is the corrosion current density, E.W. is the equivalent
weight of specimen, A is the area of specimen in cm2, and D is the
density of the specimen in g/cm3 [16].
3. Results and discussion
The CO2 recovery in the pilot plant was studied in aqueous
solutions of MEA at the various absorbent ﬂow rates, ﬂue gas
temperature, injection height of absorbent, stripper pressure, and
stripper temperature.
3.1. Basic operating condition and temperature proﬁle
The basic operating conditions for the pilot plant are given in
Table 2. The ﬂue gas was introduced to the absorber at a ﬂow rate of
350.0 Sm3/h and was cooled to about 40 C. The moisture in the gas
was removed before being introduced in the absorber. The
temperature of CO2-lean MEA solution fed to the absorber and
reboiler of a stripper were 40 and 113 C, respectively. The pressure
of the stripper was operated at 0.50 kgf/cm2 to prevent the boiling
of MEA solution in the stripper. In our experiment, the average
removal of CO2 remained at 90% based on the guideline for the
economic analysis of CO2 capture process published by IEA
Greenhouse Gas R&D Program [7].
Temperature proﬁles of the absorber and stripper during the
operation are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Fig. 2 is the local temperature
proﬁle of the inner part (T2 w T5) and the exit of the absorber (T1)
during the operation. It shows that time-averaged temperature of
T1, T2, T3, T4 and T5 is 42.3, 44.2, 47.2, 54.2, and 66.9 C, respectively.
In addition the temperature rose (about 17 C) in the higher part of
the absorber (T5 in Fig. 2) which means that an exothermic
absorption reaction takes place at this area of the tower. Emission of
the highest heat of reaction occurred with the low CO2 loading
solution (lean amine) after the higher parts of the absorber (T4 and
T5), where water evaporates from the MEA solution.
Fig. 3 shows the local temperature proﬁle of the inner part (T6 to
T9) of the reboiler. It shows that reboiler temperature remained
almost constant during the operation (T9_ave ¼ 113.6 C), though T6
and T7 showed some variations during the operation and the time-
averaged values were T6 (111.6 C), T7 (112.8 C) and T8 (113.8 C).

3.2. The effect of absorbent (MEA) ﬂow rate and injection height of
absorbent
The effect of absorbent ﬂow rate on regeneration energy for
different feeding positions of the MEA is shown Fig. 4. Other
operation conditions are the same as basic operation conditions
except ratio of the ﬂow rate of liquid and ﬂue gas [kg/Sm3]. The
ﬁgure shows that the optimum point for regeneration energy was
2.7e4.0 L/G ratio.
The effect of absorbent feeding height in the absorber tower
above 12.6 m was small because aqueous MEA solution reacted
rapidly with CO2. Also, CO2 loadings of rich-MEA at feeding heights
above 12.6 m were almost the same as 0.50 mol CO2/mol MEA at
a 2.7 L/G ratio. Regeneration energy at a feeding height of 8.4 m
increased to more than 6.0 GJ/ton CO2 at a 2.7 L/G ratio. And CO2
loading of rich-MEA decreased to 0.42 mol CO2/mol MEA at a 2.7 L/
G ratio.
3.3. The effect of ﬂue gas temperature
The effect of absorbent temperature and ﬂue gas temperature
on CO2 recovery ratio for the same concentration of MEA (30 wt%) is
shown in Fig. 5. The ﬂue gas was introduced to the absorber at
a ﬂow rate of 350.0 Sm3/h and L/G ratio was 3.7 kg/Sm3. The
average removal of CO2 kept 90% in an experiment. The pressure of
the stripper was operated at 0.50 kgf/cm2. Other operation condi-
tions are the same as the basic operation conditions. The regener-
ation energy increased with increase in ﬂue gas temperature from
36 to 45 C because the absorbent absorbs more CO2 at low
temperatures. The regeneration energy is the lowest at 3.82 GJ/
tons-CO2 at 36 C.
3.4. The effect of stripper pressure and CO2 loadings of lean and rich
MEA solutions
The effect of stripper pressure on regeneration energy is shown
Fig. 6. The ﬂow rate of ﬂue gas, input temperature of absorbents,
and bottom temperature of stripper were 1300 kg/h, 40 C, and
113 C, respectively. The average removal of CO2 kept 90% in an
experiment. Fig. 6 shows that regeneration energy of carbon
dioxide increases with an increase in stripper pressure. The
regeneration energy is the lowest at 3.86 GJ/tons-CO2 in MEA
(30 wt%) at stripper pressure of about 0.35 kgf/cm2.
The CO2 loadings of lean and rich MEA solutions were measured
every 8 h and calculated using Eqs.(4) and (5). The CO2-loading
mainly depends on the operational condition and affects both the
degree of CO2 removal and the regeneration energy. In Fig. 6, CO2
loadings of lean and rich solutions during the operation are shown.
Rich CO2 loadings are constant as 0.50 mol CO2/mol MEA and lean

3.5. MEA concentration
The MEA concentration in the CO2-lean/rich MEA solutions was
also measured every 8 h using the titration method in basic oper-
ation conditions. The ﬂow rate of MEA was 1300 kg/h. The regen-
eration energy and the degree of CO2 removal were directly affected
by the MEA concentration; the high MEA concentration decreases
the reboiler duty substantially while the degree of CO2 removal
increased with an increase in MEA concentration. But the MEA of
high concentrations can generally cause corrosion problems. Thus
it is very important to keep the MEA concentration at a constant
level for the stable operation of the process.
Fig. 7 represents the changing of the MEA concentration of CO2-
lean/rich solution during the operation. Each value increased with
increasing operating time. This is mainly due to the water vapor
discharge at the top of the absorber. Operating temperature at the
top of the absorber is more than 60 C. For detailed analysis, a water
trap was installed at the exit of the absorber and showed that the
average capture rate of liquids during the operation was 0.6 kg/day.
From these results, it could be concluded that the increase of MEA
concentration during the operation was mostly due to the reduc-
tion of water content in the solution. Thus, the wash water
recycling zone was established at the top of the absorber to prevent
evaporation of water.
3.6. Corrosion rate in basic operating condition
The corrosion rate of the pilot plant was measured at six points.
The three prepared specimens were located at the top (AIT 21),
middle (AIT 22) and bottom (AIT 23) of the absorber tower; other
three specimens were located at the top (AIT 24), middle (AIT 25)
and bottom (AIT 26) of the stripper tower. Fig. 8 shows the corro-
sion rate is greater at the bottom of each tower. In particular, it is
the largest as 79.11 mpy at the bottom of the stripper tower.
Therefore, we recommend the bottom of the stripper tower be
designed with corrosion-resistant materials.
4. Conclusions
We studied the recovery of CO2 in MEA at a 2 ton-CO2/day pilot
plant as a function of temperature, concentration, and ﬂow rate of
absorbent, pressure and temperature of stripper, and ﬂow rate and
temperature of ﬂue gas. We observed that while regeneration
energy increases with an increase in ﬂue gas temperature and
stripper pressure, it decreases with an increase in injection height
of absorbent. The regeneration energy had optimum point at 3.7 to
3.8 L/G ratio. MEA concentration increased slightly during the
operation mainly from the reduction of water content in the solu-
tion. Thus, at wash water recycling zone needs to be installed at the
top of the absorber to prevent evaporation of water. We recom-
mend the bottom of stripper tower be designed with corrosion-
resistant materials because of its high corrosion rate.
