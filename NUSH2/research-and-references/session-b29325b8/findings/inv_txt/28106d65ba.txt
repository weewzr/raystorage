Comparative techno-economic and environmental assessment of 
HTGR-integrated hydrogen production via helium-heated and electrified 
steam methane reforming

A B S T R A C T
Two HTGR-integrated hydrogen-production pathways based on steam methane reforming—helium-heated SMR 
(h-SMR) and electrified SMR (e-SMR)—are proposed. The former directly supplies HTGR heat to the reformer 
through helium, whereas the latter converts HTGR heat into electricity. Aspen Plus evaluates these systems 
against conventional and electrolysis-based benchmarks. Elimination of the fired furnace reduces natural gas 
consumption by 18.8% for h-SMR and 23.3% for e-SMR relative to gray hydrogen. Levelized cost of hydrogen is 
estimated at $2.37/kg for h-SMR and $2.99/kg for e-SMR, indicating cost competitiveness relative to the 
benchmarked pink and green hydrogen pathways under the assumed conditions. Furthermore, life-cycle as­
sessments reveal net global warming potentials of 3.5 and 3.18 kgCO2/kgH2 for h-SMR and e-SMR, a reduction of 
>70% relative to gray hydrogen. Sensitivity analysis confirms the economic robustness of the h-SMR pathway 
across market scenarios. HTGR-integrated SMR processes offer a practical and cost-effective transition pathway 
for large-scale hydrogen production.

1. Introduction
Achieving net-zero pathways commonly requires decarbonization 
strategies that combine various renewable electricity sources, such as 
wind and solar energy, with dispatchable low-carbon options, including 
nuclear generation and fossil-based systems equipped with carbon 
capture and storage (CCS) [1]. In this context, nuclear energy is an 
attractive low-carbon option because it can supply electricity and 
high-grade heat continuously and stably on a large scale, enabling 
centralized substitution for fossil-based energy inputs [2]. Many fossil 
fuel-based hydrogen production routes, such as reforming and gasifi­
cation, are strongly endothermic, and a significant fraction of plant-wide 
CO2 emissions originate from the combustion of additional fuels 
required to supply process heat. Therefore, replacing this heat input 
with non-carbon energy sources presents a direct opportunity to reduce 
CO2 emissions while conserving fossil fuels [3].
In this context, small modular reactors are of particular interest 
because their modular deployment and enhanced safety characteristics 
can enable more flexible operation than conventional large reactors, 
including the ability to vary thermal and electrical outputs through

power ramping and cogeneration [2]. Among small modular reactor 
designs, high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) concepts are 
frequently highlighted due to their inherent safety features and high 
outlet temperatures reaching ~950 ◦C [4], thereby supporting efficient 
electricity generation and the delivery of high-grade process heat to 
chemical and petroleum applications [5]. Although alternative 
nuclear-driven hydrogen pathways—including thermolysis, thermo­
chemical cycles, radiolysis, and photolysis—have been proposed, tech­
nological and economic limitations have hindered their near-term, 
large-scale deployment [6]. Consequently, HTGR-integrated steam 
methane reforming (SMR) is widely considered a promising near-term 
scalable route for low-carbon hydrogen production [7].
Helium-heated steam methane reforming (h-SMR) has been pro­
posed as a nuclear-assisted route for reducing the carbon intensity of 
conventional hydrogen production by directly supplying high- 
temperature heat to the reformer [8]. In Japan, the technical feasi­
bility of HTGR–SMR integration has been experimentally examined 
using a large-scale mockup facility [9]. Additionally, the Japan Atomic 
Energy Agency (JAEA) is conducting a project to integrate an SMR with 
the 30 MWt High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor (HTTR), which 
supplies process heat at temperatures ranging from 750 to 800 ◦C. This 
system is expected to reduce methane consumption by 35% compared 
with the conventional route [10], with a demonstrated hydrogen pro­
duction capacity exceeding 800 Nm3/h [11]. Furthermore, the HTTR 
has achieved a reactor-outlet coolant temperature of 950 ◦C, enabling 
HTGR usage beyond electricity generation by providing high-grade heat 
suitable for advanced power cycles and hydrogen production [12]. In 
the United States, techno-economic evaluations performed by the Idaho 
National Laboratory have similarly suggested that HTGR-integrated 
SMR can reduce natural gas consumption by 11.6% and CO2 emissions 
by up to 38.6% when CCS is implemented [13]. Beyond such 
system-level assessments, reactor-scale studies have advanced the 
design of helium-heated reformers. For instance, Wu et al. developed a 
one-dimensional reaction–flow model for an HTGR-heated reformer 
tube and validated it against experimental results [14]. Later, they 
further extended this framework to a membrane reformer to quantify the 
effect of selective H2 removal on methane conversion and hydrogen 
yield [15]. Additionally, Mozdzierz et al. employed numerical analysis 
to examine the operating configuration of a helium-heated reformer and 
discuss a parallel-flow scheme for alleviating excessive temperature 
gradients and the associated thermal stress risks. Helium heating has 
also been explored for other endothermic catalytic processes [16]. For 
example, Zhang et al. performed a non-dominated sorting genetic 
algorithm-based multi-objective optimization of an industrial-scale 
helium-heated reverse water-gas shift reactor using a two-dimensional 
pseudo-homogeneous model [17].
Electrified steam methane reforming (e-SMR) is driven by the limi­
tations of conventional fired reforming in which heat must be supplied 
externally, resulting in inefficient reactor heat transfer and substantial 
methane fuel consumption [18]. Resistive heating, also known as Ohmic 
or Joule heating, is a specific implementation of electrification that in­
volves passing an electrical current through a conductive reactor to 
directly generate high-temperature heat [19]. Wismann et al. demon­
strated this concept using Ni-based catalyst-coated FeCrAl alloy tubes 
with Cu sockets at the tube ends to deliver electrical power for Ohmic 
heating [20]. This direct resistive heating scheme is associated with 
highly uniform heat delivery near the catalyst layer. Specifically, the 
predicted temperature difference across the catalytic coating is report­
edly <2 ◦C, supporting stable operation under strongly endothermic 
conditions [21]. Such integrated electrified SMR concepts enable 
compact reactor configurations and rapid start-up responses, as heat is 
supplied directly by electricity rather than by an externally fired furnace 
[22]. Significantly, scaling analysis indicated that a conventional 
1100 m3 side-fired reformer producing 2230 kmol H2/h could be 
replaced by a 5 m3 resistance-heated reformer, demonstrating the 
effectiveness of this technology for modular scale applications [23].
Despite these advances, a comprehensive plant-wide analysis is 
essential, as reactor and heat-supply configurations significantly influ­
ence the utility, heat recovery, and optimal energy layout of hydrogen 
plants [24]. Accordingly, this study proposes HTGR-integrated nuclear 
heat integration (h-SMR) and nuclear electricity integration (e-SMR) 
and comparatively evaluates their techno-economic and environmental 
performances. Specifically, the system boundary explicitly incorporates 
the HTGR-based supply of both heat and electricity, rather than treating 
nuclear energy as an external utility. To ensure comparability, all 
pathways are evaluated based on consistent functional units and system 
boundaries. To further evaluate the competitiveness of the proposed 
HTGR-integrated configurations, pink and green hydrogen production 
pathways are established as reference benchmarks. These pathways 
represent the most prominent low-carbon alternatives currently being 
pursued and provide broader context for comparative assessment. In 
Fig. 1, gray and blue hydrogen correspond to SMR without and with 
CCS, respectively, while pink and green hydrogen refer to hydrogen 
produced via nuclear- and renewable-powered electrolysis [26].
2. Process description and modeling
2.1. Technological overview
Both h-SMR and e-SMR are hydrogen production processes based on 
SMR, which is the primary industrial process employed for hydrogen 
production [27]. SMR involves the reaction of natural gas with 
high-temperature steam; however, since natural gas typically contains 
sulfur and heavier hydrocarbons—in addition to its primary component, 
methane—a pre-reformer is employed to prevent coke formation in the 
main reformer [28]. During pre-reforming, the conversion of represen­
tative species, such as ethane and propane, follows the reaction stoi­
chiometry outlined in Eqs. (1) and (2): 
C2H6 + 2H2O →2CO + 5H2, ΔH◦= 347 kJ / mol,
(1) 
C3H8 + 3H2O →3CO + 7H2, ΔH◦= 499 kJ / mol.
(2) 
Subsequently, SMR converts natural gas into hydrogen according to 
the reforming reaction outlined in Eq. (3). Since this reaction is highly 
endothermic, a continuous external heat supply is required. In conven­
tional SMR processes, a fired furnace is employed in which radiant heat 
is transferred to the process gas flowing through catalyst-filled tubes. 
CH4 + H2O ↔CO + 3H2, ΔHR = 206 kJ/mol
(3) 
To maximize the hydrogen yield, the reformer effluent was directed 
into a water-gas shift (WGS) reactor. Since the WGS reaction (outlined in 
Eq. (4)) is exothermic, the conversion in the high-temperature shift 
reactor is thermodynamically limited by equilibrium at elevated 
temperatures. 
CO + H2O ↔CO2 + H2, ΔHR = −41 kJ/mol
(4) 
Subsequent hydrogen purification is achieved using pressure-swing 
adsorption (PSA), which exploits the principle that impurities are 
selectively adsorbed onto solid adsorbents at elevated pressures as 
hydrogen passes through. The PSA process typically consists of five 
primary steps: adsorption, regeneration, pressure equalization, depres­
surization, and pressurization. These steps proceed sequentially in a 
cyclic manner across multiple adsorbent beds. In conventional pro­
cesses, high-purity hydrogen is recovered for storage, whereas the 
remaining gas mixture, referred to as PSA tail gas, is utilized as fuel for 
the SMR furnace.
The transition from gray to blue hydrogen was achieved by inte­
grating a carbon capture unit (CCU) into a conventional SMR process. In 
this study, the CO2 capture unit was placed downstream of the WGS 
section and upstream of the PSA unit to capture CO2 from the shifted 
syngas stream. Although various carbon capture technologies have been 
developed, including adsorption, absorption, and membrane separation,

In the h-SMR process, the conventional gas-fired furnace is replaced 
with a helium-heated steam reformer. This unit operates as a shell-and- 
tube type system, where high-temperature secondary helium from the 
HTGR flows through the shell side, transferring thermal energy to the 
catalyst-filled tubes where the reforming reaction occurs [30]. By 
eliminating the requirement for natural gas combustion as a heat source, 
this configuration significantly reduces direct CO2 emissions from the 
furnace stack. In the e-SMR process, a conventional gas-fired furnace is 
replaced by an electric heating mechanism based on Ohmic heating 
principles [23]. Unlike traditional SMR in which heat is supplied via 
combustion, e-SMR uses electrical energy to directly drive the endo­
thermic reforming reaction. In the process simulation, the required 
thermal duty is supplied by electricity generated from the Brayton cycle 
of the HTGR [31]. Electrification necessitates a significant modification
