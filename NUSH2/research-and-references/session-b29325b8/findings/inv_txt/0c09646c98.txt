A B S T R A C T
Post-combustion CO2 capture is a widely recognised technology for controlling industrial CO2 emissions.
However, issues like the substantial energy demands for solvent regeneration still need to be solved. The
current work aims to discuss the outcomes of process optimisation within a pilot-scale CO2 capture facility
by implementing interheating in the stripper to reduce energy consumption. The pilot-scale experiments
were executed at Amager Bakke, a Waste-to-Energy plant in Copenhagen, Denmark. These tests used 30 wt%
Monoethanolamine as the solvent. A semi-lean solvent from the middle of the stripper is withdrawn for external
heating by an interheater and fed to the stripper at the same height. A steam generator with adjustable
electrical duty is used as the heat source for the interheater. The novelty of this work lies in the pilot scale
demonstration of stripper interheating at an industrial facility, which has seldom been done. The objective is to
experimentally demonstrate the performance of the Interheating configuration at the pilot scale and analyse
the potential improvements in the energy consumption of the process. The interheating experiments were
conducted by varying the reboiler duty in the range of 12 to 20 kW and the interheater duty in the range of 2
to 5 kW while maintaining a solvent flow of 267 kg/h and a flue gas flow of 99 kg/h. The results provide several
possible scenarios, are discussed in detail, and are compared to previously obtained base case results. While
maintaining a capture efficiency of around 83%, stripper interheating has demonstrated a specific reboiler duty
of 2.71 GJ/tonne CO2 compared to 3.71 GJ/tonne CO2 achieved by the base case operation. A 3% reduction in
the specific reboiler duty was achieved by interheating at a capture efficiency slightly over 90%. A preliminary
model based on the experimental results has shown a further reduction of SRD to 2.25 GJ/tonne CO2 at high
capture efficiencies.
1. Introduction
Post-combustion capture (PCC) is a critical technology essential to
eliminate industrial CO2 emissions into the atmosphere. PCC is closely
linked to achieving SDG goals such as Climate Action and Afford-
able and Clean Energy. Amine-based CO2 capture is a well-established
technology which can effectively capture 90 to 100% of the CO2
emissions from flue gases released by fossil-based power plants, Waste-
to-Energy (WtE) facilities processing Municipal Solid Waste (MSW)
and biogenic waste, and biogas plants. Monoethanolamine (MEA) is
one of the most known and commonly used amine solvents. MEA
has several advantages, such as fast reaction kinetics, high solvent

advanced process configurations that optimise the stripper operation
can help overcome the disadvantages. MEA is a well-documented and
tested solvent in operation, solvent degradation, emissions, and process
simulation. Therefore, it is a good candidate for testing advanced
process configurations. There are several documented process config-
urations in the literature which report an improvement in the energy
efficiency of CO2 capture, such as intercooled absorber, interheated
stripper, split flow, lean vapour compression, among several others [9].
In our previous work, we have discussed in detail the demonstration
of split flow configuration [10]. The current work aims to discuss the
implementation of stripper interheating.
Interheating is done by withdrawing a stream of semi-lean solvent
from the middle of the stripper and injecting it back into the strip-
per [9,11–15]. This is a simplified version of heat integrated stripper
and is easier to implement on existing structures [9]. Interheating in
the stripper optimises the temperature profile in the stripper’s top and
middle. An optimal temperature profile can be achieved by heating
the semi-lean solvent, bringing the stripper temperature in the middle
closer to the reboiler temperature [9]. The interheater modification was
predicted to reduce the stripper parasitic loss by 4.6 to 6.1% [13,15,
16]. Several other works can be found in the literature which conducted
simulations of interheating of the stripper [17–26].
The semi-lean solvent could be interheated by various sources [9]:
• the hot flue gas before it is cooled and sent to the absorber [27]
• the reboiler condensate [11,16]
• the hot lean solvent exiting the stripper bottom [11,16,28]
• a combination [11]
• high-temperature heat pumps integrated with waste heat recov-
ery.
Simulations were conducted to model waste heat recovery from
different parts of a post-combustion power plant as a heat source for
interheating the semi-lean solvent in the stripper [12]. The patent
says that waste heat recovery for interheating in the stripper causes
a reduction in the reboiler duty by 6.77% and an increase in the
condenser duty by 1% [18].
Muhammad et al. [29] has simulated the use of reboiler conden-
sate for interheating the stripper. Simulations were done to optimise
the withdrawal and feed stages in the stripper. An optimal SRD was
observed when a CO2-rich solvent was withdrawn from stage 2 (from
the top) and fed to the stripper at stage 15 (from the top). When the
solvent was interheated, it caused further steam and CO2 production.
As the interheated solvent was fed to stage 15, the vapour stream had
a longer contact time with the solvent in the stripper, thus stripping

De Miguel Mercader et al. [15] provides an overall perspective of
the implementation of several configurations with no and low level of
heat integration and their impact on the net efficiency of a 1070 MWe
coal-fired power plant connected to a CO2 capture plant (250 MWe
equivalent). It was reported that implementing an interheated stripper
with low-level heat integration results in the least penalty on the net
efficiency of the power plant. The interheated case with low-level heat
integration gave the best results compared to several configurations,
such as base case, Lean Vapour compression (LVC), split flow, and
LVC + split flow. However, an interheated stripper without any heat
integration was shown to have a higher penalty on efficiency when
compared to LVC + split flow with no integration. Karimi et al. [30]
has detailed the positive and negative effects of interheating through a
simulation study. Interheating was shown to negatively impact the SRD
of the process without heat integration in the system. However, when
done in conjugation with heat integration, interheating was beneficial
by bringing the temperature profile in the stripper closer to the optimal.
An optimal stripper temperature profile would be linear, eliminating
pinch regions and efficiently using the packing. Both the reboiler and
the condenser duty were shown to be reduced by 5.7% and 13.3% for
one interheater and 9.9% and 22.7%, respectively, for two interheater
cases. The optimal location for the placement of the interheater was
also simulated and said to be 1/3rd from the top of the column.
Van Wagener [26] has shown that interheating has the most sig-
nificant impact when done in the middle of the stripper. When the
interheater was placed at the bottom, directly above the reboiler, the
extracted solvent from the stripper was much hotter than the middle
of the stripper. Therefore, less heat could be exchanged. Additionally,
the driving force at the reboiler was higher than the middle or the
stripper top. Implementing interheating just above the reboiler would
cause desorption of CO2 due to a high driving force rather than heating
the solvent. The interheater at the stripper middle would operate
closer to the mass transfer pinch point, thus effectively heating the
solvent. The equivalent work was shown to be reduced by 1.5 kJ/mol
CO2 by implementing interheating. It was presented that the stripper’s
performance was improved by interheating due to higher selectivity of
CO2 compared to water at high temperatures. Hot lean solvent exiting
the stripper was used to interheat the semi-lean solvent.
Frailie et al. [17] conducted a simulation study of interheating in
stripper for 8 m PZ, 7 m MDEA/2 m PZ, and 5 m PZ solvents. The hot
fluid for interheating was the hot lean solvent exiting the stripper.
As the duty was displaced from the main HE to the interheater, the
temperature of the hot rich solvent entering the stripper top was lower.
A decrease in the hot rich solvent temperature resulted in an increased
value of PCO2∕PH2O and consequently reduced the SRD of the system. Li
et al. [19] also showed that the use of hot lean solvent for interheating
the stripper reduces the energy requirement for the production of
steam and sensible heating of the solvent, thus reducing the SRD by
6.1% from 3.6 GJ/tonne CO2 to 3.38 GJ/tonne CO2. Zhao et al. [20]
has shown that the SRD could be reduced to 2.56 GJ/tonne CO2 by
implementing interheating by hot lean solvent for MDEA/PZ based
solvent. Additionally, the net efficiency penalty was reported to reduce
from 8.49% to 8.25%.
Li et al. [25] conducted a Techno-economic assessment on several
advanced process configurations such as split flow, intercooling, and
interheating. Interheating the stripper was reported to reduce the net
costs by US$2.9/tonne CO2 despite the increased capital costs due to
extra heat exchanger requirements.
Stoffregen et al. [31] reported a pilot-scale demonstration of strip-
per interheating at a coal-fired power plant. However, no informa-
tion is available regarding the configuration’s performance. Stripper
interheating by use of the hot lean solvent and steam condensate
from the reboiler has been commercialised by Mitsubishi Heavy In-
dustries at a natural gas-fired power plant in Abu Dhabi [32]. The
commercial plant utilised KS-1 solvent, and an energy consumption of
2.9 GJ/tonne CO2 was obtained using stripper interheating. However,
there is no documentation of the operation or detailed analysis of the
configuration.
