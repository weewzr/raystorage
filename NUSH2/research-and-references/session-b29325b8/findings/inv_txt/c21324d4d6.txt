Mass Transfer Performance of CO2 Absorption from Natural Gas
using Monoethanolamine (MEA) in High Pressure Operations
Hairul N. Abdul Halim,†,‡ Azmi M. Shariﬀ,*,† Lian S. Tan,† and Mohammad A. Bustam†
†Department of Chemical Engineering, Universiti Teknologi PETRONAS, Bandar Seri Iskandar, 31750 Tronoh, Perak Malaysia
‡School of Bioprocess Engineering, Universiti Malaysia Perlis, Kompleks Pusat Pengajian Jejawi 3, 02600 Arau, Perlis Malaysia
ABSTRACT: Most carbon dioxide (CO2) absorption studies that use amine based solvents for ﬂue gas treatments were
conducted at atmospheric pressure. This study reports the absorption performance of monoethanolamine (MEA) for the
removal of CO2 from natural gas (NG) at high pressure conditions. The absorption experiments were conducted in an
absorption column packed with Sulzer metal gauze packing at 5.0 MPa operating pressure. The eﬀects of gas ﬂow rate (18.89−
35.08 kmol/m2·h), liquid ﬂow rate (1.81−4.51 m3/m2·h), MEA concentration (1.0−4.0 kmol/m3), and liquid temperature (27−
45 °C) on the mass transfer performance were evaluated in terms of CO2 removal (%) and overall volumetric mass transfer
coeﬃcient (KGav). From this study, the KGav was found to increase with increasing liquid ﬂow rate and MEA concentration.
However, the mass transfer performance was not aﬀected by the total gas ﬂow rate. The optimum condition for liquid
temperature was found to be at 40 °C.
■INTRODUCTION
Natural gas (NG) is a fossil fuel which consists of combustible
mixtures of hydrocarbon gases such as methane (CH4), ethane
(C2H6), propane (C3H8), butane (C4H10), pentane (C5H12),
and impurities such as carbon dioxide (CO2), oxygen (O2),
nitrogen (N2), and hydrogen sulﬁde (H2S) as well as rare gases
(argon, helium, xenon). NG has a high energy conversion for
power generation and emits low potentially harmful byproducts
to the atmosphere, making it one of the superior choices for
fuel.
Removing acid gases such as CO2 is an important step in
natural gas puriﬁcation technology because the presence of
CO2 reduces its heating value. Furthermore, in the presence of
moisture, CO2 may react with water to produce carbonic acid
which would accelerate corrosion in the pipelines and process
equipment. It must also be removed in order to prevent
crystallization during cryogenic processes (liquefaction proc-
ess).
CO2 removal technologies include absorption, adsorption,
membrane, and cryogenic separation. Among them, chemical
absorption is the most established technology for CO2 removal
due to its ability to eﬀectively reduce CO2 concentration to the
desired target. Amine-based solvent is the most preferred
choice as it oﬀers fast reactivity, thus reducing the capital cost
by allowing the use of smaller process equipment compared to
physical absorption. For ﬂue gas treatment, numerous
researches for CO2 removal via chemical absorption have
been conducted at atmospheric pressure with low CO2
concentrations (<15%).1−10 The motivation behind these
studies is to reduce CO2 concentration before releasing the
treated gas into the atmosphere. The performance of several
solvents such as ethanolamine (MEA), methyldiethanolamine
(MDEA), 2-amino-2-methyl-1-propanol (AMP), diethylenetri-
amine (DETA), and ammonia have been extensively studied in
packed absorption columns at atmospheric pressure. However,
the performance of chemical solvents at high pressure
operations, speciﬁcally for natural gas puriﬁcation, is still
limited and has to be further explored.
Mass transfer studies in packed absorption columns have
been previously reported by Benadda et al.11 and Maalej et al.12
for high pressure operations of up to 1.2 and 5.0 MPa,
respectively. The CO2 removal in their systems involved the use
of Na2CO3, NaHCO3, and NaOH while the performances were
reported in terms of interfacial area (a) and volumetric liquid
mass transfer coeﬃcient (kLa). A selectivity study of H2S and
CO2 removal from nitrogen gas (N2) using MEA in a packed
absorption column at 1.0 MPa was conducted by Godini and
Mowla.13 They discovered that by increasing the pressure and
the ratio of molar ﬂow rate of liquid/molar ﬂow rate of gas (L/
G), the absolute eﬃciency in H2S and CO2 absorptions was
also increased. Tan et al.14 reported the removal of 75 vol %
CO2 in NG at 1.0 MPa using a mixed amines solvent, known as
Stonvent-II in a pilot scale absorption column packed with
IMTP No. 25 packing. They discovered that the performance
of liquid temperature at 35 °C was better than at 45 °C.
However, the overall volumetric mass transfer coeﬃcient
(KGav) study, in packed absorption columns at high pressure
has never been reported in the open literature.
In this paper, the mass transfer performance of CO2
absorption from CO2/NG gas mixture in a high pressure
operation using MEA solvent is reported. Two of the most
commonly used evaluation criteria, KGav and CO2 removal (%)
that represented the mass transfer performance, were
determined by conducting experiments at various process
parameters such as gas ﬂow rate, liquid ﬂow rate, MEA
concentration, and liquid temperature. In this study, the
experiments were conducted with CO2 concentration of 20%

■MATERIALS AND METHODS
Chemical. Monoethanolamine (MEA) (99% purity) was
purchased from Acros Brand. CO2 (99.99% purity) used in this
experiment was purchased from Air Product Malaysia, while
NG with 97% CH4, 2% CO2, and 1% heavier hydrocarbon was
supplied by Petronas Dagangan Bhd. All materials were used as
received without further puriﬁcation.
Equipment and Procedures. Absorption experiments
were performed in a 2.040 m height and 0.046 m inside
diameter (ID) packed column. This column was packed with
Sulzer metal gauze packing (Sulzer Chemtech Pte Ltd.,
Winterthur, Switzerland). The surface area of the structured
packing is approximately 500 m2/m3. The column and
structured packing were made of 316 stainless steel. The
absorption column was designed with six sampling points at
diﬀerent levels for measuring gas concentration proﬁle along
the column.
A schematic diagram of the experimental set up is shown in
Figure 1. Prior to conducting the experiments, the desired gas
composition of CO2 and NG was prepared by setting the gas
ﬂow rate of each gas using the individual mass ﬂow controllers.
Then, the mixed gas was compressed and stored in a high
pressure gas vessel. This gas was then introduced into the
bottom of the packed absorption column at the desired ﬂow
rate using a gas ﬂow controller.
All experiments were conducted at 5.0 MPa with a back
pressure regulator strategically placed at the outlet of the
absorption column. The liquid absorbent was pumped into the
absorber at the desired ﬂow rate by using a high pressure liquid
pump. It was also introduced from the top of the column to
create a countercurrent contact with the gas. Thus, the CO2 in
the gas phase will be absorbed into the liquid. The treated gas
exited the column at the top while the CO2 rich solution exited
from the bottom of the column and collected in a storage tank.
Each absorption experiment was operated until steady-state
conditions were reached. CO2 concentrations at the inlet,
outlet, and sampling points along the column were measured
using a CO2−CH4 IR gas analyzer (Fuji Electric Instrument).
The gas concentration at diﬀerent levels along the column were
sampled by switching from one sampling point to another, and
readings were taken only after a steady state for each level was
reached. The liquid entrainment was avoided by installing a
ﬁlter in the sampling line. Details of the experimental
conditions of the absorption process are listed in Table 1.
■RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Validation of the Experimental data. In order to validate
the experimental results produced by this bench-scale packed
absorption system, a few experimental runs have been
conducted with the same conditions and compared with the
results reported by Maneeintr et al.19 The equipment set up
and experimental conditions for both processes are summarized
in Table 2. The main diﬀerences between these two systems are
the type of structured packing used and the size of the column.
Validations were conducted by comparing the trend of the KGav
results.
Maneeintr et al.19 has reported that with increasing MEA
concentrations, ranging from 1.0 to 3.0 kmol/m3, the KGav
values had increased from 0.2 to about 0.6 kmol/m3·h·kPa. In
comparison, our experimental results (see Figure 2) clearly
followed the same trends. In addition, the KGav values were
within the range previously reported by Maneeintr et al.19
This validation study conﬁrmed that the system had
performed well and in agreement with the results reported by
