Background
There is an urgent need to control GHG emissions to 
the atmosphere, particularly CO2, which is considered 
to be a major contributor due to its abundance [1,2]. In 
addition to environmental reasons for CO2 removal, 
there are some industrial processes, such as ammo­
nia production and hydrogen production, which also 
require the removal of CO2 [3]. The absorption of CO2 
into chemical solvents (alkanolamines) is one of the 
most promising technologies for capturing CO2 due to 
its maturity, cost–effectiveness and capability of han­
dling large amounts of exhaust stream [3]. One of the 
key parameters for this technology is to use an effec­
tive solvent, which should have fast reaction kinetics, 
high mass-transfer performance, high absorption 
capacity, low energy requirement for regeneration, low 
degradation rate and low corrosiveness.
It is generally accepted that reaction kinetics data are 
one of the most important parameters for CO2 absorp­
tion with chemical solvents. This is because reaction 
kinetics data are required for effectively designing 
and simulating an absorption column [4]. In order to 
obtain reaction kinetics data, CO2 absorption experi­
ments need to be performed using laboratory-scale 
gas–liquid contactors such as stirred cell reactor, wet­
ted wall column, laminar jet absorber, wetted sphere 
absorber, string of disc contactor or stopped-flow appa­
ratus. The absorption rate data obtained from the CO2 
absorption equipment can then be interpreted using 
graphical methods, simplified kinetics models based on 
reaction mechanisms (i.e., zwitterion, termolecular or 
base-catalyzed hydration mechanisms) and compre­
hensive numerically solved reaction kinetics models 
that take into account the coupling between chemical 
equilibrium, mass-transfer and chemical kinetics of all 
possible reactions.
This article is a comprehensive review of the kinet­
ics of CO2 absorption into amine solutions, including 
measurement and determination of kinetics data from 
laboratory-scale gas–liquid contactors, proposed reac­
tion mechanisms for the CO2–amine reaction, proposed 
reaction kinetics models and kinetics behavior.

Reaction kinetics is one of the most important parameters for the amine-based CO2 capture process because 
it indicates how fast CO2 reacts with amine, and it is one of the key parameters required for simulating 
and designing the absorption column. Reaction kinetics has been studied for several decades to aid the 
understanding of the reaction mechanism, obtaining the kinetics data and understanding the kinetics 
behavior. This article will comprehensively review these research activities from past to present and look at 
the future perspective of the study of CO2 absorption kinetics.

 Stirred cell reactor
In the stirred cell reactor (Figure 1A), the gas and liquid 
phases are stirred separately using separated adjustable 
impellers. The gas–liquid interface needs to be smooth, 
and a consistent interface is achieved by controlling the 
agitation speed from impellers. A pressure transducer 
is located in the gas-phase side for measuring the total 
pressure in the reactor. In addition, temperature sensors 
are located in both the gas and liquid sides of the reactor, 
and baffles are installed in the liquid side in order to 
avoid formation of vortices. The gas absorption rate is 
determined by measuring the change in total pressure 
in the reactor [15,19]. The advantage of the stirred cell 
reactor is that only gas-phase measurement is required. 
Therefore, this reactor is suitable for toxic gas or liq­
uid. In addition, the reactor can be used to measure 
the solubility of gas into liquid. However, its disadvan­
tage is the difficulty of maintaining the smooth and 
consistent gas–liquid interface. Many researchers have 
used the stirred cell reactor for studying the kinetics 
of CO2 absorption in various amine solutions, such as 
MEA, MDEA, triethanolamine (TEA), AMP, blended 
MEA–MDEA and blended MEA–TEA [19–21].

 String of disc contactor
The string of disc contactor is presented in Figure 1E. 
A number of circular discs are held together by a verti­
cal wire in the absorption column. The reactive liquid 
flows downward from the top of the contactor. CO2 is 
introduced counter-currently from the bottom of the 
contactor. The active surface area can be calculated based 
on the diameter and number of the discs. In addition, 
the absorption surface area of this contactor can be var­
ied by introducing or removing the disc elements in the 
column. A disadvantage of this contactor is that absorp­
tion rate at specific concentrations of liquids and/or gas 
cannot be achieved. This is because the concentration of 
CO2 and the composition of reactive liquid are changed 
accordingly along the height of the string column [14,30]. 
Therefore, the overall absorption rate, which can be cal­
culated by the difference of mass flux at inlet and outlet 
of the column, is applied [11]. The reaction kinetics of 
AEEA and DETA were successfully investigated using 
the string of disc contactor.
  
 Stopped-flow apparatus
The stopped-flow technique is a homogeneous direct 
method for measuring the gas absorption into liquid 
solvent. Saturated gas and liquid solutions are mixed in 
the stopped-flow apparatus as presented in Figure 1F. The 
conductivity cell monitors ion formation as a function of 
time, since the ion formation initiates a voltage change. 
Thereafter, the microcomputer automatically generates 
the observed pseudo first-order constant based on the 
output voltage values [31]. However, the disadvantage of 
this technique is that a conductivity measurement can 
only be done at low amine concentration. The kinetics of 
CO2 absorption into aqueous solution of several amines 
(i.e., AMP, ethylenediamine, ethyl ethanolamine and 
diethyl monoethanolamine) have been determined using 
the stopped-flow apparatus [31,32].
  
 Overview
Several experimental gas–liquid contactors for measuring 
the CO2 absorption rate (e.g., stirred cell reactor, wetted 
wall column, laminar jet absorber, wetted sphere absorber, 
string of disc contactor and stopped-flow apparatus) have 
been reviewed. Aboudheir et al. summarized the values of 
kinetics parameters in terms of the reaction-rate constant 
for MEA obtained from different gas–liquid contactors 
at various concentrations (0.05–4.8 M) and tempera­
tures (295–353 K) [26]. They pointed out that the use of 
different measurement methods provide different val­
ues of the reaction rate constant, even the experiments 
that are operated under similar MEA concentration and 
temperature. This is due to the difference in physics and 
chemistry associated with the absorption process in the 
contactors. For stirred cell reactor, laminar jet absorber 
and wetted sphere absorber, the CO2 concentration feed 
into the absorber needs to be pure CO2 (or almost 100% 
CO2). This is because these absorbers are a closed system; 
after introducing a certain amount of CO2, the system 
will be closed. The absorbed CO2 is then measured by a 
change in pressure in the reactor for stirred cell reactor 
or by soap flow meter for laminar jet and wetted sphere 
absorbers. If CO2 balanced with inert gas (e.g., N2) is fed 
into the system, the concentration of CO2 will be reduced 
(i.e., gas-phase mass-transfer coefficient will be increased) 
accordingly as the absorption reaction proceeds. Thus, 
the measured CO2 absorption rate is found to be unreli­
able. On the other hand, CO2 is flown continuously in 
the wetted wall column and string of disc contactor. The 
overall CO2 absorption rate is calculated based on the 
difference of mass flux of inlet and outlet gas streams. 
Therefore, in this case, the inlet CO2 can be mixed with 
inert gas. Unlike the other techniques, the stopped-flow 
apparatus is a homogeneous direct method. The CO2 will 
be saturated in liquid phase and reacts with reactive liq­
uid. Following this, the kinetics data will be interpreted 
via conductivity measurement. Although the experiments 
are performed under the same amine concentration and 
temperature, the physics and chemistry of the absorption 
process in the various contactors differ. 
However, among the experimental gas–liquid contac­
tors, the wetted wall column and laminar jet absorber 
have been found to be predominantly used in the litera­
ture. This is because their absorption surface area can be 
measured directly (for laminar jet absorber, the surface 
area can be varied) [15,27] and the physical diffusivity of 
CO2 into the absorbent can be accurately determined 
[9,15,27]. In addition, the laminar jet absorber is suggested 
for the fast absorption process because of a short contact 
time generated in the jet absorber [15,27]. For toxic solvent, 
the stirred cell reactor is recommended, since it is a closed 
system [15,30]. Lastly, for novel solvents screening, the 
stopped-flow apparatus is suggested. This is because a 
very small quantity of solvent is required and the experi­
mental procedure is simple [30,31]. However, the limita­
tion is that only very low solvent concentration can be 
applied. In the work of Li et al. the solvent concentration 
is found to be in a range of 0.0026–0.196 M [31].

Kinetics of CO2 absorption
 Reaction kinetics is generally reported in terms of second-order rate constant (k2).
 The k2 can be extracted from CO2 absorption experiments conduct in a gas–liquid contactor (e.g., stirred cell reactor, wetted wall column, 
laminar jet absorber, wetted sphere absorber, string of disc contactor or stopped-flow apparatus).
Reaction mechanism
 A zwitterion mechanism is usually applicable for primary and secondary amines (i.e., primary sterically hindered and secondary cyclical

 Recently, a termolecular mechanism has been reintroduced and found to be applicable for several amines such as and diethylenetriamine 
(DETA), 2-([2-aminoethyl]amino)ethanol (AEEA) and MEA.
 Base-catalyzed hydration mechanism is usually applicable for tertiary amine such as triethanolamine and methyldiethanolamine (MDEA).
Kinetics model
 Comprehensive numerically solved and simplified models provide similar k2.
 The comprehensive numerically solved model seems to be more accurate than the simplified model because it takes into account the 
coupling between chemical equilibrium, mass-transfer and chemical kinetics of all possible chemical reactions.
Kinetics behavior
 The higher the k2, the faster the reaction kinetics.

the second-order reaction rate constant (k2). The values 
and correlations of k2 for several amine systems (i.e., both 
single and blended amines of aqueous and non-aqueous 
solutions) have been explicitly published in open litera­
ture. However, these kinetics models are mostly devel­
oped without considering the effect of CO2 loading or 
only considering it at the low CO2 loading range (<0.3). 
Therefore, the model should be expanded to the higher 
CO2 loading range.
Recently, one of the most comprehensive numerically 
solved CO2 absorption rate/kinetic models was established 
by the International Test Centre for CO2 Capture. The 
developed 1D and 2D models take into account the cou­
pling between chemical equilibrium, mass-transfer and 
chemical kinetics of all possible chemical reactions at 
various operating temperatures, amine concentrations, 
gas–liquid contact times and CO2 loadings. It is generally 
accepted that the CO2 absorption reaction is an exother­
mic reaction. This generated heat directly affects reaction 
kinetics in the absorption process. Therefore, more com­
prehensive kinetics models should be developed by incor­
porating the heat transfer equation into the calculation 
scheme. 
Generally, for both simplified and comprehensive 
numerically solved kinetics models, the concentration 
of chemical species is an estimated value from either 
Equation 39 or vapor–liquid equilibrium models. The 
experimental value of chemical species should be measured 
for the aid of generating more accurate kinetics models.
Moreover, the real industrially used amine-based CO2 
absorption processes not only have CO2–amine–water, 
but also amine degradation products, by­products from 
corrosion processes, degradation inhibitors and corrosion 
inhibitors. Therefore, further investigation on the effects 
of these chemical species on the reaction kinetics needs 
to be completed.
