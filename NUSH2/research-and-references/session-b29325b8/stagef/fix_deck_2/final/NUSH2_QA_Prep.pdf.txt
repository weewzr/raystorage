Questions 
Q1. Why 210 MWth? 
210 MWth is the rating the design holds. The process needs 178.5 MWth, so 31.5 MWth is 
margin that earns no revenue. 
The rating comes from the original project basis and is kept with Case B as the headline (decision D03). 
The sizing walk starts from a first sum of 206.0 MWth at INL Case A scale, which is 140.8 MWth at the 
100,000 Nm3 h−1 of Case B. Restoring the purge load and counting losses and circulators gives 
165.9 MWth. MEA’s reboiler gap gives 179.0 MWth, and syngas heat on its gated model brings the need 
to 178.5 MWth, within a range of 155.8 to 214.1 MWth. 
The rating stays because the need is uncertain. On Ahn and Lee’s heat basis the margin falls to 
6.5 MWth, and at the adverse corner the plant is 4.1 MWth short. The spare capacity is paid for: full cost 
is S$189.7/tCO2e charged at 210 MWth against S$161.2/tCO2e sized to need, at BOAK over 25 y. 
INL’s Case A cannot be the design at this rating. With MEA and recycle it needs 260.7 MWth, 50.7 MWth 
above the rating, so it stays a reference. 
If pressed: The reboiler gap rests on an SRD of 3.8 GJ t−1 with no syngas measurement behind it. 
Across 3.4 to 4.2 GJ t−1 the need moves from 174.1 to 182.9 MWth. 
Show: Slides 14, 15 and 16. 
Q2. Why HTGR instead of a PWR? 
Reforming needs heat near 871 °C, and of the reactor types compared here only a high-
temperature gas-cooled reactor reaches that band. 
On the process temperature chart, light-water reactors stop far short of the reforming band, which is 
why the design uses a gas-cooled reactor in place of a PWR. A helium-cooled HTGR with a 925 °C outlet 
gives secondary helium at 900 °C, and 925 °C is INL’s only case that removes the fired stage. INL’s yield 
optimum is 875 °C, with a fired stage kept. 
TRISO coatings are the first barrier to fission product release, provided fuel stays below about 1,600 °C, 
which leaves headroom above the outlet temperature. 
If pressed: Only test reactors of 46 MW or less have run this hot. Commercial HTR-PM, and Xe-100 as 
the analogue, run near 750 °C, so the 925 °C reactor here is generic and unproven at scale. 
Show: Slides 12, 13 and 35.

Q3. Why MEA instead of Selexol? 
MEA is the design basis (decision D25). The evidence is mixed, and the project’s own 
sensitivity favours Selexol slightly. 
IEAGHG 2022-07 calls MEA the preferred chemical solvent, while IEAGHG 2017-02 favours aMDEA on 
shifted syngas, and INL’s reference plant uses Selexol. The model runs 30 wt% MEA as the reference and 
carry Selexol and aMDEA as sensitivities. 
In the model Selexol abates 0.589 Mt y−1 at a full cost of S$176.3/tCO2e, against 0.578 Mt y−1 at 
S$189.7/tCO2e for MEA, at BOAK over 25 y. It also leaves 44.1 MWth spare against 31.5 MWth. aMDEA 
sits close to Selexol, at 0.589 Mt y−1 and S$187.8/tCO2e. Selexol would also keep the recycle INL actually 
modelled, which removes the largest process risk. 
If pressed: Say plainly that the numbers do not show MEA ahead. MEA stays the basis by design 
decision D25, and a switch to Selexol or aMDEA remains open. 
Show: Slides 10 and 31. 
Q4. How is MEA regeneration powered? 
By steam, taken in a fixed order, with reactor steam covering only the gap that remains. 
The reboiler needs 51.0 MW at an SRD of 3.8 GJ t−1. It draws first on the steam freed by removing 
Selexol, 12.7 MW, then on diverted export steam, 16.8 MW, then on recovered syngas heat, 8.4 MW. 
Reactor steam covers the remaining 13.1 MW, and that gap is the reboiler step of the sizing walk. 
Diverting export steam is a real cost: lost steam export adds S$15.2M/y at L5. Electricity for the MEA 
pumps and fans comes from the site power block, inside the 12.3 MWe of process loads. 
If pressed: SRD has no syngas measurement behind it. Across 3.4 to 4.2 GJ t−1 the reactor need moves 
from 174.1 to 182.9 MWth, and cost barely moves because the reactor is charged at its rating. 
Show: Slide 15; backup 52. 
Q5. Is the system thermally self-sufficient? 
Yes on the central balance: the reactor covers every heat and power load with 31.5 MWth to 
spare. 
The reactor needs 178.5 MWth against a 210 MWth rating. Unmet thermal duty is 0.0 MW and unmet 
electrical load is 0.0 MWe. A small power block supplies the 19.8 MWe site load, 12.3 MWe of it for 
process loads and the rest for helium circulators, so grid import at rated size is 0 MWe. 
If pressed: The margin is thin. It falls to 6.5 MWth on Ahn and Lee’s heat basis, and the adverse corner 
leaves the plant 4.1 MWth short. Ship liquefaction load for CO2 is unmodelled. The balance also rests on 
the IHX delivering 900 °C, which no measured unit has done. Outside the 85% availability hours there is 
no hydrogen unless backup firing is added, a sensitivity whose furnace capital is unpriced. 
Show: Slides 13, 15, 16 and 20. 
Q6. Why exclude PSA tail gas? 
It is no longer excluded. The PSA stays outside the boundary as an unsimulated block, and its 
reject now returns across the boundary to be recycled (decision D04).

A recycle compressor feeds the reject to a cryogenic unit. Its methane-rich cut returns to the reformer 
feed and its hydrogen goes back towards the PSA, while the nitrogen-rich cut is burned and vented. 
That stack is the plant’s only residual source: 38,309 tCO2 y−1, or 0.57 kg CO2 kg−1 H2. The PSA purge 
load is restored in the power balance. 
Recycle is why NUS-H2 takes 23.2 MMSCFD of gas against 35.9 MMSCFD for a same-size unabated 
steam methane reforming plant. 
If pressed: INL modelled recycle with Selexol, never with MEA. If it fails to transfer, gas rises to 
30.4 MMSCFD and abatement falls to 0.467 Mt y−1. Full cost then rises to S$283.1/tCO2e. INL also never 
modelled the larger CO2 slip that MEA sends through the cryogenic unit, which stays an open risk. 
Show: Slides 6, 9, 28 and 31. 
Q7. What are the safety barriers? 
TRISO fuel coatings come first, inside the reactor’s own barriers, and the IHX is the boundary 
to the chemical plant. 
TRISO coatings are the first barrier to fission product release, provided fuel stays below about 1,600 °C. 
HTTR counts four barriers: coatings, pressure vessel, containment and reactor building. Tritium is the 
known exception, since it can leave cracked coatings and also forms in graphite. 
The IHX tubes separate the nuclear island from the process helium loop. Secondary helium is held 
slightly above primary pressure, so a leak runs towards the reactor, and purification with monitoring 
manages tritium permeation. Isolation needs a high-temperature emergency shutoff valve, which the 
figure omits. 
The emergency planning zone follows source term and dose, so only a design-specific safety case can 
size it, and no distance is claimed. That case must also cover process hazards beside the reactor, such as 
fire and toxic release. 
If pressed: A thermal buffer for a reformer trip is still open in the design. 
Show: Slides 18, 19 and 21. 
Q8. Why Singapore? 
Singapore runs on imported gas and plans for hydrogen, so low-carbon hydrogen made here 
meets a national need. 
Natural gas made up 94% of the electricity fuel mix in 2024, and the national hydrogen strategy sees 
low-carbon hydrogen supplying up to 50% of power needs by 2050. Hydrogen is already made at 
industrial scale on Jurong Island, and SOXAL’s plant sets the plant size of 100,000 Nm3 h−1, used for size 
only. 
Singapore is also building the capability to decide on nuclear energy: the IAEA INIR Phase 1 assessment 
is tentatively set for 2027 and covers 19 areas. 
If pressed: Singapore has made no decision on nuclear energy. CO2 has to be stored abroad under the 
Singapore-Indonesia arrangement, and the accounting across the border is unresolved. For scale only, 
the abatement equals 1.2 to 1.3% of Singapore’s 2035 target level. 
Show: Slides 1, 2 and 22.

Q9. Why Western Island? 
It is the author’s proposal, with no official approval: new land planned for energy and 
industry, linked to Jurong Island. 
The new western island from the 2026 National Day Rally is planned for future energy and industry use 
among others, with a planned link to Jurong Island, where gas supply and hydrogen users already sit. 
The plant is a new build there, compared with a same-size unabated steam methane reforming plant. 
If pressed: The government page does not mention nuclear. Reclamation runs over coming decades, so 
the land may not exist at the milestone dates, and every link to the island is unpriced. 
Show: Slides 24 and 25. 
Q10. How realistic is the 2050 roadmap? 
Possible on paper. Every date is illustrative, and each gate opens only if the stage before it 
delivers. 
The roadmap has four gated stages: national readiness from 2027, reactor and process-heat 
qualification from 2030, project development and an investment decision from 2035, then construction 
from 2039 towards potential operation by 2050. The construction window leans on the 11 years HTR-
PM took from construction start to commercial operation. 
If pressed: The weakest links are the IHX and the land. The highest measured IHX secondary 
temperature in the sources held is 859 °C on a 10 MW unit, against the 900 °C the design needs, and 
reclamation has no published date. The first plant would also be first of a kind, at a full cost of 
S$277.9/tCO2e over 25 y. 
Show: Slides 22, 25 and 20. 
Q11. Does the project satisfy the emissions target? 
Yes. Net abatement is 0.578 MtCO2e y−1 against a target of more than 0.250 Mt y−1, 2.3 
times the target. 
This counts direct emissions to atmosphere against a same-size unabated steam methane reforming 
plant on INL’s 9.21 kg CO2 kg−1 H2 baseline, a 93.8% cut. The plant captures 90.4% of its carbon at 90% 
per pass, and 218,274 tCO2 y−1 never forms. With the storage chain burden charged the result is 
0.569 Mt y−1. Cho’s and IEAGHG’s baselines give 0.587 and 0.564 Mt y−1. 
Every stress case with working capture stays above the target: 0.442 Mt y−1 at 65% availability, and 
0.467 Mt y−1 if recycle fails. 
If pressed: With capture offline only 0.218 Mt y−1 remains, which misses the target, so capture has to 
work. Abatement is counted at the Singapore stack while the CO2 is stored abroad. 
Show: Slides 28, 29, 30 and 31. 
Q12. Does the project satisfy the cost target? 
At central inputs, no: 0 of 8 central cases reach S$100/tCO2e. In the named scenario 
MATURE_60Y, a 10th‑of‑a‑kind plant on INL’s BOAK learning route over 60 y meets it at 
S$96/tCO2e when gas costs S$20.7/GJ, above the S$10 to S$20/GJ range tested.

The full cost by the annual ledger is S$189.7/tCO2e at BOAK over 25 y and S$165.6/tCO2e over 60 y. A 
first of a kind costs S$277.9/tCO2e and S$241.7/tCO2e. The discounted cash flow agrees within 0.47%

Hydrogen costs S$5.26/kg against S$3.62/kg unabated. 
No single lever closes the gap. Gas would have to reach S$30.7/GJ, about 2.0 times the central price. 
Reactor capital would have to fall to US$824/kWt, under INL’s low of US$1,456/kWt. Transport and 
storage would have to go negative. 
Three conditions together cross the target, and each is an engine run. A 10th‑of‑a‑kind reactor on INL’s 
BOAK learning route takes BOAK over 25 y from S$189.7/tCO2e to S$146.3/tCO2e. A 60 y life takes it to 
S$128.2/tCO2e. Gas at S$20.7/GJ then gives S$95.6/tCO2e on the ledger and S$95.9/tCO2e by cash flow, 
under the target at the S$96/tCO2e point. The engine solves that gas price at S$20.63/GJ on the ledger 
and S$20.68/GJ by cash flow, and the slide prints the larger, rounded up. That price lies above the S$10 
to S$20/GJ range tested. At S$20/GJ, the top of that range, the scenario gives S$99.6/tCO2e on the 
ledger and S$99.9/tCO2e by cash flow. Higher gas lowers the abatement cost because NUS-H2 burns 
less gas than the unabated plant. 
If pressed: The S$96/tCO2e case is a set of conditions on the base, and the base stays the reference: 
S$189.7/tCO2e on the ledger and S$190.1/tCO2e by cash flow. Do not quote the named step L3 as the 
answer either. L3, at S$137.7/tCO2e, leaves out real costs of this plant such as construction interest and 
lost steam export, and it still misses.

Q13. Could public co-funding bring the cost down? 
Yes, at a price to the public budget. Co-funding S$483.0M, 65.0% of the S$742.8M reactor 
capital, brings BOAK over 25 y to S$96/tCO2e on the ledger. 
The cash flow needs S$483.6M, 65.1%. Over 60 y the ledger needs S$416.0M, 56.0%, and the cash flow 
S$417.1M, 56.2%. These are engine solves on the BOAK case at n = 1.0 with 210 MWth charged. 
If pressed: Co-funding moves cost to the public budget. The total resource cost stays the same, so it is 
a policy choice and leaves the engineering result unchanged. 
Show: Slide 39; Q&A slide 72. 
Q14. Which assumptions dominate uncertainty? 
Recycle transfer to MEA and the reactor cost treatment move the result most. The IHX 
temperature decides whether the design works at all. 
• 
Recycle transfer to MEA. Without it abatement is 0.467 Mt y−1 and full cost S$283.1/tCO2e, against 
0.578 Mt y−1 and S$189.7/tCO2e. 
• 
Reactor size law. At BOAK over 25 y, full cost is S$189.7/tCO2e at n = 1.0, S$277.7/tCO2e at n = 0.6 
and S$368.7/tCO2e at n = 0.31. 
• 
Build maturity. A first of a kind carries a 1.6 times premium on reactor capital, which gives 
S$277.9/tCO2e against S$189.7/tCO2e. INL’s low and high reactor capital give S$141.7/tCO2e and 
S$213.8/tCO2e. 
• 
Availability. 85% has no measured HTGR record behind it. At 65% abatement falls to 0.442 Mt y−1 
and full cost rises to S$253.0/tCO2e.
