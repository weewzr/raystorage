=== n1
Switch on a light in Singapore tonight. In 2024, natural gas made up 94 percent of the fuel behind that electricity, and we import all of it.
=== n2
The national hydrogen strategy points one way out. Low-carbon hydrogen could supply up to 50 percent of our power needs by 2050. Everything then rests on the word low-carbon.
=== n3
Steam methane reforming is the established route. It burns gas for heat, and it strips carbon from the gas it converts. On Idaho National Laboratory’s numbers, every kilogram of hydrogen leaves 9.21 kilograms of CO₂ in the air.
=== n4
This is NUS-H2: Nuclear-Upgraded Steam Methane Reforming with Carbon Capture, for low-carbon hydrogen in Singapore. I am Wee Zhern Ray, and the question here is how far a conventional reformer can go once a reactor replaces its furnace.
=== n5
Our project sets two tests for a solution at scale in Singapore: abate more than 0.250 million tonnes of CO₂e a year, at under S$100 a tonne. Here is the answer up front. The abatement target is met. The cost target is missed at central inputs. The rest of this talk shows by how much, and what would close the gap.
=== n6
We size one new-build plant at 100,000 normal cubic metres of hydrogen an hour, the scale of SOXAL’s plant on Jurong Island, used for size only. At 85 percent availability that is 66,925 tonnes a year. The model is steady state. The PSA stays an external, unsimulated block, and its reject returns across our boundary as a stream.
=== n7
Here is the plant. On the left, a generic high-temperature gas-cooled reactor rated at 210 megawatts thermal, with helium leaving at 925 degrees. An intermediate heat exchanger, the IHX, passes that heat to a separate helium loop at 900 degrees, which heats the reformer tubes. Shift and MEA capture follow, then CO₂ compression and ship offshore storage. The PSA sits outside the dashed line.
=== n8
First proof point. The process needs 120.8 megawatts of heat, covering reforming and preheat, at 900 degrees. In INL’s study, 925 degrees is the only case that removes the fired stage. The reformer outlet of 871 degrees is INL’s equilibrium assumption, and we disclose it as one.
=== n9
Second proof point. A same-size unabated reformer takes 35.9 million standard cubic feet of gas a day. NUS-H2 takes 23.2, because no gas burns for reforming heat and PSA reject recycles. The published evidence disagrees on this saving: 35.2 percent on INL’s basis, 18.8 percent in Ahn and Lee. INL also never modelled recycle with MEA, and we come back to that.
=== n10
Third proof point. The absorber takes 90 percent of the CO₂ on each pass, a project assumption, using 30 weight percent MEA. Absorber slip recycles through the reformer, so the whole plant captures 90.4 percent of its carbon. We print both numbers together so the two never get mixed up.
=== n11
You need not take our word for this. INL modelled reactor heat for reforming in 2010, and the line on screen is theirs. At 925 degrees they found 30.9 percent less gas than a conventional plant with capture. INL used Selexol, and it has never reviewed NUS-H2. We borrow its method as our anchor, along with its limits.
=== n12
Why nuclear? Reforming sits in the hot band on this chart. Light-water reactors stop far short of it, which is why we use a gas-cooled reactor in place of a PWR. INL’s yield optimum is 875 degrees with a fired stage kept, and 925 is its only case without one. The catch: only test reactors of 46 megawatts or less have run this hot, and commercial HTR-PM runs near 750.
=== n13
Fission gives us heat with no combustion at the reformer. The same reactor also runs the site: a small power block supplies 19.8 megawatts electric for process loads and helium circulators, so grid import at rated size is 0.
=== n14
Sizing starts from INL’s Case 6, at 130 million standard cubic feet of hydrogen a day. We call it Case A, and it stays a reference, separate from our design. With MEA and recycle, Case A would need 260.7 megawatts thermal, 50.7 above our rating. So we size the plant to fit.
=== n15
So we scale to Case B and walk the reactor size. The first sum was 206.0 at Case A scale, which is 140.8 at ours. Restoring the purge load and counting losses and circulators brings it to 165.9. MEA’s reboiler gap gives 179.0, and syngas heat on its gated model brings the need to 178.5. That reboiler demand, 3.8 gigajoules per tonne, has no syngas measurement behind it.
=== n16
We keep the 210 megawatt rating, so 31.5 megawatts sit spare and earn nothing. We still pay for them: full cost is S$189.7 a tonne as charged, against S$161.2 sized to need. The margin is also thin. On Ahn and Lee’s heat basis it falls to 6.5, and at our adverse corner the need reaches 214.1.
=== n17
Join the two plants and each one sheds equipment. The reformer loses its fired furnace. The reactor loses its large condensing power plant and keeps a small power block for site loads. With the furnace gone and fuel gas recycled, 218,274 tonnes of baseline CO₂ a year never forms.
=== n18
Now the risks, starting with safety. TRISO coatings are the first barrier to fission product release, provided fuel stays below about 1,600 degrees. HTTR counts four barriers: coatings, pressure vessel, containment and reactor building. Tritium is the known exception, since it can leave cracked coatings and also forms in graphite.
=== n19
The IHX is the first boundary between the nuclear island and the process loop. Its tubes are a pressure boundary, and they remove nothing. Secondary helium is held slightly above primary pressure, so a leak runs towards the reactor. Purification and monitoring manage tritium permeation. Isolation needs a high-temperature emergency shutoff valve, which our figure omits. A thermal buffer for a reformer trip is also still open in our design.
=== n20
This is our largest technical gap. The highest measured IHX secondary temperature in our sources is 859 degrees, on a 10 megawatt HTTR unit. We need 900 degrees at an IHX duty of 121.7 megawatts, and no source we hold shows an IHX heating a chemical plant. Our 25 kelvin approach is INL’s assumption. At 50 kelvin, the IAEA’s typical value, the reformer pinch falls from 29 to 4 kelvin, and the no-furnace claim is at risk.
=== n21
Emergency planning matters most in a country our size. A low-power TRISO HTGR may justify a smaller emergency planning zone, subject to a design-specific safety case. The zone follows source term and dose, so we claim no distance. The same case must also cover process hazards beside the reactor, such as fire and toxic release.
=== n22
On regulation and people, Singapore is building the capability to decide. The IAEA’s INIR Phase 1 assessment is tentatively set for 2027 and covers 19 areas. On 26 October 2026, METI and EMA launched manpower schemes for public officers and selected companies. Government is clear that no decision on nuclear energy has been made.
=== n23
Public acceptance is a precondition. The Singapore Nuclear Research and Safety Institute runs public lectures open to anyone, including students. That outreach has to run long before any site decision.
=== n24
Our proposed site is the new western island from the 2026 National Day Rally, linked to Jurong Island’s industry. This is my proposal. It has no official approval, and the government page does not mention nuclear. Reclamation runs over coming decades, so land may not exist at our milestone dates, and the links to the island are unpriced.
=== n25
Our roadmap runs from 2027 to 2050 in four stages, and every date is illustrative. National readiness comes first, then reactor and process-heat qualification, then project development and an investment decision, then construction. The 2039 start leans on the 11 years HTR-PM took from construction to commercial operation. Each gate opens only if the stage before it delivers.
=== n26
Emissions now, and the boundary comes first. Inside the dashed line sit the reactor, helium loops, reformer, shift, capture, compression and ship offshore storage. The PSA sits outside, with its reject crossing back in. Upstream gas and construction are excluded. We count abatement at the Singapore stack. Storage abroad rests on the Singapore-Indonesia arrangement, and its accounting is unresolved.
=== n27
The method is a mass balance at equal hydrogen. The comparator is a same-size new-build unabated steam methane reforming plant at 9.21 kilograms per kilogram, or 616,361 tonnes a year. Cho’s 9.35, from 33 US plants, and IEAGHG’s 8.996 bound it. Net abatement is that baseline less what reaches the atmosphere, and hydrogen runs only in the 85 percent availability hours.
=== n28
Absorber slip recycles through the reformer, so the residual comes from one place: the burned nitrogen-rich cut at the cryogenic unit. That stack emits 38,309 tonnes a year, 0.57 kilograms per kilogram of hydrogen. Another 359,777 tonnes goes to storage.
=== n29
Baseline less residual gives 0.578 million tonnes a year, a 93.8 percent cut against that unabated plant. That is 2.3 times the target of 0.250. Charge the storage chain’s burden and it reads 0.569. For scale only, the result equals 1.2 to 1.3 percent of Singapore’s 2035 target level.
=== n30
We stressed one input at a time. At 80 percent per-pass capture we get 0.564. At 65 percent availability, 0.442. Lifecycle burdens give 0.566, and counting lost steam export gives 0.528. All clear the target. These corner runs carry no probability, and 85 percent has no measured HTGR record behind it.
=== n31
One assumption matters most. If recycle fails to transfer from INL’s Selexol case to MEA, and we hold hydrogen output, abatement falls to 0.467 and gas rises to 30.4. That still clears the target. With capture offline, only the carbon that never forms counts, 0.218, which misses it. So capture has to work.
=== n32
What does the market compare us with? Singapore’s carbon tax is S$45 a tonne in 2026 and 2027, with a stated view to S$50 to S$80 by 2030. Tax is a transfer, so it stays outside our cost metric. No hydrogen offtake is contracted, and there is competition: Air Liquide and Aster have an MoU on autothermal reforming with up to 99 percent capture here.
=== n33
Now the cost. We use two methods at equal hydrogen output. An annual ledger adds each cost difference against the unabated steam methane reforming plant. A discounted cash flow runs both plants year by year at 8 percent. They differ by at most 0.47 percent over all 72 points. Both share one set of inputs, so that agreement tests our annualisation and does not validate the inputs.
=== n34
These inputs drive the result. Gas is S$15 per gigajoule. Transport and storage is S$60 a tonne, between S$23.2 and S$112, and no Singapore-Indonesia tariff has been published. Reactor capital is US$2,184 per kilowatt thermal. Capture capital is bottom-up on an aMDEA basis, so an MEA plant could cost more. The full table is in backup.
=== n35
We price a generic 925 degree HTGR on INL’s recipe, charged at 210 megawatts. BOAK is INL’s term for a build between a first and an Nth of a kind, and it gives S$743 million. A first of a kind, FOAK, carries a 1.6 times premium, S$1,188 million, and that is what a single plant would be. The Xe-100 shown is an analogue only, with a 750 degree outlet and no vendor quotation.
=== n36
How a 600 megawatt estimate scales down to 210 is uncertain, so we print three treatments at equal size. n of 1.0 is INL’s recipe and our central case. n of 0.6 is a project assumption. n of 0.31 is the ratio of two first-of-a-kind estimates. At BOAK and 25 years, full cost runs from S$189.7 to S$368.7 a tonne.
=== n37
Here is the money. Unabated hydrogen costs S$3.62 a kilogram. Over 25 years NUS-H2 costs S$5.26 at BOAK and S$6.02 at FOAK. Over 60 years, S$5.05 and S$5.71. That is a BOAK premium of S$1.64 a kilogram, which a buyer would have to carry. Both plants carry the same reformer island from IEAGHG, on the assumption that reformer differences cancel.
=== n38
This ladder shows what each line adds at BOAK over 25 years. Gas saved pays back S$64.8 million a year. Reactor capital costs S$69.6 million. Stop at the named step L3 and you read S$137.7 a tonne. Construction interest and lost steam export are real costs of this plant too, so the full figure is S$189.7.
=== n39
Here is the levelised abatement cost from the cash flow. BOAK is S$190.1 at 25 years and S$166.0 at 60. FOAK is S$278.4 and S$242.2. Against S$100, the target is missed: 0 of 8 central cases pass.
=== n40
What would S$100 take? One lever at a time, at BOAK and 25 years, we are S$51.9 million a year over the ceiling. Gas would have to reach S$30.7 per gigajoule, 2.0 times our central price. Reactor cost would have to fall to US$824, under INL’s low of 1,456. Transport and storage would have to go negative. Sizing to need reaches only S$161.2. No single lever closes it.
=== n41
In the named scenario MATURE_60Y, a 10th‑of‑a‑kind plant on INL’s BOAK learning route, run for 60 years, meets the S$100 target at S$96 a tonne when gas costs S$20.7 a gigajoule: S$95.6 on the ledger and S$95.9 by cash flow. Dearer gas lowers the cost because NUS-H2 burns less gas. That price is above the range we tested, and at S$20, its top, the scenario gives S$99.6 and S$99.9. The base stays on screen: BOAK over 25 years costs S$189.7 and S$190.1, and misses.
=== n42
Every bar is an engine run, ledger first. From the base of S$189.7, a 10th‑of‑a‑kind reactor on INL’s BOAK learning route and a 60 year life take off S$43.4 and S$18.1. Gas at S$20.7, above the range we tested, then takes off S$32.6, to S$95.6, under the line at the S$96 point. These are conditions on the base, which stays our reference.
=== n43
So is it feasible? On carbon, yes, on this boundary. On cost, the target is missed at central inputs and met only in the mature case, at a gas price above the range we tested. On technology, the plant rests on an IHX nobody has built at this duty. On siting, it rests on land and a national decision that do not yet exist. NUS-H2 is a credible carbon route whose cost closes only for a mature plant, after demonstration overseas.
=== n44
Now the limits of our own numbers. Many results are reduced-order balances anchored on INL cases. Of 650 validation rows, 360 are independent of the source data, and only 92 of those pass. 32 fail and stay on the record. Of the rest, 251 have no limit to test against and 42 did not run. The list on screen is what the next stage has to close.
=== n45
NUS-H2 is presented by its author, Wee Zhern Ray.
=== n46
To close. NUS-H2 heats a reformer with a 210 megawatt HTGR and captures CO₂ with MEA. It abates 0.578 million tonnes a year, so the carbon target is met. Abatement costs S$189.7 a tonne at BOAK and S$277.9 at FOAK, so the cost target is missed at the base. A 10th‑of‑a‑kind plant over 60 years meets it at S$96 with gas at S$20.7, above the range we tested. The next steps are IHX qualification overseas and a design-specific safety case. Thank you.
