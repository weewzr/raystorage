P: Script
P: HOOK   0:00 to 0:48
P: 1. Switch on a light tonight
P: 0:00 to 0:14, 14 s. On screen: 94% natural gas in the electricity fuel mix, 2024.
P: Switch on a light in Singapore tonight. In 2024, natural gas made up 94 percent of the fuel behind that electricity, and we import all of it.
P: 2. Hydrogen could carry up to half
P: 0:14 to 0:29, 15 s. On screen: up to 50% of power needs by 2050.
P: The national hydrogen strategy points one way out. Low-carbon hydrogen could supply up to 50 percent of our power needs by 2050. Everything then rests on the word low-carbon.
P: 3. Today’s hydrogen carries a carbon bill
P: 0:29 to 0:48, 19 s. On screen: 9.21 kg CO2 kg−1 H2.
P: Steam methane reforming is the established route. It burns gas for heat, and it strips carbon from the gas it converts. On Idaho National Laboratory’s numbers, every kilogram of hydrogen leaves 9.21 kilograms of CO2 in the air.
P: NAME REVEAL   0:48 to 1:07
P: 4. NUS-H2
P: 0:48 to 1:07, 19 s.
P: This is NUS-H2: Nuclear-Upgraded Steam Methane Reforming with Carbon Capture, for low-carbon hydrogen in Singapore. I am Wee Zhern Ray, and the question here is how far a conventional reformer can go once a reactor replaces its furnace.
P: SCOPE   1:07 to 2:04
P: 5. Two targets, one straight answer
P: 1:07 to 1:36, 29 s. On screen: 0.250 Mt y−1 and S$100/tCO2e.
P: Our project sets two tests for a solution at scale in Singapore: abate more than 0.250 million tonnes of CO2e a year, at under S$100 a tonne. Here is the answer up front. The abatement target is met. The cost target is missed at central inputs. The rest of this talk shows by how much, and what would close the gap.
P: 6. One plant at real industrial scale
P: 1:36 to 2:04, 28 s. On screen: 100,000 Nm3 h−1 of purified hydrogen.
P: We size one new-build plant at 100,000 normal cubic metres of hydrogen an hour, the scale of SOXAL’s plant on Jurong Island, used for size only. At 85 percent availability that is 66,925 tonnes a year. The model is steady state. The PSA stays an external, unsimulated block, and its reject returns across our boundary as a stream.
P: WHAT IT IS   2:04 to 2:34
P: 7. A reformer heated by helium
P: 2:04 to 2:34, 30 s. On screen: 210 MWth reactor.
P: Here is the plant. On the left, a generic high-temperature gas-cooled reactor rated at 210 megawatts thermal, with helium leaving at 925 degrees. An intermediate heat exchanger, the IHX, passes that heat to a separate helium loop at 900 degrees, which heats the reformer tubes. Shift and MEA capture follow, then CO2 compression and ship offshore storage. The PSA sits outside the dashed line.
P: PROOF   2:34 to 3:51
P: 8. Helium heat replaces the furnace
P: 2:34 to 2:57, 23 s. On screen: 120.8 MWth at 900 °C.
P: First proof point. The process needs 120.8 megawatts of heat, covering reforming and preheat, at 900 degrees. In INL’s study, 925 degrees is the only case that removes the fired stage. The reformer outlet of 871 degrees is INL’s equilibrium assumption, and we disclose it as one.
P: 9. Less gas for the same hydrogen
P: 2:57 to 3:26, 30 s. On screen: 23.2 against 35.9 MMSCFD.
P: Second proof point. A same-size unabated reformer takes 35.9 million standard cubic feet of gas a day. NUS-H2 takes 23.2, because no gas burns for reforming heat and PSA reject recycles. The published evidence disagrees on this saving: 35.2 percent on INL’s basis, 18.8 percent in Ahn and Lee. INL also never modelled recycle with MEA, and we come back to that.
P: 10. Capture, counted two ways
P: 3:26 to 3:51, 24 s. On screen: 90% per pass and 90.4% of plant carbon.
P: Third proof point. The absorber takes 90 percent of the CO2 on each pass, a project assumption, using 30 weight percent MEA. Absorber slip recycles through the reformer, so the whole plant captures 90.4 percent of its carbon. We print both numbers together so the two never get mixed up.
P: EXPERT VOICE   3:51 to 4:18
P: 11. In INL’s own words
P: 3:51 to 4:18, 28 s. On screen: 30.9% less gas than INL’s conventional plant with capture, at 925 °C.
P: You need not take our word for this. INL modelled reactor heat for reforming in 2010, and the line on screen is theirs. At 925 degrees they found 30.9 percent less gas than a conventional plant with capture. INL used Selexol, and it has never reviewed NUS-H2. We borrow its method as our anchor, along with its limits.
P: WHY NUCLEAR   4:18 to 5:11
P: 12. Why nuclear: the temperature fits
P: 4:18 to 4:52, 33 s. On screen: 925 °C reactor outlet.
P: Why nuclear? Reforming sits in the hot band on this chart. Light-water reactors stop far short of it, which is why we use a gas-cooled reactor in place of a PWR. INL’s yield optimum is 875 degrees with a fired stage kept, and 925 is its only case without one. The catch: only test reactors of 46 megawatts or less have run this hot, and commercial HTR-PM runs near 750.
P: 13. Heat and power from one reactor
P: 4:52 to 5:11, 19 s. On screen: 19.8 MWe site load, grid import 0.
P: Fission gives us heat with no combustion at the reformer. The same reactor also runs the site: a small power block supplies 19.8 megawatts electric for process loads and helium circulators, so grid import at rated size is 0.
P: SIZING   5:11 to 6:32
P: 14. INL reference: Case A
P: 5:11 to 5:36, 25 s. On screen: 130 MMSCFD hydrogen, INL reference.
P: Sizing starts from INL’s Case 6, at 130 million standard cubic feet of hydrogen a day. We call it Case A, and it stays a reference, separate from our design. With MEA and recycle, Case A would need 260.7 megawatts thermal, 50.7 above our rating. So we size the plant to fit.
P: 15. The walk to 178.5 MWth
P: 5:36 to 6:05, 29 s. On screen: 178.5 MWth needed, range 155.8 to 214.1.
P: So we scale to Case B and walk the reactor size. The team’s first sum was 206.0 at Case A scale, which is 140.8 at ours. Restoring the purge load and counting losses and circulators brings it to 165.9. MEA’s reboiler gap takes the need to 178.5. That reboiler demand, 3.8 gigajoules per tonne, has no syngas measurement behind it.
P: 16. A margin that earns nothing
P: 6:05 to 6:32, 27 s. On screen: 31.5 MWth spare, no revenue.
P: We keep the 210 megawatt rating, so 31.5 megawatts sit spare and earn nothing. We still pay for them: full cost is S$189.7 a tonne as charged, against S$161.2 sized to need. The margin is also thin. On Ahn and Lee’s heat basis it falls to 6.5, and at our adverse corner the need reaches 214.1.
P: BENEFITS   6:32 to 6:55
P: 17. Integration removes equipment
P: 6:32 to 6:55, 24 s. On screen: 218,274 tCO2 y−1 never formed.
P: Join the two plants and each one sheds equipment. The reformer loses its fired furnace. The reactor loses its large condensing power plant and keeps a small power block for site loads. With the furnace gone and fuel gas recycled, 218,274 tonnes of baseline CO2 a year never forms.
P: SAFETY   6:55 to 8:54
P: 18. TRISO: the first barrier
P: 6:55 to 7:20, 24 s. On screen: 1,600 °C fuel limit.
P: Now the risks, starting with safety. TRISO coatings are the first barrier to fission product release, provided fuel stays below about 1,600 degrees. HTTR counts four barriers: coatings, pressure vessel, containment and reactor building. Tritium is the known exception, since it can leave cracked coatings and also forms in graphite.
P: 19. The IHX: the boundary we must isolate
P: 7:20 to 7:53, 33 s.
P: The IHX is the first boundary between the nuclear island and the process loop. Its tubes are a pressure boundary, and they remove nothing. Secondary helium is held slightly above primary pressure, so a leak runs towards the reactor. Purification and monitoring manage tritium permeation. Isolation needs a high-temperature emergency shutoff valve, which our figure omits. A thermal buffer for a reformer trip is also still open in our design.
P: 20. 900 °C helium is still unproven
P: 7:53 to 8:28, 35 s. On screen: 859 °C measured against 900 °C needed.
P: This is our largest technical gap. The highest measured IHX secondary temperature in our sources is 859 degrees, on a 10 megawatt HTTR unit. We need 900 degrees at 12 times that duty, and no source we hold shows an IHX heating a chemical plant. Our 25 kelvin approach is INL’s assumption. At 50 kelvin, the IAEA’s typical value, the reformer pinch falls from 29 to 4 kelvin, and the no-furnace claim is at risk.
P: 21. Emergency zone: set by a safety case
P: 8:28 to 8:54, 26 s.
P: Emergency planning matters most in a country our size. A low-power TRISO HTGR may justify a smaller emergency planning zone, subject to a design-specific safety case. The zone follows source term and dose, so we claim no distance. The same case must also cover process hazards beside the reactor, such as fire and toxic release.
P: REGULATION   8:54 to 9:20
P: 22. Singapore is building capability
P: 8:54 to 9:20, 26 s. On screen: 2027, INIR Phase 1 assessment, tentative date, 19 areas.
P: On regulation and people, Singapore is building the capability to decide. The IAEA’s INIR Phase 1 assessment is tentatively set for 2027 and covers 19 areas. On 26 October 2026, METI and EMA launched manpower schemes for public officers and selected companies. Government is clear that no decision on nuclear energy has been made.
P: COMMUNITY   9:20 to 9:36
P: 23. Acceptance has to be earned
P: 9:20 to 9:36, 15 s.
P: Public acceptance is a precondition. The Singapore Nuclear Research and Safety Institute runs public lectures open to anyone, including students. That outreach has to run long before any site decision.
P: LOCATION   9:36 to 10:04
P: 24. Proposed site: Western Island
P: 9:36 to 10:04, 28 s.
P: Our proposed site is the new western island from the 2026 National Day Rally, linked to Jurong Island’s industry. This is the team’s proposal. It has no official approval, and the government page does not mention nuclear. Reclamation runs over coming decades, so land may not exist at our milestone dates, and the links to the island are unpriced.
P: ROADMAP   10:04 to 10:32
P: 25. 2027 to 2050, every date illustrative
P: 10:04 to 10:32, 28 s. On screen: 2050, potential operation, illustrative.
P: Our roadmap runs from 2027 to 2050 in four stages, and every date is illustrative. National readiness comes first, then reactor and process-heat qualification, then project development and an investment decision, then construction. The 2039 start leans on the 11 years HTR-PM took from construction to commercial operation. Each gate opens only if the stage before it delivers.
P: EMISSIONS BOUNDARY   10:32 to 11:00
P: 26. Where we draw the line
P: 10:32 to 11:00, 28 s.
P: Emissions now, and the boundary comes first. Inside the dashed line sit the reactor, helium loops, reformer, shift, capture, compression and ship offshore storage. The PSA sits outside, with its reject crossing back in. Upstream gas and construction are excluded. We count abatement at the Singapore stack. Storage abroad rests on the Singapore-Indonesia arrangement, and its accounting is unresolved.
P: EMISSIONS METHOD   11:00 to 11:29
P: 27. Same hydrogen, two plants
P: 11:00 to 11:29, 29 s. On screen: 616,361 tCO2 y−1 baseline.
P: The method is a mass balance at equal hydrogen. The comparator is a same-size new-build unabated steam methane reforming plant at 9.21 kilograms per kilogram, or 616,361 tonnes a year. Cho’s 9.35, from 33 US plants, and IEAGHG’s 8.996 bound it. Net abatement is that baseline less what reaches the atmosphere, and hydrogen runs only in the 85 percent availability hours.
P: EMISSIONS RESULT   11:29 to 12:13
P: 28. What still reaches the air
P: 11:29 to 11:49, 20 s. On screen: 38,309 tCO2 y−1 to atmosphere.
P: Absorber slip recycles through the reformer, so the residual comes from one place: the burned nitrogen-rich cut at the cryogenic unit. That stack emits 38,309 tonnes a year, 0.57 kilograms per kilogram of hydrogen. Another 359,777 tonnes goes to storage.
P: 29. Net abatement clears the target
P: 11:49 to 12:13, 24 s. On screen: 0.578 MtCO2e y−1, 93.8% below unabated steam methane reforming.
P: Baseline less residual gives 0.578 million tonnes a year, a 93.8 percent cut against that unabated plant. That is 2.3 times the target of 0.250. Charge the storage chain’s burden and it reads 0.569. For scale only, the result equals 1.2 to 1.3 percent of Singapore’s 2035 target level.
P: SENSITIVITY   12:13 to 13:03
P: 30. Stress tests hold above target
P: 12:13 to 12:37, 25 s. On screen: 0.442 Mt y−1 at 65% availability.
P: We stressed one input at a time. At 80 percent per-pass capture we get 0.564. At 65 percent availability, 0.442. Lifecycle burdens give 0.566, and counting lost steam export gives 0.528. All clear the target. These corner runs carry no probability, and 85 percent has no measured HTGR record behind it.
P: 31. The assumption that matters most
P: 12:37 to 13:03, 26 s. On screen: 0.467 Mt y−1 if recycle fails.
P: One assumption matters most. If recycle fails to transfer from INL’s Selexol case to MEA, and we hold hydrogen output, abatement falls to 0.467 and gas rises to 30.4. That still clears the target. With capture offline, only the carbon that never forms counts, 0.218, which misses it. So capture has to work.
P: MARKET   13:03 to 13:34
P: 32. What the market compares us with
P: 13:03 to 13:34, 31 s. On screen: S$45/tCO2e carbon tax, 2026 to 2027.
P: What does the market compare us with? Singapore’s carbon tax is S$45 a tonne in 2026 and 2027, with a stated view to S$50 to S$80 by 2030. Tax is a transfer, so it stays outside our cost metric. No hydrogen offtake is contracted, and there is competition: Air Liquide and Aster have an MoU on autothermal reforming with up to 99 percent capture here.
P: ECONOMICS METHOD   13:34 to 14:04
P: 33. Cost, rebuilt two ways
P: 13:34 to 14:04, 30 s. On screen: 0.2% largest gap between the methods.
P: Now the cost. We use two methods at equal hydrogen output. An annual ledger adds each cost difference against the unabated steam methane reforming plant. A discounted cash flow runs both plants year by year at 8 percent. They differ by at most 0.2 percent. Both share one set of inputs, so that agreement tests our annualisation and does not validate the inputs.
P: COST ASSUMPTIONS   14:04 to 14:31
P: 34. The inputs that drive cost
P: 14:04 to 14:31, 27 s. On screen: S$15/GJ gas, the largest credit.
P: These inputs drive the result. Gas is S$15 per gigajoule. Transport and storage is S$60 a tonne, between S$23.2 and S$112, and no Singapore-Indonesia tariff has been published. Reactor capital is US$2,184 per kilowatt thermal. Capture capital is bottom-up on an aMDEA basis, so an MEA plant could cost more. The full table is in backup.
P: REACTOR COST   14:31 to 15:34
P: 35. Reactor capital: first plant against later plants
P: 14:31 to 15:05, 34 s. On screen: S$743M BOAK and S$1,188M FOAK.
P: We price a generic 925 degree HTGR on INL’s recipe, charged at 210 megawatts. BOAK is INL’s term for a build between a first and an Nth of a kind, and it gives S$743 million. A first of a kind, FOAK, carries a 1.6 times premium, S$1,188 million, and that is what a single plant would be. The Xe-100 shown is an analogue only, with a 750 degree outlet and no vendor quotation.
P: 36. The scale law moves everything
P: 15:05 to 15:34, 29 s. On screen: S$189.7, S$277.7 and S$368.7 per tonne, equal size.
P: How a 600 megawatt estimate scales down to 210 is uncertain, so we print three treatments at equal size. n of 1.0 is INL’s recipe and our central case. n of 0.6 is a team assumption. n of 0.31 is the ratio of two first-of-a-kind estimates. At BOAK and 25 years, full cost runs from S$189.7 to S$368.7 a tonne.
P: MONEY   15:34 to 16:02
P: 37. Hydrogen cost, both plants
P: 15:34 to 16:02, 29 s. On screen: S$3.62/kg unabated steam methane reforming and S$5.26/kg NUS-H2, BOAK, 25 y.
P: Here is the money. Unabated hydrogen costs S$3.62 a kilogram. Over 25 years NUS-H2 costs S$5.26 at BOAK and S$6.02 at FOAK. Over 60 years, S$5.05 and S$5.71. That is a BOAK premium of S$1.64 a kilogram, which a buyer would have to carry. Both plants carry the same reformer island from IEAGHG, on the assumption that reformer differences cancel.
P: ABATEMENT COST   16:02 to 17:48
P: 38. Every real cost is on the ladder
P: 16:02 to 16:29, 27 s. On screen: S$189.7/tCO2e, rebuilt full cost.
P: This ladder shows what each line adds at BOAK over 25 years. Gas saved pays back S$64.8 million a year. Reactor capital costs S$69.6 million. Stop at the named step L3 and you read S$137.7 a tonne. Construction interest and lost steam export are real costs of this plant too, so the full figure is S$189.7.
P: 39. Levelised abatement cost: target missed
P: 16:29 to 16:48, 18 s. On screen: S$190.1, S$166.0, S$278.4, S$242.2 per tonne.
P: Here is the levelised abatement cost from the cash flow. BOAK is S$190.1 at 25 years and S$166.0 at 60. FOAK is S$278.4 and S$242.2. Against S$100, the target is missed: 0 of 8 central cases pass.
P: 40. What S$100/tCO2e would take
P: 16:48 to 17:20, 32 s. On screen: US$824/kWt needed, against INL’s low of US$1,456/kWt.
P: What would S$100 take? One lever at a time, at BOAK and 25 years, we are S$51.9 million a year over the ceiling. Gas would have to reach S$30.7 per gigajoule, 2.0 times our central price. Reactor cost would have to fall to US$824, under INL’s low of 1,456. Transport and storage would have to go negative. Sizing to need reaches only S$161.2. No single lever closes it.
P: 41. S$96/tCO2e: S$100/tCO2e target met for a 10th‑of‑a‑kind plant over 60 years with gas at S$20.7/GJ
P: 17:20 to 17:48, 28 s. On screen: S$95.6/tCO2e on the ledger and S$95.9/tCO2e by cash flow, at S$20.7/GJ against the central S$15/GJ.
P: A 10th‑of‑a‑kind plant run for 60 years meets the S$100 target at S$96 a tonne when gas costs S$20.7 a gigajoule: S$95.6 on the ledger and S$95.9 by cash flow. Dearer gas lowers the cost because NUS-H2 burns less gas than the unabated plant. The base stays on screen: BOAK over 25 years costs S$189.7 and S$190.1, and misses.
P: GROWTH CHART   17:48 to 18:13
P: 42. How the cost steps down to the target
P: 17:48 to 18:13, 25 s. On screen: S$189.7/tCO2e at the base and S$95.6/tCO2e after the three steps.
P: Every bar is an engine run, ledger first. From the base of S$189.7, a 10th‑of‑a‑kind reactor and a 60 year life take off S$43.4 and S$18.1. Gas at S$20.7 then takes off S$32.6, to S$95.6, under the line at the S$96 point. These are conditions on the base, which stays our reference.
P: FEASIBILITY   18:13 to 18:50
P: 43. Overall feasibility
P: 18:13 to 18:50, 37 s. On screen: Abatement met, cost missed at the base.
P: So is it feasible? On carbon, yes, on this boundary. On cost, the target is missed at central inputs and met only in the mature case. On technology, the plant rests on an IHX nobody has built at this duty. On siting, it rests on land and a national decision that do not yet exist. We read NUS-H2 as a credible carbon route whose cost closes only for a mature plant, and overseas demonstration has to come first.
