Model status 28 Executive Summary Singapore imports all of its natural gas, and conventional steam methane reforming (SMR) emits CO₂ from both its furnace and its reforming chemistry. NUS-H₂ removes the furnace. A 210  MW th  high-temperature gas-cooled reactor (HTGR) heats the reformer through helium, and 30  wt%  monoethanolamine (MEA) captures CO₂ from shifted syngas. The plant is a new build at SOXAL scale on the proposed Western Island, a siting proposal of this study with no official approval. It makes 100,000  Nm 3   h -1  of purified hydrogen, 66,925  t  H 2   y -1  at 85% availability, from 120.8  MW th  of process heat and a reactor need of 178.5  MW th . Pressure swing adsorption (PSA) stays an external block, and its reject returns to the reformer as a stream crossing the boundary. Net abatement is 0.578  MtCO 2 e  y -1  against a same-size unabated SMR, which meets the 0.25  MtCO 2 e  y -1  target by a factor of 2.3. The  S$100/ tCO 2 e  cost target is missed in all 8 central cases. The base full cost is  S$189.7/ tCO 2 e  at 25  y  and  S$165.6/ tCO 2 e  at 60  y  for a build between first and Nth of a kind (BOAK), and  S$277.9/ tCO 2 e  and  S$241.7/ tCO 2 e  for a first of a kind (FOAK). A discounted cash flow (DCF) reproduces these within 0.2%. The levelised cost of hydrogen is  S$5.26/kg  at BOAK, 25  y , against  S$3.62/kg  for the unabated SMR. A named scenario, MATURE_60Y, states what it would take to meet the target. A tenth-of-a-kind reactor over 60  y  with gas at  S$20.7/GJ , against the central  S$15/GJ , gives  S$95.6/ tCO 2 e  on the ledger and  S$95.9/ tCO 2 e  by DCF. Dearer gas lowers the abatement cost because NUS-H₂ burns less gas than the unabated plant. Deployment towards 2050 depends on a qualified reactor and intermediate heat exchanger (IHX), a design-specific emergency planning zone (EPZ), a licensing framework and a contracted CO₂ storage pathway. 1. Problem and Proposed Solution 1.1 The problem and its Singapore context Singapore's energy transition requires reliable industrial energy while reducing carbon emissions. With no domestic natural gas resources, Singapore relies on imported pipeline gas and liquefied natural gas (LNG); natural gas accounted for approximately 94% of electricity-generation fuel in 2024 [1,2]. This dependence highlights the strategic importance of diversifying low-carbon energy supply, particularly for continuous high-temperature industrial processes. Hydrogen offers a pathway for decarbonising industrial applications and supporting Singapore's future energy system. The National Hydrogen Strategy (October 2022) identifies low-carbon hydrogen as a potential contributor to up to half of the country's power needs by 2050 [3]. SMR is an established hydrogen-production technology; it generates CO₂ through methane conversion and conventionally burns fossil fuel to supply the endothermic reforming duty [4]. Addressing both emissions sources creates an opportuni ty to integrate nuclear process heat with carbon capture and storage (CCS). CCS captures the CO₂ generated in the reforming train [5], while nuclear process heat displaces natural gas combustion for the reforming duty [6]. Steam reforming of natural gas operates within approximately 500 to 900 °C  (Figure 1) [7], a range that HTGRs can serve and light-water reactors cannot. Figure 1.  Process heat temperature ranges of industrial applications, with the ranges that light-water reactors (LWRs) and HTGRs can serve [7]. This report proposes NUS-H₂, a single-train HTGR-heated SMR integrated with MEA carbon capture, as a new build on the proposed Western Island. The site is a proposal of this study and has no official approval. The plant is sized on the SOXAL hydrogen plant on Jurong Island, about 100,000  Nm 3   h -1  [8,9], and is compared with a same-size unabated SMR delivering equal hydrogen in equal hours. The design targets are net abatement above 0.25  MtCO 2 e  y -1  within Singapore, at a cost below  S$100/ tCO 2 e . 1.2 Proposed solution The proposed configuration integrates a 210  MW th  HTGR with an SMR hydrogen-production system (Figure 2). Nuclear fission heats helium in the primary reactor circuit, and an IHX transfers thermal energy to an independent secondary helium loop supplying the reformer. This arrangement replaces fossil-fired reformer heating and keeps the nuclear and chemical systems physically separate [10]. Reactor capacity beyond the process duty covers the MEA reboiler  gap and 19.8  MW e  of site electricity, and the remaining 31.5  MW th  stays as spare with no revenue. Natural gas and steam react over a nickel catalyst in the helium-heated reformer to produce hydrogen-rich synthesis gas [4,11]. Water-gas shift (WGS) conversion increases hydrogen yield and converts carbon monoxide to CO₂. After syngas cooling and condensate removal, a 30  wt%  aqueous MEA system absorbs CO₂ from the hydrogen-rich gas. A stripper regenerates the rich solvent, releasing concentrated CO₂ for compression and shipment to offshore storage. The Idaho National Laboratory (INL) reference removes CO₂ with Selexol [6,11], so MEA regeneration energy is assessed separately from INL's utility data. Pilot tests of 30  wt%  MEA on flue gas report specific reboiler duties of approximately 3.46 to 4.0  GJ  tCO 2 -1  [12,13]. The lower value excludes heat loss at 26 to 27  wt% , the upper value is at 85% capture on 3.5  vol%  CO₂, and no measurement on syngas was found. Figure 2.  NUS-H₂ arrangement. A 210  MW th  HTGR supplies 120.8  MW th  of process heat through an IHX and secondary helium circuit. The dashed line is the boundary: CO₂ compression and ship offshore storage sit inside it, the external PSA sits outside, and its reject returns across it. The PSA stays an unsimulated external block. Its reject returns across the boundary through a recycle compressor and a cryogenic unit, as in INL's Case 6 [6]. Methane-rich gas goes back to the reformer feed, hydrogen returns ahead of the PSA, and a nitrogen-rich cut is burned. 1.3 Design basis Table 1 sets the headline Case B beside the INL reference Case A. Technical and Practical Feasibility 2.1 Process heat and integration The proposed system combines established SMR and CO₂-capture operations with continuous high-temperature heat. Helium heats the reformer directly, so no natural gas is burned for reforming heat, and the chemical plant retains established reforming and amine equipment. Japan's High Temperature Engineering Test Reactor reached a 950 °C  outlet [14], yet it is a 30  MW t  test reactor. China's HTR-PM entered commercial operation in December 2023 [15] and runs near 750 °C  [16]. This work identified no commercial reactor at 900 °C , so the design assumes a generic 925 °C  HTGR and treats Xe-100, also near 750 °C  [17], as a labelled analogue only. Secondary helium reaches the reformer at 900 °C  and returns at 466 °C  [6]. The 871 °C  reformer outlet is INL's equilibrium assumption. Run through the Level 3 flowsheet, a cooler outlet leaves more methane unconverted: at 825 and 800 °C  abatement falls to 0.559  MtCO 2 e  y -1  and 0.544  MtCO 2 e  y -1  (Table 5). 2.2 Thermal and electrical balance Table 2 closes the balance at the headline basis term by term, with duct loss, vessel loss and circulator shaft heat as separate rows. The literature is mixed: IEAGHG 2022-07 calls MEA the preferred chemical solvent [19], while IEAGHG 2017-02 favours aMDEA on shifted syngas [5]. The absorber runs at 40 °C  and 26.8  bar  with rich loading capped at 0.50  mol  mol -1 , and per-pass capture of 90% is a project assumption. A rate-based absorber solved to that duty needs 15.16  m  of packing at Case B. The specific reboiler duty (SRD) of 3.8  GJ  tCO 2 -1 , range 3.4 to 4.2, is assumed from flue-gas pilots, since no syngas measurement exists. The equilibrium-stage stripper overstated the DICE pre-FEED design SRD by 46% and the Kwak pilot SRD by 27% [20,21], so the column gate failed and the assumed SRD stays in the headline (Appendix E). The 51.0  MW  reboiler at 121 °C  draws first on freed Selexol steam (12.7  MW ), then diverted export steam (16.8  MW ), syngas heat above 130 °C  (8.4  MW ) and reactor steam for the remaining 13.1  MW . The column models rest on the Aronu extended UNIQUAC solvent model [22]. 2.4 Reactor safety and emergency planning Tristructural isotropic (TRISO) fuel particles incorporate multiple protective layers that retain fission products, while helium cooling and the graphite-based core support high-temperature operation [10]. The primary helium boundary, IHX and independent secondary loop provide successive physical barriers between the reactor and hydrogen-production equipment. Singapore's compact geography makes site-specific emergency planning essential [23]. Advanced-reactor assessments use accident frequencies and projected radiological consequences to determine protective actions [24,25,26]. Figure 3 illustrates this method using the Xe-100 safety-case example [27]. That assessment presents a 400  m  emergency-planning reference distance for its specific case [27,28], and HTR-PM studies evaluate offsite arrangements [29,30]. Here 400  m  is a conceptual reference only and is no approved Singapore boundary. Emergency arrangements would come from a reactor-specific safety case and regulatory review. Figure 3. Adapted from the Xe-100 construction permit application [27]. 2.5 Regulation and community engagement Singapore is strengthening its capability to assess advanced nuclear technologies and has made no decision on nuclear energy [31,32]. A Phase 1 International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Integrated Nuclear Infrastructure Review (INIR) is tentatively set for 2027 and covers 19 areas of infrastructure, including emergency preparedness and radioactive waste management [31]. Manpower schemes for public officers begin in 2027 [33], and separately NUS and NTU are working towards a joint Master's in nuclear enginee ring in 2027 [34]. The Singapore Nuclear Research and Safety Institute contributes reactor modelling and safety analysis alongside public education [32,35,36]. The National Environment Agency's responsibilities and the  Energy Market Authority's feasibility study add to the institutional basis, supported by regulatory cooperation abroad [37,38,39]. A future project needs one licensing framework that spans the reactor and its industrial heat interface, with emergency preparedness built in. Early engagement with Western Island planners, neighbouring Jurong Island operators and nearby communities would support informed assessment [35,36,40]. Clear communication of the plant's industrial purpose and protective design features is central to project development. 2.6 Risks and limitations The assessment adopts INL process conditions [6,11] and a 210  MW th  rating, with balances anchored on INL cases and one term on a gated model (Appendix E). The government has announced that smaller islands around Jurong Island will be connected into a  new western island, reclaimed over coming decades, and its page names new power generation infrastructure with no mention of nuclear [43,44]. The reference case is a new build at SOXAL scale, so it retires no existing source. Central hydrogen runs only in the 85% availability hours, and backup firing stays a sensitivity. Captured CO₂ is shipped to offshore storage abroad under Singapore-Indonesia cooperation [42]. Pipeline links to the island are unpriced. Net Emissions Abatement 3.1 Method Net abatement is the difference between the operational emissions of a same-size unabated SMR and the carbon the proposed plant sends to air: Δ E net  =  E baseline  -  E residual (3.1) E residual  =  E cut  +  E grid (3.2) The furnace is removed, yet the nitrogen-rich cut of recycled PSA reject is burned, and that carbon  E cut  is the residual. Absorber slip returns through the reformer and reaches air only through this cut. The grid term is zero as a calculated result at central inputs: unmet electricity is 0  MW e  at the rated reactor. All values are in tonnes per year at 85% availability. 3.2 System boundary and assumptions The boundary follows Figure 2. It holds a nuclear island (reactor, primary and secondary helium circuits, small power block) and a chemical plant (reformer, WGS, MEA capture, recycle compressor, cryogenic unit, CO₂ compressor and ship offshore storage). The PSA sits outside the line, with its reject returning across it. Ship and storage sit inside the line for mass balance and cost, while ship fuel stays in the lifecycle sensitivity. The CO₂ compressor load is INL's pipeline basis scaled to Case B, and the  liquefaction a ship needs is neither modelled nor priced. The baseline uses SOXAL's scale of about 100,000  Nm 3   h -1  [8]. At 0.08988  kg  Nm -3  and 7,446  h  y -1 , output is 66,925  t  H 2   y -1 . Baseline intensity is INL's 9.21  kg  CO 2   kg -1   H 2  [11], with 9.35 from Cho et al. [45] and 8.996, the unrounded IEAGHG Base Case value [5], as bounds. Per-pass capture of 90%, a project assumption, gives 90.4% plant capture because slip returns to the reformer, so the two always print together. 3.3 Net emissions abated Table 4 gives the carbon ledger. Economics and Implementation 4.1 Method and boundary Two methods feed one result, both in real 2025 Singapore dollars at 8%, with  S$1.3065/US$  [47] and escalation factors of 1.6195 from 2019 US dollars and 2.322 from 2014 euros [48,49]. The first is an annual incremental ledger against a same-size new-build unabated SMR at 66,925  t  H 2   y -1 , with levels L0 to L5 adding items step by step: Δ C  =  i C i ,   AC =  Δ C A ,   CRF =  r 1- 1+ r - L (4.1) Here  C i  is an annual cost line,  A  is net abatement and CRF annualises capital over life  L  at rate  r , giving 0.0937 at 25  y  and 0.0808 at 60  y . Common items cancel, including reformer capital differences by assumption. The target sets a ceiling of  S$57.8M/y . Items with a 25  y  life are replaced on longer horizons with unused life credited at the end, and decommissioning  is paid from cash flow at end of life. Both plants carry the SMR island of the IEAGHG Base Case,  S$517.6M  of capital and  S$17.5M/y  of fixed O&amp;M [5]. The plant abates 0.578  MtCO 2 e  y -1  at 90% per-pass and 90.4% plant capture, and the result holds across the baseline bounds. It rests on working capture and on recycle transferring to MEA. Economic performance.  The full cost of  S$165.6/ tCO 2 e  to  S$277.9/ tCO 2 e  at  n  = 1.0  misses the target in every central case, by both methods. The MATURE_60Y scenario reaches  S$95.6/ tCO 2 e  only with a tenth-of-a-kind reactor over 60  y  and gas at  S$20.7/GJ . Comparison with conventional SMR plus CCS.  IEAGHG Case 3 at  S$203.0/ tCO 2 e  on matched T&amp;S sits above the NUS-H₂ BOAK case and below its FOAK case, so the proposal competes only once a reactor is past first of a kind. The comparison is indicative. NUS-H₂ remains distinct by removing the furnace, with residual emissions of 0.57  kg  CO 2   kg -1   H 2 . Implementation in Singapore.  Reactor qualification, the nuclear-chemical heat interface, site-specific emergency planning and contracted CO₂ transport and storage form the main requirements (Figure 4). A competing pathway exists: Air Liquide and Aster have signed a memorandum on autothermal reforming with integrated capture in Singapore [58]. Principal uncertainties.  Reactor size treatment spans  S$189.7/ tCO 2 e  to  S$368.7/ tCO 2 e  at BOAK, 25  y . The gas saving, the SRD on syngas, IHX performance and the spare on Ahn and Lee's heat basis all remain unresolved. The column models and the Level 3 flowsheet failed their gates, so most terms stay anchored (Appendix E). 5.2 Conclusion NUS-H₂ pairs a 210  MW th  HTGR with an SMR and 30  wt%  MEA at SOXAL scale. The abatement target is met at 0.578  MtCO 2 e  y -1 . The cost target of  S$100/ tCO 2 e  is missed in every central case, with a base full cost of  S$189.7/ tCO 2 e  at BOAK, 25  y . It is met only under the stated MATURE_60Y conditions, a tenth-of-a-kind reactor over 60  y  with gas at  S$20.7/GJ . Singapore's next steps are reactor and IHX qualification overseas, site-specific safety and licensing, hydrogen offtake and a contracted CO₂ storage pathway.
